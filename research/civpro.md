## 4. I.A Federal subject-matter jurisdiction (cp1) 

### 4.1 Black-letter rules

**Baseline.** Federal courts have limited jurisdiction. SMJ cannot be waived or conferred by consent; any party (or the court) may raise it at any time before final judgment; the court must dismiss when it finds it lacking (Rule 12(h)(3); 28 USC 1447(c) for removed cases). [V]

**Federal question, 28 USC 1331.** Civil actions "arising under" the Constitution, laws or treaties of the US. [K text]
- **Well-pleaded complaint rule:** the federal question must appear on the face of the plaintiff's properly pleaded complaint. A federal *defense* (including preemption as a defense) does not create jurisdiction, and neither does an anticipated federal defense. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley* (1908) [K].
- A **counterclaim** cannot supply "arising under" for removal (*Holmes Group v. Vornado* (2002), *Vaden v. Discover Bank* (2009)) [K]; and a party brought in only by a counterclaim (a third-party counterclaim defendant) is not "the defendant" who may remove under 1441(a) or CAFA 1453(b): *Home Depot U.S.A. v. Jackson* (2019), 5-4, Thomas. [V]
- **Creation test:** most cases arise under federal law because federal law creates the cause of action (*American Well Works v. Layne & Bowler*, Holmes) [K].
- **State-law claim with an embedded federal issue** (narrow): jurisdiction exists if the federal issue is (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution in federal court without disrupting the federal-state balance. *Grable & Sons Metal Products v. Darue Engineering* (2005) [K]; *Gunn v. Minton* (2013): legal-malpractice claim requiring a hypothetical "case within a case" about patent law fails the substantiality and balance factors, so state court keeps it (unanimous). [V: Gunn]
- *Merrell Dow v. Thompson* (1986): a federal statute with no private right of action, used as an element of a state tort, does not by itself confer jurisdiction [K].
- **Complete preemption** is a narrow exception to well-pleaded complaint (e.g., LMRA 301, ERISA 502(a), National Bank Act usury) [K].
- Exclusive federal jurisdiction (patent, copyright, antitrust damages, bankruptcy etc.) is not tested at depth; "concurrent jurisdiction" is the default and state courts can hear federal claims [K].

**Diversity, 28 USC 1332(a).** Matter in controversy **exceeds $75,000**, exclusive of interest and costs, between: (1) citizens of different States; (2) citizens of a State and citizens/subjects of a foreign state (with the proviso: a lawfully admitted permanent resident is deemed a citizen of the State where domiciled); (3) citizens of different States with foreign citizens as additional parties; (4) a foreign state as plaintiff and citizens of a State. [V summary]
- **Complete diversity:** no plaintiff may share citizenship with any defendant. *Strawbridge v. Curtiss* (1806) [K]. It is tested as of **filing** (time-of-filing rule; *Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global* (2004); later move does not destroy jurisdiction) [K].
- **Individuals:** citizen of the State of **domicile** (physical presence + intent to remain); residence is not enough. A US citizen domiciled abroad is "stateless": cannot sue or be sued in diversity (*Newman-Green v. Alfonzo-Larrain* (1989)) [K].
- **Corporations:** citizen of **every State of incorporation** AND the State of its **principal place of business** (1332(c)(1)). PPB = **nerve center**, where high-level officers direct, control and coordinate the corporation's activities (*Hertz v. Friend* (2010)); typically headquarters, not a mail drop or place where board meets occasionally. [V]
- **Unincorporated entities** (partnerships, LLCs, unincorporated associations): citizen of **every State of which any member is a citizen**. *Carden v. Arkoma* (1990) [K]. (Trusts: depends on form; *Americold Realty Trust v. ConAgra* (2016): a REIT trust takes its shareholders' citizenship as a "business trust"; traditional trust takes the trustee's) [K].
- **Legal representatives** of a decedent: decedent's citizenship; of an infant or incompetent: that person's. **Insurers in direct actions** (insured not a party): also citizen of the insured's State. (1332(c)(2), (c)(1)) [V]
- **Amount in controversy:** the plaintiff's good-faith claim controls unless it appears to a **legal certainty** the claim is for less (*St. Paul Mercury v. Red Cab* (1938)) [K]. Includes compensatory and punitive damages (where available) and attorney fees if a contract or statute provides them; excludes interest and costs. A **counterclaim** does not count toward the plaintiff's amount. Equitable relief: value of the object of the litigation (*Hunt v. Washington Apple Advertising* (1977)) [K].
- **Aggregation:** one plaintiff may aggregate **all** claims, related or not, against one defendant. Multiple plaintiffs cannot aggregate separate claims (*Snyder v. Harris*) [K]. But: if **at least one plaintiff** satisfies the amount and complete diversity exists, supplemental jurisdiction (1367) reaches other plaintiffs' smaller claims, overruling *Zahn*: *Exxon Mobil v. Allapattah* (2005) [K].
- **Exceptions:** domestic-relations and probate exceptions are narrow (*Ankenbrandt v. Richards* (1992)) [K].
- **Rule 7.1(a)(2):** in a diversity case each party must file a statement naming every individual/entity whose citizenship is attributed to it and its citizenship, with first appearance. [V]
- **Misjoined/dismissed nondiverse party:** a court of appeals (or district court) may drop a *dispensable* nondiverse party under Rule 21 to save jurisdiction (*Newman-Green*). But if a district court wrongly dismisses a nondiverse defendant over the plaintiff's objection and proceeds to judgment, reversal on that point forces vacatur of the judgment: *Hain Celestial Group v. Palmquist* (Feb 24, 2026, unanimous). [V]

**Supplemental jurisdiction, 28 USC 1367.** [V text]
- (a) Courts with original jurisdiction have supplemental jurisdiction over claims "so related" that they form part of the **same case or controversy** (common nucleus of operative fact, *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs* (1966) [K]); includes claims involving joinder or intervention of additional parties.
- (b) **In diversity-only cases**, no supplemental jurisdiction over **claims by plaintiffs** against persons made parties under Rules **14, 19, 20 or 24**, or over claims by persons proposed to be joined as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or intervening under Rule 24, when that would be inconsistent with 1332. Effect: it codifies *Owen Equipment v. Kroger* (1978) (plaintiff cannot sue a nondiverse third-party defendant). Does NOT bar: a defendant's counterclaim, crossclaim, impleader of a third party, or intervenor-defendant claims. [V; Kroger K]
- (c) Court **may decline** if: (1) novel/complex state-law issue; (2) state claim substantially predominates; (3) court dismissed all original-jurisdiction claims; (4) exceptional circumstances. [V]
- (d) Limitations period tolled while the claim is pending and for **30 days** after dismissal (unless state law gives longer). [V]
- Compulsory counterclaims (Rule 13(a)) are within supplemental jurisdiction; **permissive** counterclaims: majority of circuits require an independent jurisdictional basis unless they share the same nucleus [K, uncertain; do not make this a standalone exam item].
- **Royal Canin U.S.A. v. Wullschleger** (Jan 15, 2025, unanimous, Kagan): when a plaintiff, after removal on federal-question grounds, amends the complaint to delete every federal claim, the federal court **loses supplemental jurisdiction** over the remaining state claims and must remand. (Different from a mere decision to decline under 1367(c) when federal claims are dismissed on the merits.) [V]

### 4.2 Fact patterns (our own)

**SMJ-1 (well-pleaded complaint).** A tenant sues her landlord in State A state court for breach of the lease. The complaint cites only state law. The landlord, a citizen of State A like the tenant, files an answer asserting that a federal statute preempts the tenant's claim and removes to federal court. The tenant moves to remand. How should the court rule?
(A) Deny, because a preemption defense raises a federal question. (B) Deny, because the landlord, as the party invoking federal law, may choose the forum. (C) **Grant, because federal-question jurisdiction depends on the plaintiff's complaint and a federal defense does not supply it.** (D) Grant, because the parties are citizens of the same State and removal in diversity cases is barred by the forum-defendant rule.
*Best: C.* Wrong: A states the opposite of the well-pleaded complaint rule (ordinary preemption is a defense). B invents a defendant's option. D gets to the right outcome via the wrong reason: the case is not removable in diversity at all (no diversity), not merely barred by the forum-defendant rule (which applies only to diversity cases with diverse parties).

**SMJ-2 (counterclaim cannot create it).** A seller sues a buyer in state court for unpaid invoices under state contract law. The buyer files a compulsory counterclaim under a federal statute and removes. Is the case properly removed?
(A) Yes, because the counterclaim arises under federal law. (B) Yes, because the buyer is a defendant and the counterclaim is compulsory. (C) **No, because federal-question jurisdiction is determined from the plaintiff's complaint, and a counterclaim does not create it.** (D) No, because compulsory counterclaims may be asserted only in the court where the original action is pending.
*Best: C.* Wrong: A and B ignore that the "arising under" inquiry looks at the complaint; D is not a rule (and compulsory counterclaims are fine in federal court).

**SMJ-3 (Grable/Gunn).** A client sues her former lawyer in state court for malpractice, alleging he negligently handled a patent infringement case. To prove causation the client must show she would have won the patent case. The lawyer wants to remove. Does a federal court have jurisdiction?
(A) Yes, because patent law is exclusively federal. (B) Yes, because the claim necessarily requires deciding a federal patent issue. (C) **No, because the federal issue is not substantial to the federal system and hearing the claim would upset the federal-state balance.** (D) No, because malpractice claims are always state-law claims.
*Best: C* (*Gunn v. Minton*). Wrong: A confuses exclusive jurisdiction over patent *claims* with this state-law claim. B stops at step one of four-part test. D is an absolute that the *Grable* doctrine contradicts.

**SMJ-4 (corporate citizenship).** A Delaware corporation has its headquarters, where its CEO and other officers run the company, in Ohio and its largest factory in Texas. An Ohio citizen sues it for $200,000 in federal court. Does the court have diversity jurisdiction?
(A) Yes, because the corporation is a Delaware citizen. (B) Yes, because the factory in Texas is the principal place of business. (C) **No, because the corporation is also an Ohio citizen, the State of its nerve center.** (D) No, because corporations cannot be citizens for diversity purposes.
*Best: C.* A forgets the dual citizenship; B uses the old "place of operations" idea rejected by *Hertz*; D contradicts 1332(c)(1).

**SMJ-5 (LLC).** An LLC organized in Delaware with its principal place of business in Texas has two members: a California citizen and an Ohio citizen. An Ohio citizen sues the LLC in federal court for $300,000. Diversity jurisdiction?
(A) Yes, because the LLC is a citizen of Delaware and Texas. (B) Yes, because the amount exceeds $75,000. (C) **No, because an LLC takes the citizenship of each of its members, and one is an Ohio citizen like the plaintiff.** (D) No, because LLCs cannot be sued in federal court.
*Best: C* (*Carden*). A applies the corporate rule to an unincorporated entity.

**SMJ-6 (stateless citizen and permanent resident).** Pick the pair for which there is NO diversity jurisdiction (amount is satisfied in each): (A) a Mexican citizen domiciled in Mexico v. a Texas citizen; (B) **a citizen of Mexico lawfully admitted for permanent residence and domiciled in Texas v. a Texas citizen**; (C) a Texas citizen v. a Japanese corporation with its principal place of business in Tokyo; (D) a Texas citizen v. an Oklahoma citizen and a French citizen.
*Best: B* (permanent resident treated as citizen of the State of domicile, so Texas v. Texas). Wrong: A is alienage diversity; C is State v. alien; D is mixed party diversity under 1332(a)(3).

**SMJ-7 (time of filing).** A New York citizen files a $150,000 diversity suit in federal court against a California citizen. After filing, the plaintiff moves to California and becomes a California citizen. Defendant moves to dismiss. The court should:
(A) Dismiss, because the parties are now citizens of the same State. (B) Dismiss without prejudice and allow refiling in state court. (C) **Deny the motion, because diversity is measured when the action is filed.** (D) Deny the motion only if the defendant consents.
*Best: C* (time-of-filing rule). SMJ cannot be consented to (D).

**SMJ-8 (legal certainty).** A buyer sues a seller in federal court, diverse parties, claiming $120,000 for breach of a contract whose valid, enforceable clause caps all damages at $40,000. The seller moves to dismiss for lack of SMJ. Result?
(A) Denied; the plaintiff pleaded more than $75,000 in good faith. (B) **Granted, because it appears to a legal certainty that the claim cannot exceed $75,000.** (C) Denied, because the amount is measured at the time of judgment. (D) Granted, because contract claims are within state jurisdiction.
*Best: B.* The good-faith claim controls unless legal certainty; the cap makes the $75k impossible. 

**SMJ-9 (aggregation / Allapattah).** Two plaintiffs, both citizens of State A, sue a defendant, a State B citizen, in federal court on claims arising from the same accident. Plaintiff 1 claims $100,000; Plaintiff 2 claims $30,000. Can the court hear Plaintiff 2's claim?
(A) No, because each plaintiff must independently meet the amount in controversy. (B) No, because plaintiffs cannot aggregate their claims. (C) **Yes, because complete diversity exists, Plaintiff 1's claim satisfies the amount, and Plaintiff 2's claim is within supplemental jurisdiction.** (D) Yes, but only if Plaintiff 2 stipulates his damages exceed $75,000.
*Best: C* (*Exxon Mobil v. Allapattah*). A and B state the pre-2005 *Zahn* rule; D is invented.

**SMJ-10 (supplemental, plaintiff v. third-party defendant).** A State A citizen sues a State B citizen in federal diversity for $200,000 for a collision. The defendant impleads a State A citizen under Rule 14 for contribution. The plaintiff then asserts a related negligence claim against the third-party defendant (the same State A citizen). Does the court have SMJ over the plaintiff's claim against the third-party defendant?
(A) Yes, because it is part of the same case or controversy. (B) Yes, because the third-party defendant was properly joined under Rule 14. (C) **No, because 1367(b) bars supplemental jurisdiction over a plaintiff's claim against a party joined under Rule 14 when it would be inconsistent with the diversity statute.** (D) No, because impleader is unavailable in federal diversity cases.
*Best: C.* A applies 1367(a) and misses (b). D is false: the defendant's impleader claim itself is within supplemental jurisdiction.

**SMJ-11 (declining supplemental).** In a federal-question case, the plaintiff joined a related state-law claim. The court grants summary judgment to the defendant on the only federal claim, long before trial. The court's best course?
(A) Retain the state claim because it is part of the same case or controversy and the court must exercise jurisdiction once it has it. (B) Dismiss the state claim with prejudice. (C) **Probably decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction under 1367(c)(3) and dismiss the state claim without prejudice, so that the plaintiff can refile in state court with tolling.** (D) Certify the state claim to the state supreme court as the only option.
*Best: C.* A: exercise is discretionary (1367(c)). B: no prejudice because the court is not deciding merits, and 1367(d) tolls limitations. D: not the rule.
(Variant for removal cluster: see RM-6, Royal Canin.)

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:*
- "A federal defense never makes a federal case. Look at the plaintiff's complaint, not the answer."
- "Corporation: two citizenships. LLC: all its members' citizenships. Individual: domicile only."
- "Complete diversity at filing, more than $75,000, and plaintiffs can't use 1367 to sneak in a nondiverse co-party."

## 5. I.A Removal and remand (cp2)

### 5.1 Black-letter rules

- **Who and where.** A civil action in state court may be removed by "the defendant or the defendants" to the federal district court "for the district and division embracing the place where such action is pending" if the federal court would have original jurisdiction (1441(a)). Venue rules (1391) do **not** apply to removed cases; the removal statute fixes the forum (*Polizzi v. Cowles Magazines*, 1953 [K]). Removal does not waive personal-jurisdiction objections [K]. Plaintiffs cannot remove; third-party/counterclaim defendants cannot remove (*Home Depot*). [V, V]
- **Diversity-only limit: forum-defendant rule (1441(b)(2)).** A case removable *only* on diversity grounds may not be removed if any **properly joined and served** defendant is a citizen of the forum State. Citizenship of fictitious (Doe) defendants is disregarded in diversity removals (1441(b)(1)). The forum-defendant rule is a **procedural** defect, not jurisdictional: the plaintiff waives it by failing to move to remand within 30 days. (Complete diversity, amount in controversy and federal-question jurisdiction are jurisdictional.) [V text; waiver point K]
- **Mixed federal/state claims (1441(c)):** if a federal-question claim is joined with a claim outside federal jurisdiction or made nonremovable by statute, the **entire action** may be removed, then the court **severs and remands** the claims not within original or supplemental jurisdiction. Only defendants against whom the federal claim is asserted must join. [V]
- **Procedure (1446):** notice of removal in the district and division where the action is pending, signed under Rule 11, short and plain statement of grounds, with all process, pleadings and orders served; **30 days** from receipt of initial pleading (or from service of summons if shorter; formal service matters, *Murphy Bros. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing* (1999) [K]); **unanimity**: all defendants properly joined and served must join or consent when removal is solely under 1441(a); each defendant has its own 30 days; later-served defendant may remove and earlier-served defendants may consent (1446(b)(2)(B)-(C)); if not removable initially, 30 days after receipt of amended pleading, motion, order or other paper from which removability can first be ascertained (1446(b)(3)); **diversity cases may not be removed on (b)(3) grounds more than 1 year after commencement** unless the plaintiff acted in bad faith to prevent removal; amount in controversy: the sum demanded in good faith in the initial pleading is deemed the amount, but a notice may assert the amount when state practice bars specific demands or the pleading seeks non-monetary relief, and then removal is proper if the court finds by a **preponderance** that the amount is exceeded (1446(c)(2)). Prompt written notice to adverse parties and the state clerk (1446(d)): the state court "shall proceed no further unless and until the case is remanded." [V]
- **Notice of removal pleading standard:** only a plausible allegation of the amount in controversy is required (*Dart Cherokee Basin Operating Co. v. Owens* (2014)) [K].
- **Time limits are mandatory, not jurisdictional, not subject to equitable tolling:** *Enbridge Energy v. Nessel* (Apr. 22, 2026, unanimous, Sotomayor) re 1446(b)(1). [V via CRS summary: everycrsreport.com LSB11434]
- **Remand (1447):** a motion to remand on any ground *other than* lack of SMJ must be made within **30 days** of the notice of removal; the court must remand "at any time before final judgment" if it appears it lacks SMJ; remand order may require payment of just costs and actual expenses incl. attorney fees; **remand orders are not reviewable on appeal or otherwise** (except federal officer/civil-rights removals under 1442/1443). (Fee award on remand: *Martin v. Franklin Capital* (2005): only if the removing party lacked an objectively reasonable basis [K].) [V text]
- **After-removal pleading:** Rule 81(c): FRCP apply after removal; answer due longest of 21 days after receipt of initial pleading, 21 days after service of summons, or 7 days after notice of removal. A party who expressly demanded a jury under state law before removal need not renew. [V]
- **Federal-officer removal (1442(a)(1))** and CAFA are separate routes [V for 1442 via Chevron USA v. Plaquemines Parish (Apr. 17, 2026)]. Not worth more than a recognition item.
- **Snap removal** (removing before a forum defendant is served): circuits are split / permit it in the text-based view; not to be tested as settled [K, uncertain].
- *Hain Celestial v. Palmquist* (2026) and *Royal Canin* (2025) are the two newest SMJ/removal holdings (see sections 4.1 and 11). [V]

### 5.2 Fact patterns

**RM-1 (forum-defendant rule, waiver).** An Ohio citizen sues a Texas citizen in a Texas state court for $200,000 on a state-law claim. The defendant removes to federal court 20 days later. The plaintiff does nothing for 45 days, then moves to remand arguing that the defendant is a citizen of the forum State. The court should:
(A) Remand, because the case is not removable on diversity grounds. (B) Remand, because removal was a jurisdictional error. (C) **Deny the motion, because the forum-defendant rule is a procedural defect that was waived when the plaintiff did not move to remand within 30 days.** (D) Deny the motion, because the forum-defendant rule applies only if the plaintiff is also a citizen of the forum.
*Best: C.* Parties are diverse and amount met, so SMJ exists; 1441(b)(2) is not jurisdictional; 1447(c) 30-day limit. D invents a condition.

**RM-2 (timing).** A defendant is served with a state-court complaint on March 1 and removes on April 15. The plaintiff moves to remand on April 20, asserting only that the notice was late. The court should:
(A) Deny, because removal timing is jurisdictional only after judgment. (B) Deny, because the 30-day clock starts when the plaintiff files, not when served. (C) **Remand, because the notice of removal was filed more than 30 days after service and the plaintiff raised the defect within 30 days.** (D) Remand, because the one-year limit has expired.
*Best: C.* D: the one-year limit applies only to (b)(3) diversity removals. 

**RM-3 (unanimity).** A plaintiff sues two defendants in state court on a federal claim. Both are served on the same day. Defendant 1 timely removes without Defendant 2's consent; Defendant 2 does not join or object. The plaintiff moves to remand on this ground within 20 days. How should the court rule?
(A) Deny; a single defendant may remove a federal-question case. (B) **Grant, because all properly joined and served defendants must join in or consent to removal under 1441(a).** (C) Grant, because federal-question cases may not be removed. (D) Deny, because the lack of unanimity is cured by the court's inherent power.
*Best: B.*

**RM-4 (plaintiff cannot remove / counterclaim defendant).** A bank sues a customer in state court on a state-law claim. The customer files a federal-law counterclaim. May the bank remove?
(A) Yes, because the counterclaim arises under federal law. (B) Yes, because the bank is the master of the controversy. (C) **No, because only a defendant may remove, and "the defendant" under 1441(a) is the party sued in the original complaint, not a counterclaim defendant.** (D) No, but only because the amount is below $75,000.
*Best: C* (*Home Depot*; *Shamrock Oil*).

**RM-5 (one-year limit).** A Texas citizen sues a Nevada citizen and a Texas citizen in a Texas state court. Fourteen months later, the plaintiff voluntarily dismisses the Texas defendant. The Nevada defendant removes immediately. The plaintiff moves to remand and offers no evidence of any scheme. The court should:
(A) Deny; the case became removable when the nondiverse defendant left. (B) **Grant; a diversity case may not be removed on (b)(3) grounds more than one year after it commenced unless the plaintiff acted in bad faith to prevent removal.** (C) Grant, because a defendant never can remove a case once a forum-citizen defendant was named. (D) Deny, because the one-year limit applies to federal-question removals.
*Best: B.* Also the voluntary-dismissal rule (a case becomes removable only through the plaintiff's voluntary act) [K].

**RM-6 (Royal Canin).** A plaintiff sues in state court on a federal statutory claim and a state-law claim. The defendant removes. Plaintiff then amends the complaint to drop the federal claim and asks for remand. How should the court rule?
(A) Deny; jurisdiction at the time of removal governs. (B) Deny; the court has discretion to keep supplemental claims for judicial economy. (C) **Grant; the amended complaint is the operative pleading and, with no federal claim left, the federal court has no supplemental jurisdiction and must remand.** (D) Dismiss the state claim with prejudice.
*Best: C* (*Royal Canin v. Wullschleger*, 2025). Fine point: this is NOT the 1367(c)(3) "decline" situation.

**RM-7 (venue after removal / where).** A suit pending in a state court in the Eastern District of X is removed. The defendant says venue is improper in that district because the defendant lives in the Western District. Result?
(A) The case must be transferred under 1406(a). (B) **Venue is determined by the removal statute: the federal district embracing the state court where the action was pending.** (C) The court must dismiss. (D) The plaintiff may choose the district.
*Best: B* (1441(a); *Polizzi*).

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Remove within 30 days, all defendants on board, never to the home-state defendant's own backyard (diversity only), and never after year one on diversity." / "Remand orders: can't appeal them. Almost."

## 6. I.B-I.C Personal jurisdiction, service of process and notice (cp3)

### 6.1 Black-letter rules: personal jurisdiction

**Two questions in federal court.** (1) *Authorization:* does a rule or statute allow the court to reach this defendant? Under Rule 4(k)(1) service or waiver establishes PJ over a defendant who (A) is subject to the courts of general jurisdiction of the State where the district court sits (so the state long-arm statute and the 14th Amendment limit a diversity court), (B) is a Rule 14 or 19 party served within the US and **not more than 100 miles** from the summons-issuing courthouse, or (C) is covered by a federal statute authorizing it (e.g., nationwide service statutes). Rule 4(k)(2): for a **federal** claim, if the defendant is not subject to the courts of general jurisdiction of **any** State, and jurisdiction is consistent with the Constitution and laws, service establishes PJ (aggregates national contacts). (2) *Due process:* 14th Amendment (state courts, and federal courts borrowing state reach) or 5th Amendment (federal statutes). [V Rule 4(k)]
**Federal statutes and the 5th Amendment:** *Fuld v. PLO* (June 20, 2025, unanimous, Roberts): Congress's PSJVTA, deeming the PLO/PA to consent to PJ in Antiterrorism Act suits through specified conduct, does not violate the 5th Amendment Due Process Clause; the Court did not decide the outer limits of congressional power. Recognition-level item only. [V]

**Traditional bases (always valid):** (a) **domicile** (individual in home State); (b) **physical presence when served** ("tag" jurisdiction): *Burnham v. Superior Court* (1990) - served while voluntarily present in the State; fraud or force that lures the defendant in defeats it, and parties/witnesses in other proceedings are generally immune from service while attending [K]; (c) **consent**: appointment of an agent, forum-selection clause, appearance without objection, or a **registration-consent statute**; (d) **waiver** of the defense (Rule 12(h)(1)).
- **Consent by registration:** *Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway* (June 27, 2023), 5-4 (Gorsuch; parts of the opinion are a plurality): due process does not bar a State from requiring out-of-state corporations that register to do business there to consent to general jurisdiction (*Pennsylvania Fire* (1917) still controls). Most States' registration statutes do **not** say so, so registration alone usually is not consent; the item must state that the statute says so. [V]

**General (all-purpose) jurisdiction:** only where the defendant is "at home" (*Goodyear Dunlop v. Brown* (2011); *Daimler AG v. Bauman* (2014); *BNSF v. Tyrrell* (2017)): individual = domicile; corporation = State of incorporation and principal place of business; only in an "exceptional case" elsewhere (*Perkins v. Benguet*, 1952, is the paradigm). Large in-state sales, "continuous and systematic" business, even a big in-state plant do not make a corporation at home there. [K, standard; Daimler/Goodyear widely known but not re-opened]

**Specific (case-linked) jurisdiction:** (1) the defendant **purposefully availed** itself of the forum (or purposefully directed conduct at it): *Hanson v. Denckla* (1958), *World-Wide Volkswagen v. Woodson* (1980): the unilateral activity of the plaintiff or a third party cannot create it, and mere foreseeability that a product may reach the forum is not enough, the question is whether the defendant could reasonably anticipate being haled into court there; (2) the claim **"arises out of or relates to"** the contacts: *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District* (2021): strict causation not required; jurisdiction proper where Ford systematically served the forum market for the very models that injured forum residents in forum accidents, even though the specific cars were first sold elsewhere [V]; *Bristol-Myers Squibb v. Superior Court* (2017): nonresident plaintiffs whose claims have no connection to the forum (not prescribed, injured, or treated there) cannot sue there even if resident plaintiffs can [K]; (3) **reasonableness / fair play**: burden on defendant, forum's interest, plaintiff's interest, efficiency, shared substantive policy (*Burger King v. Rudzewicz* (1985): contract alone is not enough, look at prior negotiations, contemplated future consequences, terms, course of dealing; once min contacts are shown the defendant must present a "compelling case" that jurisdiction is unreasonable). [K]
- **Intentional torts:** *Calder v. Jones* (1984) "effects" test; but *Walden v. Fiore* (2014): the relationship must arise out of contacts the *defendant himself* creates with the forum **State**; contacts with a plaintiff who lives there, or knowledge of the plaintiff's residence, is not enough. [K]
- **Stream of commerce:** *Asahi Metal v. Superior Court* (1987) split (O'Connor "something more"/Brennan "awareness"); *J. McIntyre Machinery v. Nicastro* (2011) (plurality: defendant must target the forum, one machine reaching it through a distributor is not enough; Breyer concurring on narrower grounds). Expect the exam to avoid pure plurality cases or to give facts that resolve it either way. [K]
- **E-commerce:** interactive website plus actual sales/shipments to forum residents shows purposeful availment; a merely accessible passive website does not. (*Zippo* sliding scale is lower-court, not Supreme Court.) [K]
- **Quasi in rem:** *Shaffer v. Heitner* (1977): all assertions of jurisdiction are judged by the *International Shoe* standard; property in the State is only one contact. [K]

**Raising it.** Rule 12(b)(2). Waived if omitted from a first Rule 12 motion (12(g)(2), 12(h)(1)(A)) or not raised in the first responsive pleading (12(h)(1)(B)). A defendant who defaults can attack a resulting judgment collaterally; a defendant who appears and litigates cannot (*Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites* (1982) [K]). On a default-judgment attack under Rule 60(b)(4) the "reasonable time" limit of 60(c)(1) applies: *Coney Island Auto Parts v. Burton* (Jan. 20, 2026). [V via CRS]

### 6.2 Black-letter rules: service of process and notice

- **Constitutional notice:** notice "reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances," to apprise interested parties of the action and give them an opportunity to object; publication is not adequate for parties whose names and addresses are known (*Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank*, 1950) [K].
- **Rule 4(c):** a summons must be served **with** a copy of the complaint; any non-party **18 or older** may serve, so the plaintiff's lawyer may, but a party may not. [V]
- **Individuals (4(e)):** (1) follow state law of the forum State or of the State where service is made, or (2) deliver personally; leave a copy at the individual's **dwelling or usual place of abode with someone of suitable age and discretion who resides there**; or deliver to an agent authorized by appointment or law. Certified mail alone is valid only if state law allows it under (e)(1). [V]
- **Corporations/associations (4(h)(1)):** state-law methods, or deliver to an **officer, a managing or general agent, or any other agent authorized by appointment or law** (plus mailing if a statute so requires). [V]
- **Waiver (4(d)):** plaintiff may send a notice and request with two copies of the waiver form and a prepaid return; defendant gets **30 days** to return (60 if abroad); a timely waiver gives the defendant **60 days from the date the request was sent** to answer (90 if abroad); a waiver waives only service objections, **not** objections to PJ or venue; a US defendant who refuses without good cause bears later service expenses. [V]
- **Timing (4(m)):** **90 days** after filing; court must dismiss without prejudice or order service by a specified time; if plaintiff shows **good cause** the court **must** extend; the court may also extend without good cause (*Henderson v. United States*, 1996 [K]). Does not apply to foreign service under 4(f). [V]
- **Defects:** 12(b)(4) (form of the summons/process) and 12(b)(5) (manner of service): both waivable. Proof of service by the server's affidavit; failure to prove service does not affect its validity (4(l)). [V]
- **Murphy Bros.:** service under Rule 4 is a prerequisite to the running of the removal clock; mere receipt of a courtesy copy is not enough [K].

### 6.3 Fact patterns

**PJ-1 (general jurisdiction, Daimler).** A Delaware corporation headquartered in Illinois makes 8% of its national sales in Texas through 40 stores there. A passenger injured in an Oregon accident involving the corporation's equipment sues it in a Texas state court. The corporation has no connection to Texas related to this accident. Does the Texas court have personal jurisdiction?
(A) Yes, because the corporation does continuous and systematic business in Texas. (B) Yes, because Texas has a strong interest in providing a forum for the plaintiff. (C) **No, because the claim does not arise from the corporation's Texas contacts and the corporation is not "at home" in Texas.** (D) No, because Texas stores do not count as contacts.
*Best: C.* A applies the pre-*Daimler* standard. B ignores the minimum-contacts prerequisite. D is false (the contacts count; they are just not enough).

**PJ-2 (specific jurisdiction, relates to).** A tractor manufacturer incorporated in Iowa heavily advertises, sells and services its tractors through dealers in Kansas. A Kansas farmer buys a used tractor of that model from a private seller (the tractor was first sold in Nebraska). A defect injures him in Kansas, and he sues the manufacturer in Kansas. The manufacturer moves to dismiss for lack of PJ. How should the court rule?
(A) Grant, because the specific tractor was not sold by the manufacturer in Kansas. (B) Grant, because the manufacturer is not "at home" in Kansas. (C) **Deny, because the manufacturer purposefully serves the Kansas market for this model and the injury arose out of or relates to those contacts in Kansas.** (D) Deny, because the plaintiff is a Kansas citizen.
*Best: C* (*Ford*). A insists on strict causation, rejected in *Ford*. B conflates general and specific jurisdiction. D relies on plaintiff residence alone.

**PJ-3 (BMS).** A product maker is incorporated and headquartered in Delaware and New York. Seven hundred plaintiffs from many States, who bought, used and were injured by its drug outside California, join in a California state-court suit with some California residents. The manufacturer sells the drug in California but did not develop, make, label, or market it to the nonresident plaintiffs there. Can the California court hear the nonresidents' claims?
(A) Yes, because the manufacturer has sales in California. (B) Yes, because the claims are identical to the residents' claims. (C) **No, because the nonresidents' claims do not arise out of or relate to the manufacturer's California contacts.** (D) No, because the manufacturer is not a California citizen.
*Best: C* (*BMS*).

**PJ-4 (Walden).** A Georgia police officer, knowing two Nevada residents are Nevadans, seizes cash from them at a Georgia airport and files a false affidavit in Georgia. The residents sue him in a Nevada court for the harm they felt in Nevada. Does Nevada have PJ over the officer?
(A) Yes, because the officer knew the plaintiffs lived in Nevada and the harm was felt there. (B) Yes, because intentional torts always support jurisdiction where the effects are felt. (C) **No, because the officer's own conduct was in Georgia and he created no contacts with Nevada itself.** (D) No, because police officers are immune from suit.
*Best: C* (*Walden*). A and B say what *Walden* rejected.

**PJ-5 (Mallory consent).** A State statute provides that any out-of-state corporation registering to do business there "consents to personal jurisdiction in the courts of this State on any cause of action." A corporation registers and later is sued in that State by a resident on a claim arising entirely in another State. The corporation objects on due-process grounds. Result?
(A) PJ lacking, because the corporation is not at home there. (B) PJ lacking, because the claim does not arise from forum contacts. (C) **PJ proper; the Due Process Clause does not bar jurisdiction based on consent obtained through such a registration statute.** (D) PJ lacking, because consent can never be implied.
*Best: C* (*Mallory*). The item MUST state the statute's wording and be tagged as a five-justice outcome.

**PJ-6 (tag jurisdiction).** A resident of State A makes a one-day business trip to State B. While eating lunch there, he is handed a summons and complaint in a suit by a State B resident for an unrelated contract claim. He moves to dismiss for lack of PJ. How should the court rule?
(A) Grant, because the claim is unrelated to his State B contacts. (B) Grant, because a one-day visit is not minimum contacts. (C) **Deny, because he was personally served while voluntarily present in State B.** (D) Deny, but only if he intended to remain.
*Best: C* (*Burnham*). Trap variant: lured into State B by a trick promise to meet, then served: defeated.

**PJ-7 (waiver by omission).** A defendant served in a federal action files a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6). The court denies it. The defendant then files an answer, raising for the first time lack of personal jurisdiction. The plaintiff moves to strike that defense. The court should:
(A) Deny; personal jurisdiction can be raised at any time. (B) Deny; defenses can be added by amended answer as of right. (C) **Grant; the defense was available but omitted from the earlier Rule 12 motion and was therefore waived.** (D) Grant; personal jurisdiction objections must be raised by a motion filed with the complaint.
*Best: C* (12(g)(2), 12(h)(1)(A)). Contrast: failure to state a claim and SMJ are not waived.

**PJ-8 (e-commerce).** A craft seller incorporated and based in Maine runs an interactive website, advertises nationally, and ships a custom wood-burning stove to a buyer in Ohio. The stove explodes and injures the buyer in Ohio. The buyer sues in Ohio. The seller moves to dismiss for lack of PJ. Result?
(A) Granted, because the seller has no physical presence in Ohio. (B) Granted, because websites cannot create jurisdiction. (C) **Denied, because the seller purposefully sold and shipped the product into Ohio and the claim arises from that sale.** (D) Denied, because Ohio law provides a remedy.
*Best: C.* D confuses the forum's interest with contacts.

**SV-1 (abode service).** A process server hands a summons and complaint to the defendant's neighbor at the neighbor's house and tells her to give it to the defendant. The defendant later gets it. Is service effective under Rule 4(e)?
(A) Yes, because the defendant received actual notice. (B) Yes, because any adult may be given papers for delivery. (C) **No, because the papers were not delivered to the defendant, left at her dwelling with a resident of suitable age and discretion, or given to an authorized agent.** (D) No, because Rule 4 requires service only by certified mail.
*Best: C.* (Actual notice does not cure defective service in the usual case. Some courts are liberal; the exam stem should say a motion under 12(b)(5) was made.)

**SV-2 (waiver of service).** A plaintiff mails a defendant a request for waiver under Rule 4(d) on January 3, with two copies of the form and a prepaid return envelope. The defendant signs and returns it on January 20. By what date must she answer, and what objections does the waiver preserve?
(A) Jan 24; all objections including PJ and venue are waived. (B) Jan 24; PJ and venue are preserved. (C) **March 4 (60 days after the request was sent); PJ and venue objections are preserved, and only service objections are given up.** (D) March 21; all objections are preserved.
*Best: C.* (Jan 3 + 60 days = March 4 in a non-leap year.) Avoid leap-year dates in the app item. 

**SV-3 (Rule 4(m)).** A plaintiff files a complaint on March 1 and does not serve the defendant. On June 15 the defendant moves to dismiss for failure to serve within 90 days. The plaintiff has no good-cause explanation and the statute of limitations has now run. The court:
(A) Must dismiss with prejudice. (B) Must dismiss without prejudice. (C) **May dismiss without prejudice or order that service be made within a specified time, and may extend the time even absent good cause.** (D) Must extend, because dismissal would bar the claim.
*Best: C.* B and A treat dismissal as mandatory; D misreads the "must extend if good cause" sentence.

**SV-4 (Mullane).** A trustee sends notice of a proceeding by publication in a local newspaper to beneficiaries whose names and addresses are in the trustee's files. A beneficiary challenges the adequacy of notice. Result?
(A) Publication suffices; the proceeding is in rem. (B) **Publication is constitutionally inadequate for beneficiaries whose names and addresses are known; mail or similar notice reasonably calculated to reach them is required.** (C) Notice is unnecessary; the beneficiaries can learn of the case from the docket. (D) Publication suffices if the notice is run for four consecutive weeks.
*Best: B.* 

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "At home = domicile or incorporation + headquarters. Everything else needs the claim to arise from, or relate to, what the defendant did in that State." / "Skip the 12(b)(2) in your first motion and you've waived it for good."

## 7. I.D Venue, transfer, forum non conveniens (cp4)

### 7.1 Black-letter rules

- **Venue is statutory, not constitutional, and not jurisdictional.** An objection is waived if omitted from the first Rule 12 response (12(h)(1)), and parties may agree on a forum by a forum-selection clause. [V for 12(h); K]
- **28 USC 1391(b) - where a civil action may be brought** [V]: (1) a district in which **any** defendant resides, **if all defendants are residents of the State** in which the district is located; (2) a district in which **a substantial part of the events or omissions** giving rise to the claim occurred, **or** a substantial part of the **property** that is the subject of the action is located; (3) **only if there is no district** in which the action may otherwise be brought, any district in which any defendant is **subject to the court's personal jurisdiction** for the action (fallback). Several districts can satisfy (b)(2) (no "best" venue).
- **Residency, 1391(c)** [V]: natural person (incl. lawful permanent resident alien) resides where **domiciled**; an **entity with capacity to sue and be sued** (corporation or unincorporated) as a **defendant** resides in any district where it is **subject to personal jurisdiction with respect to the civil action in question**, and as a **plaintiff** only where it has its **principal place of business**; a defendant not residing in the US may be sued in any district, and its joinder is disregarded in applying (b)(1)-(3).
- **1391(d):** in a State with more than one district, a corporate defendant is deemed to reside in any district in which its contacts would suffice for jurisdiction if that district were a separate State; otherwise in the district with the most significant contacts. [V]
- **Improper venue remedy, 1406(a):** the court "shall dismiss, or if it be in the interest of justice, transfer" to a district where it could have been brought; transfer is available even if the transferor court lacks PJ over the defendant (*Goldlawr v. Heiman*, 1962) [K]; 1406(b): no effect on jurisdiction if no timely and sufficient objection. [V text] Also **28 USC 1631** transfer to cure want of jurisdiction, treated as filed on the original date. [V]
- **Convenience transfer, 1404(a)** [V text]: "For the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice," a district court may transfer to any other district or division **where it might have been brought** or to which **all parties have consented**. Available even where venue was proper. Factors: private (access to proof, witnesses, cost) and public (court congestion, local interest, law); plaintiff's choice gets deference [K].
- **Law applied after transfer** [K for Van Dusen and Ferens; V for Atlantic Marine]: after a **1404(a)** transfer, the transferee court applies the choice-of-law rules (and thus law) the **transferor** State would have applied (*Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 1964; true even when the plaintiff sought the transfer, *Ferens v. John Deere*, 1990). After a **1406(a)/1631** transfer, the transferee's own law applies. **Exception:** where the transfer enforces a valid **forum-selection clause**, the transferee applies its own State's choice-of-law rules (*Atlantic Marine*).
- **Forum-selection clauses:** *Atlantic Marine Construction v. U.S. District Court* (2013, unanimous): the proper vehicle is a 1404(a) motion (when the clause points to another federal district) or forum non conveniens (state or foreign court), not 1406(a) or Rule 12(b)(3), because "improper" venue is determined only by the venue statutes; a valid clause is given controlling weight in all but the most exceptional cases; the plaintiff's choice of forum gets no weight; the private-interest factors are deemed to favor the clause; only public-interest factors are weighed, and the party defying the clause bears the burden. [V]
- **Forum non conveniens (common law)** [K; Piper Aircraft v. Reyno 1981, Gulf Oil v. Gilbert 1947, Sinochem 2007]: a court with proper jurisdiction and venue may **dismiss** (not transfer) when another forum, outside the federal system (a foreign country or a state court), is far more convenient. Requirements: an **adequate alternative forum** (defendant amenable; remedy not so clearly inadequate as no remedy; an unfavorable change in law is not by itself inadequate) and balance of **private** and **public** interest factors; a foreign plaintiff's choice gets less deference; a court may dismiss on FNC before resolving SMJ or PJ (*Sinochem*). Dismissal may be conditioned (e.g., consent to jurisdiction, waiver of limitations).
- **Multidistrict litigation** (28 USC 1407, JPML) exists; not on the outline; one recognition item at most [K].

### 7.2 Fact patterns

**VN-1 (1391(b)(1) vs (b)(2)).** A plaintiff sues two defendants, a California resident and a Nevada resident, in a federal district in California on a state-law contract claim. A substantial part of the performance occurred in Oregon. Can venue in California be justified under 1391(b)(1)?
(A) Yes, because one defendant resides there. (B) Yes, because the plaintiff chose it. (C) **No; (b)(1) requires that all defendants reside in the State where the district sits.** (D) No, because venue lies only where the plaintiff resides.
*Best: C.* (Venue would lie in Oregon under (b)(2).)

**VN-2 (entity defendant).** A plaintiff in State A sues a State B-based retailer in a State A federal district, where the retailer sold the plaintiff the defective product through that district's store. The retailer is subject to specific PJ in State A. Is venue proper?
(A) No, because the retailer resides only in State B. (B) **Yes, because an entity defendant resides for venue in any district where it is subject to personal jurisdiction in that action, and a substantial part of the events occurred there.** (C) No, because venue for corporations is only where incorporated. (D) Yes, but only if the plaintiff also resides there.
*Best: B.*

**VN-3 (improper venue remedy and waiver).** A plaintiff files in a district where no defendant resides and nothing happened. The defendant files a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, loses, and later answers without raising venue. A year later it moves to dismiss for improper venue. Result?
(A) Granted; venue can be challenged any time. (B) Granted; venue is jurisdictional. (C) **Denied; the venue objection was waived by omission from the first Rule 12 motion.** (D) Denied; improper venue can never be raised in federal court.
*Best: C.* Companion: had the defendant timely raised it, the court would dismiss or transfer under 1406(a).

**VN-4 (1404(a) and Van Dusen).** A New York plaintiff, injured in Texas by a Texas defendant, sues in New York federal court on a state-law tort claim. New York's choice-of-law rules would apply Texas law, and the court transfers the case under 1404(a) to the Northern District of Texas. Which State's choice-of-law rules apply in the Texas court?
(A) Texas's, because the case is now in Texas. (B) **New York's, because the transferee court applies the law the transferor would have applied after a convenience transfer.** (C) Federal common law choice-of-law rules. (D) The plaintiff's choice.
*Best: B.* 

**VN-5 (forum non conveniens).** A foreign plaintiff injured in a crash abroad sues in US federal court, where the manufacturer is at home. Nearly all witnesses, evidence, and the plaintiff are abroad; the foreign country has courts that would hear the case and give a remedy, but its law is less favorable to the plaintiff. The court should:
(A) Deny; the less favorable law makes the foreign forum inadequate. (B) Transfer to the foreign court under 1404(a). (C) **Likely dismiss on forum non conveniens, since an unfavorable change in law alone does not make the foreign forum inadequate and private and public factors favor the foreign forum.** (D) Dismiss for lack of venue.
*Best: C.* B: 1404(a) transfers only among US federal districts.

**VN-6 (Atlantic Marine).** A contract between a subcontractor and general contractor says disputes must be litigated in State X federal court. The subcontractor sues in State Y federal court, where venue would otherwise be proper. The defendant moves to dismiss for improper venue under 1406(a). The court should:
(A) Grant; the clause makes venue improper. (B) Deny; the plaintiff's choice should be respected. (C) **Treat the motion as a 1404(a) motion and transfer to the chosen forum absent extraordinary circumstances, giving no weight to the plaintiff's choice.** (D) Deny because forum-selection clauses are unenforceable in federal court.
*Best: C.*

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Venue: where the defendants live (all in one State), where the action happened, or the last-resort district. Wrong venue: dismiss or transfer. Right venue but inconvenient: transfer. Foreign forum better: dismiss."

## 8. II Law applied by federal courts: Erie, Klaxon, federal common law (cp5)

### 8.1 Black-letter rules

- **Erie R.R. v. Tompkins (1938):** there is no general federal common law; in diversity (and in supplemental state claims, *Gibbs*) a federal court applies **state substantive law**, including the state's choice-of-law rules, and federal procedure. [K]
- **The modern two-track analysis (Hanna v. Plumer, 1965)** [K for Hanna; V for how Shady Grove and Berk apply it]:
  1. **Is there a federal constitutional provision, statute or Federal Rule of Civil Procedure that answers the question in dispute** (direct collision; "sufficiently broad to control the issue")? If yes and it is valid, it **applies** and displaces conflicting state law. A Federal Rule is valid if it falls within the Rules Enabling Act, 28 USC 2072: it must be arguably procedural ("really regulates procedure") and must not "abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right". No Erie "outcome" inquiry. *Hanna* (Rule 4(d)(1) in-hand service beat Massachusetts executor-service statute); *Burlington Northern v. Woods* (1987) (Rule 38 penalty); *Shady Grove v. Allstate* (2010, 5-4 on the result: Rule 23 governs whether the suit may proceed as a class action, so NY's bar on class actions for statutory penalties did not apply; Scalia opinion partly plurality; Stevens concurred in the judgment on a narrower test that asks whether the state law is bound up with substantive rights) [V]; **Berk v. Choy** (Jan. 20, 2026; Barrett for eight Justices, Jackson concurring in the judgment, who would rest on Rules 3 and 12(d)): Delaware's rule requiring an expert affidavit of merit to be filed with a malpractice complaint does not apply in federal court because Rules 8 and 12 answer the pleading question (a "short and plain statement" with no evidence required; Rule 12 permits dismissal on the merits only for failure to state a claim); the court needs no substance/procedure inquiry once a valid Rule answers the question. [V]
  2. **If no Federal Rule/statute is on point** (or it is not broad enough), apply the "**twin aims of Erie**": discourage forum shopping and avoid inequitable administration of the laws. If applying federal practice would produce a different outcome that significantly affects the choice of federal court or would treat in-State and out-of-State litigants unequally, state law applies (*Guaranty Trust v. York*, 1945 [K]). Countervailing federal interest: *Byrd v. Blue Ridge Rural Electric Coop.* (1958): federal courts may adhere to the **federal jury** practice even when state law assigns an issue to the judge. *Gasperini v. Center for Humanities* (1996): federal courts apply the state's standard of excessiveness for damages, with the federal district court deciding and the court of appeals reviewing for abuse of discretion. [K]
- **Classification cheat sheet** (for sorting items) [K]:
  - *State law governs (substantive for Erie):* the elements of claims and defenses; **statutes of limitation, accrual and tolling rules** (and, per *Walker v. Armco* (1980), Rule 3 does not displace a state rule that the action is commenced for limitations purposes by service); burdens of proof; state **choice-of-law** rules (**Klaxon Co. v. Stentor** (1941): the federal court sitting in diversity uses the choice-of-law rules of the State where it sits); damages caps and standards for excessiveness (*Gasperini*); the law of privilege in diversity (FRE 501).
  - *Federal law governs:* service, pleading, joinder, class certification, discovery, summary judgment, post-trial motions (all Federal Rules); the **right to a jury** (federal Seventh Amendment tradition, *Byrd*); venue and transfer statutes (**1404(a)** and a forum-selection clause: *Stewart Organization v. Ricoh*, 1988 [K]; *Atlantic Marine* [V]); federal courts' own docket management; sanctions.
- **Determining state law.** The federal court applies the law of the State's **highest court**; if that court has not spoken, it **predicts** what that court would hold; decisions of intermediate appellate courts are "datum for ascertaining state law" and are followed unless persuasive data show the highest court would decide otherwise (*West v. AT&T*, 1940; *Commissioner v. Estate of Bosch*, 1967 [K]). Federal courts may **certify** unresolved questions to the state's high court where a procedure exists (*Lehman Brothers v. Schein*, 1974 [K]).
- **Federal question cases:** federal law governs the federal claim; state law may be borrowed when the federal statute is silent (e.g., the most analogous limitations period unless a federal catch-all like 28 USC 1658 applies) [K, low priority].
- **Federal common law (MBE outline II.B; not in NextGen).** After *Erie*, federal common law exists only in areas of **uniquely federal interest**: the rights and obligations of the United States (*Clearfield Trust v. United States*, 1943: forged government checks), interstate disputes, admiralty, foreign relations/act of state (*Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*), and a few others; courts may *borrow* state law as the federal rule when no federal interest demands uniformity (*United States v. Kimbell Foods*, 1979). *Boyle v. United Technologies* (1988): the government-contractor defense to state-law design-defect claims is federal common law because of a significant conflict with a federal interest. Courts will not create private rights of action or remedies (e.g., contribution) where Congress has legislated (*Texas Industries v. Radcliff Materials*, 1981). The *preclusive effect of a federal-court judgment* is itself a matter of federal common law (*Semtek*, below). [K]

### 8.2 Fact patterns

**ER-1 (limitations).** A federal court is hearing a state-law negligence claim in diversity. The State's two-year statute of limitations expired before the plaintiff sued, but no federal statute limits the claim. The plaintiff argues the federal court should treat timeliness as a procedural matter governed by federal law. The court should:
(A) Apply no limitations period. (B) Apply the federal 4-year catch-all. (C) **Apply the State's statute of limitations because limitations rules are outcome-determinative and substantive for Erie purposes.** (D) Apply the rule the plaintiff prefers because timeliness is procedural.
*Best: C.* (In a federal-question case without its own period, different rules.)

**ER-2 (Rule on point, Shady Grove).** In a federal diversity action for statutory penalties, the plaintiff meets every requirement of Rule 23 and seeks to certify a class. A State statute bars class actions to recover statutory penalties. The defendant argues State law controls under Erie. How should the court rule?
(A) Deny certification, because the State statute is substantive. (B) Deny certification, because forum shopping would otherwise follow. (C) **Rule 23 governs whether the case can proceed as a class action and the court may certify, because a valid Federal Rule on point displaces the conflicting State law.** (D) Defer to the State statute only if the State's high court has construed it.
*Best: C* (*Shady Grove*). Note for the item: result had a 5-justice majority but splintered reasoning, so do not ask the examinee to choose between the plurality and Stevens's concurrence.

**ER-3 (Berk v. Choy).** A State statute requires a plaintiff in a malpractice case to file with the complaint an affidavit from an expert stating the claim has merit; failure requires dismissal. A plaintiff in federal diversity court files no affidavit. The defendant moves to dismiss under the State statute. The court should:
(A) Dismiss, because the statute is substantive and State law governs under Erie. (B) Dismiss, because the plaintiff must follow State pleading rules. (C) **Deny the motion; Rules 8 and 12 answer what a complaint must contain and when a claim may be dismissed on the merits, so the State affidavit requirement does not apply in federal court.** (D) Stay the case until the plaintiff obtains the affidavit.
*Best: C.* Source: *Berk v. Choy* (2026). [V] Flag status "general" until verified against the slip opinion by a human.

**ER-4 (Klaxon).** A federal court in State A hears a diversity tort case about an accident in State B. State A's choice-of-law rules call for applying the law of the place of injury (State B). The plaintiff argues that the court should apply federal common-law choice-of-law principles instead. The court should:
(A) Apply State A's choice-of-law rules, which lead to State B's substantive law. (B) Apply federal common-law principles of the most significant relationship. (C) Apply State A's substantive law because it is the forum. (D) Apply the law of the plaintiff's domicile.
*Best: A* (**Klaxon**). Companion: after a 1404(a) transfer, the transferor's rules still apply (see VN-4).

**ER-5 (state law unsettled).** In a diversity case the State's highest court has never decided a point of contract law. An intermediate appellate court of that State decided it two years ago. A federal district judge thinks the intermediate court was wrong. The federal court:
(A) Must follow the intermediate court's decision. (B) Is free to apply federal common law. (C) **Must predict how the State's highest court would rule, treating the intermediate decision as persuasive and following it unless convinced the high court would decide otherwise.** (D) Must dismiss the case.
*Best: C.* (A is the trap: "must" is too strong.)

**ER-6 (federal jury right).** In a federal diversity action on a state-law claim, the State's practice assigns the issue to a judge, but the claim would have been triable to a jury at common law in 1791 (Seventh Amendment). Who decides?
(A) The judge, following State practice, because of Erie. (B) **The jury; the right to a jury in federal court is governed by federal law, and the federal interest in jury trial outweighs the state rule.** (C) The judge if the State legislature has declared the rule substantive. (D) The plaintiff chooses.
*Best: B* (*Byrd*).

**ER-7 (federal common law).** The United States sues a bank to recover on a Treasury check that was forged and negotiated. No federal statute controls. Which law governs the rights and duties on the check?
(A) The law of the State where the check was cashed, as a matter of Erie. (B) **Federal common law, because the rights and duties of the United States on its commercial paper are a uniquely federal interest.** (C) The Uniform Commercial Code as a federal statute. (D) No law applies.
*Best: B* (*Clearfield Trust*). MBE-only (outline II.B).

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Federal Rule on point and valid? It wins. No rule? Ask: would federal practice cause forum shopping or unequal treatment? Then state law." / "Federal court in diversity: state substance, federal procedure, forum State's conflicts rules."

## 9. III.B-III.D Pleadings, Rule 11, joinder, class actions (cp6, cp7)

### 9.1 Black-letter rules: pleadings (cp6)

- **Allowed pleadings (Rule 7(a))** [K]: complaint; answer; answer to a counterclaim; answer to a crossclaim; third-party complaint and answer; and a reply only if the court orders one. Motions are not pleadings.
- **Claim (Rule 8(a))** [V]: (1) short plain statement of the grounds for jurisdiction (unless existing jurisdiction needs no new support), (2) short plain statement showing the pleader is **entitled to relief**, (3) demand for relief (alternative or different types allowed). *Bell Atlantic v. Twombly* (2007) and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal* (2009): the complaint must contain **sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to state a claim that is plausible on its face**; labels, conclusions and formulaic recitals of elements are not entitled to the presumption of truth; parallel conduct alone does not plausibly show conspiracy; plausibility is context-specific and applies in all civil actions; *Conley*'s "no set of facts" formulation is retired. *Swierkiewicz* (2002): no need to plead a prima facie case in employment discrimination. [K]
- **Fraud and mistake (Rule 9(b))** [K]: circumstances constituting fraud or mistake pleaded **with particularity** (who, what, when, where, how); malice, intent, knowledge and other states of mind may be alleged generally.
- **Responding (Rule 8(b))** [V]: admit or deny each allegation; a general denial only if the party in good faith denies everything including jurisdictional grounds; lack of knowledge or information to form a belief operates as a denial; an allegation is **admitted** if a response is required and it is not denied (except allegations about the amount of damages).
- **Affirmative defenses (Rule 8(c))** [V]: must be stated affirmatively or are forfeited; list includes accord and satisfaction, arbitration and award, assumption of risk, contributory negligence, duress, estoppel, failure of consideration, fraud, illegality, laches, license, payment, release, res judicata, statute of frauds, statute of limitations, waiver. A mislabeled defense/counterclaim is treated as properly designated if justice requires.
- **Alternative and inconsistent pleading (Rule 8(d)(2)-(3))** allowed. [V]
- **Amendment (Rule 15)** [V]: (a)(1) once **as of course** within **21 days** after serving it or, if a responsive pleading is required, **21 days** after service of the responsive pleading or a Rule 12(b), (e) or (f) motion, whichever is earlier; (a)(2) otherwise only with written consent or **leave, freely given when justice so requires** (factors: undue delay, bad faith, repeated failure, prejudice, futility [K]); (a)(3) response to an amended pleading due within the time remaining or **14 days** after service of the amendment, whichever is later. (b) amendments during and after trial: issues tried by express or implied consent are treated as pleaded; a motion to amend to conform to the evidence may be made **at any time, even after judgment**. (c) **relation back**: an amendment relates back to the date of the original pleading when (A) the law that provides the applicable limitations period allows it, (B) the amendment asserts a claim or defense arising out of the same conduct, transaction or occurrence set out in the original pleading, or (C) the amendment changes the party or the naming of the party, (B) is satisfied, and within the Rule 4(m) period (90 days after filing) the new party (i) received notice enough to avoid prejudice in defending on the merits and (ii) knew or should have known the action would have been brought against it **but for a mistake concerning the proper party's identity**. *Krupski v. Costa Crociere* (2010): the inquiry is what the new party knew or should have known, not whether the plaintiff was careless or knew the right identity. Lack of knowledge about whom to sue (a "John Doe" defendant) is not a "mistake" under most courts' reading. [K for Krupski; V rule text]
- **(d) supplemental pleadings** cover events after the original pleading (on motion, reasonable notice, just terms). [V]
- **Scheduling-order deadlines:** after the Rule 16(b) deadline to amend has passed, an amendment requires "good cause" under Rule 16(b)(4) first (majority view), not just Rule 15's liberal standard. [V text of 16(b)(4); majority-view point K]

### 9.2 Black-letter rules: Rule 11

[V: full text] Every pleading, written motion and other paper must be signed by an attorney of record or unrepresented party. By "presenting" a paper (signing, filing, submitting, **or later advocating** it) the person certifies, to the best of his knowledge, information and belief, **formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances**: (1) it is **not presented for an improper purpose** (harass, delay, increase costs); (2) **legal contentions are warranted by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument** for extending, modifying or reversing it or establishing new law; (3) **factual contentions have evidentiary support** or, if specifically so identified, **will likely have evidentiary support after a reasonable opportunity for discovery**; (4) **denials** of factual contentions are warranted on the evidence or reasonably based on belief or lack of information.
- **Procedure:** a motion for sanctions **must be made separately** from other motions, describe the conduct, and be **served** under Rule 5 but **not filed or presented to the court** unless the challenged paper is not withdrawn or corrected within **21 days** after service (the **safe harbor**). The court may also act on its **own initiative** by an order to show cause (no safe harbor).
- **Sanction:** limited to what suffices to deter repetition; may be nonmonetary, a penalty paid to the court, or (on motion) reasonable fees and expenses directly resulting from the violation. A represented **party** cannot be monetarily sanctioned for a violation of (b)(2) (the legal-contentions requirement); the court cannot impose monetary sanctions on its own initiative unless it issued the show-cause order before voluntary dismissal or settlement. A law firm is jointly responsible absent exceptional circumstances.
- **Not covered:** Rule 11 does **not** apply to disclosures, discovery requests, responses, objections or motions under Rules 26-37 (those are governed by Rule 26(g) and Rule 37). 

### 9.3 Fact patterns: pleadings and Rule 11

**PL-1 (Twombly/Iqbal).** A complaint alleges that two competing grocers each raised prices of eggs by 10% on the same day and that "the defendants must have agreed to do so in violation of the Sherman Act." It alleges no facts suggesting any communication or agreement. The defendants move to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6). The court should:
(A) Deny, because the plaintiff need not plead facts, only claims. (B) Deny, because dismissal is improper unless the plaintiff can prove no set of facts entitling it to relief. (C) **Grant, because the complaint pleads parallel conduct and a bare conclusion of agreement, and does not allege facts that make the conspiracy plausible.** (D) Grant, because antitrust claims must be pleaded with particularity under Rule 9(b).
*Best: C.* B is the retired *Conley* standard. D confuses Rule 9(b) (fraud) with plausibility.

**PL-2 (admission by silence).** A defendant's answer denies paragraphs 1-6 of the complaint and says nothing about paragraph 7, which alleges that the defendant owned the car. The plaintiff moves for a ruling on paragraph 7. How is it treated?
(A) Denied, because Rule 8 presumes silence is denial. (B) **Admitted, because an allegation requiring a response that is not denied is admitted (unless it concerns the amount of damages).** (C) Admitted only if the plaintiff files a motion to compel. (D) Neither; it must be proved at trial.
*Best: B* (Rule 8(b)(6)).

**PL-3 (amendment as of course).** On day 1 the plaintiff serves a complaint; on day 10 the defendant serves a Rule 12(b)(6) motion; on day 25 the plaintiff serves an amended complaint without consent or leave. Is the amendment proper?
(A) No; leave is always required after a motion to dismiss. (B) Yes; the plaintiff may amend once as of course within 21 days after service of the 12(b) motion (until day 31). (C) No; the 21 days ran from service of the original complaint. (D) Yes, but only if the defendant has not yet answered.
*Best: B.* (Rule 15(a)(1)(B): 21 days after service of the 12(b) motion.) Wrong C is the common misreading; the "whichever is earlier" language applies to the responsive pleading or motion.

**PL-4 (relation back, mistake).** An injured passenger sues "Sea Tours, Inc." within the limitations period after reading its name on a boat. Seventy days later (after limitations ran) she learns that "Sea Tours Holdings, LLC" owns and operates the boat, and the two entities share an office, a lawyer, and insurance, and Holdings knew of the suit from the start. She amends to substitute Holdings. Does the amendment relate back?
(A) No; adding a new defendant after limitations ran never relates back. (B) No; she was careless in not finding the correct entity. (C) **Yes; the same occurrence is pleaded, Holdings had notice within the 90-day Rule 4(m) window, and it knew or should have known it would have been sued but for a mistake as to identity.** (D) Yes, because Rule 15(a) allows amendment at any time.
*Best: C* (*Krupski*). B is the tempting wrong answer: the plaintiff's diligence is not the test.

**PL-5 (relation back, John Doe).** A plaintiff sues "Officer John Doe" for excessive force before limitations runs. After limitations runs she learns the officer's name and amends to name him. In the jurisdiction's majority view under Rule 15(c)(1)(C), does the amendment relate back?
(A) Yes; the same conduct is alleged. (B) **Generally no; lack of knowledge about whom to sue is not a "mistake concerning the proper party's identity".** (C) Yes; courts must liberally construe Rule 15(c). (D) No; Doe pleading is forbidden in federal court.
*Best: B.* [K; flag "majority view"; the question must say "federal court applying Rule 15(c)(1)(C)" and the rule of the stem. Consider cutting this item if the reviewer finds the point unsettled.]

**PL-6 (affirmative defense forfeited).** A defendant answers a negligence complaint and does not mention the statute of limitations. At summary judgment, three months later, the defendant argues the claim is time-barred. The court should:
(A) Grant, because limitations is jurisdictional. (B) **Deny, unless the court grants leave to amend, because limitations is an affirmative defense that must be pleaded or it is forfeited.** (C) Deny, because limitations defenses are never allowed at summary judgment. (D) Grant, because the court can always raise limitations itself.
*Best: B.*

**PL-7 (trial amendment).** At trial, without objection, the plaintiff introduces evidence of an oral modification not pleaded. At the close of evidence, the defendant objects that the contract claim was not pleaded that way. The court:
(A) Must exclude the evidence. (B) **May treat the issue as tried by implied consent and permit the plaintiff to amend to conform to the evidence, even after judgment.** (C) Must declare a mistrial. (D) May amend only before trial.
*Best: B* (Rule 15(b)(2)). The facts should say the evidence came in without objection.

**R11-1 (safe harbor).** On Monday, the defendant's lawyer files with the court and serves a Rule 11 motion for sanctions against the plaintiff's lawyer for a frivolous claim, requesting immediate relief. The plaintiff's lawyer withdraws the claim 5 days later. Result?
(A) The motion is granted since the claim was frivolous when filed. (B) **The motion is improper because it was filed with the court before the 21-day safe harbor ran; the motion must be served but not filed during the safe harbor, and the withdrawal avoids sanctions.** (C) The court must award fees to the defendant as prevailing party. (D) Rule 11 requires a separate hearing.
*Best: B.*

**R11-2 (factual support).** A plaintiff's lawyer alleges that the defendant knew of a product defect, stating "on information and belief, this allegation will likely have evidentiary support after discovery," because the defendant controls the relevant records and the lawyer has a pattern of similar complaints. Rule 11?
(A) Violation; every factual contention must have evidentiary support when filed. (B) **No violation, because factual contentions specifically identified as likely to have evidentiary support after reasonable opportunity for discovery are allowed.** (C) Violation, because "information and belief" is forbidden. (D) No violation only if the court has already ordered discovery.
*Best: B* (11(b)(3)).

**R11-3 (discovery).** A defendant's lawyer serves boilerplate, baseless objections to every interrogatory to delay discovery. The plaintiff moves for sanctions under Rule 11. The court should:
(A) Grant under Rule 11(b)(1). (B) **Deny the Rule 11 motion; Rule 11 does not apply to discovery papers, which are governed by Rule 26(g) and Rule 37.** (C) Grant after the 21-day safe harbor. (D) Deny, because sanctions are unavailable for discovery abuse.
*Best: B.* D is wrong: other rules sanction it.

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Plausible beats possible: facts, not labels." / "Serve the Rule 11 motion; don't file it for 21 days." / "Rule 11 ends at discovery. Rules 26(g) and 37 take over."

### 9.4 Black-letter rules: joinder, impleader, intervention, class actions (cp7)

- **Claims (Rule 18(a))**: a party asserting a claim may join as many claims as it has against an opposing party, related or not [K]. 
- **Permissive joinder of parties (Rule 20(a))** [V]: plaintiffs (or defendants) may be joined if (A) their right to relief (or liability) is asserted jointly, severally or in the alternative with respect to or arising out of **the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences**, AND (B) any **question of law or fact common** to all will arise. Not every party need be interested in all relief; Rule 20(b) lets the court protect parties (e.g., separate trials). Misjoinder is not a ground for dismissal; the court may add or drop parties or sever claims (Rule 21 [K]); consolidation and separate trials (Rule 42 [K]). NextGen mentions the "court's overriding power to sever."
- **Counterclaims (Rule 13)** [V]: **compulsory** if the claim arises out of the transaction or occurrence that is the subject of the opposing party's claim and does not require adding a party the court cannot reach; exceptions: the claim was already the subject of another pending action, or the opposing party sued by attachment without establishing PJ and the pleader asserts no counterclaim. Failure to plead a compulsory counterclaim **bars it later** (waiver/preclusion). **Permissive** counterclaims: any other claim against an opposing party. A counterclaim need not reduce the opposing party's recovery. Answer to a counterclaim: 21 days (12(a)(1)(B)).
- **Crossclaims (13(g))** [V]: against a **coparty**, arising from the same transaction/occurrence as the original action or counterclaim (or relating to property), or claiming the coparty is or may be liable to the crossclaimant for all or part of a claim against the crossclaimant. Always **optional** (no waiver by omission). Rule 13(h): Rules 19 and 20 govern adding parties to a counterclaim or crossclaim.
- **Impleader / third-party practice (Rule 14)** [V]: a **defending party** may serve a complaint on a nonparty "who is or may be liable to it for all or part of the claim against it" (derivative liability: indemnity, contribution, subrogation, warranty); **without leave if filed within 14 days after serving its original answer**, otherwise with leave. The third-party defendant must assert defenses against the third-party plaintiff (Rule 12) and compulsory counterclaims (13(a)); it may also assert against the plaintiff any defense the third-party plaintiff has against the plaintiff's claim, and may assert claims arising from the same transaction against the plaintiff. The plaintiff may assert claims against the third-party defendant arising from the same transaction. A plaintiff may implead on a counterclaim (14(b)). **Not** proper to implead someone merely because they are the "real" wrongdoer who caused the plaintiff's injury and not the defendant: that is a defense, not derivative liability. SMJ: the third-party claim is within supplemental jurisdiction; the plaintiff's claim against the third-party defendant is barred in diversity cases by 1367(b) (*Kroger*). PJ: Rule 4(k)(1)(B) 100-mile bulge. Venue: not independently required for ancillary claims [K].
- **Required parties (Rule 19)** [V], two-step: (1) **Is the absentee required** under 19(a)(1): (A) complete relief cannot be accorded among existing parties without the absentee, OR (B) the absentee claims an interest relating to the subject of the action and disposing of the action in their absence may (i) impair or impede their ability to protect the interest, or (ii) leave an existing party subject to a substantial risk of double, multiple or inconsistent obligations. (2) **Is joinder feasible**: the absentee must be subject to service of process and joinder must not destroy SMJ (and a joined party who objects to venue must be dismissed, 19(a)(3)). If feasible, the court must order joinder (the plaintiff may be made an involuntary plaintiff if refusing). If not feasible, the court decides "in equity and good conscience" whether to proceed or dismiss, weighing 19(b): (1) prejudice to the absentee or existing parties from a judgment in their absence; (2) whether protective provisions or shaping relief can lessen the prejudice; (3) whether a judgment in their absence would be adequate; (4) whether the plaintiff would have an **adequate remedy if the action is dismissed** for nonjoinder (e.g., in state court). A Rule 19 dismissal is **not** an adjudication on the merits (41(b)). Failure to join may be raised under 12(b)(7) and is **not** waived by omission (12(h)(2)). **Joint tortfeasors** (and generally joint obligors) are not required parties: *Temple v. Synthes* (1990) [K].
- **Intervention (Rule 24)** [V]: on **timely motion**, the court **must** permit intervention of **right** by anyone who (1) is given an unconditional right by federal statute, or (2) claims an **interest relating to the property or transaction** that is the subject of the action, is so situated that disposing of the action may **as a practical matter impair or impede** the ability to protect it, **unless existing parties adequately represent that interest**. The court **may** permit **permissive intervention** by anyone with a conditional statutory right or a **claim or defense that shares a common question of law or fact** with the main action; it must consider **undue delay or prejudice** to the original parties. Motion must state the grounds and be accompanied by a **pleading** setting out the claim or defense. Government officers/agencies may intervene permissively when a claim rests on a statute they administer. In diversity cases, 1367(b) bars supplemental jurisdiction over claims by persons intervening as plaintiffs under Rule 24 that are inconsistent with 1332. An intervenor seeking different relief than the existing parties must have Article III standing (*Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates*, 2017 [K]).
- **Interpleader (Rule 22 and 28 USC 1335)** [K]: a stakeholder facing adverse claims to the same fund may force claimants into one action. Rule 22 interpleader needs ordinary SMJ (complete diversity between stakeholder and claimants, $75k, or federal question); **statutory** interpleader (1335) needs only **minimal** diversity among adverse claimants and $500, with nationwide service (28 USC 2361). Low priority.
- **Class actions (Rule 23)** [V]: the class representative must satisfy **(a)** numerosity (joinder impracticable), commonality (questions of law or fact common to the class; *Wal-Mart v. Dukes* (2011): a common contention capable of classwide resolution, where the answer drives the resolution [K]), typicality, and adequacy of representation, **and one of (b)**: (b)(1) risk of inconsistent adjudications or impairing nonparties' interests (limited fund etc.); (b)(2) the opposing party acted on grounds generally applicable to the class so final injunctive/declaratory relief is appropriate (no individualized damages; *Wal-Mart*); (b)(3) **questions common to the class predominate** over individual questions and a class action is **superior** (factors: members' interest in individual control, existing litigation, desirability of forum, manageability). *Amchem v. Windsor* (1997) (predominance is demanding; settlement class still must satisfy Rule 23(a), (b) except manageability), *Comcast v. Behrend* (2013) (damages model must fit the liability theory) [K]. Certification at an "early practicable time" by order defining the class and appointing class counsel; **(b)(3) classes: best practicable notice, including individual notice to identifiable members, with the right to opt out**; (b)(1)/(b)(2) classes: no required opt-out; judgment binds all class members (and those who didn't opt out in (b)(3)). **Settlement/dismissal requires court approval** after notice and hearing if "fair, reasonable, and adequate". **Rule 23(f):** petition for permission to appeal a certification order to the court of appeals within **14 days** (discretionary; no automatic stay). Each class member must have Article III standing to recover damages (*TransUnion v. Ramirez*, 2021 [K]). Absentees can be bound without PJ over them if given notice/opt-out and adequate representation (*Phillips Petroleum v. Shutts*, 1985 [K]). **CAFA** (28 USC 1332(d)) [V]: > $5,000,000, minimal diversity; (100+ members and home-state/local-controversy exceptions [K]); a named plaintiff's stipulation to damages below $5M does not defeat it (*Standard Fire v. Knowles*, 2013 [K]).

### 9.5 Fact patterns: joinder

**JN-1 (compulsory counterclaim).** A driver sues another driver for injuries from a collision. The defendant, who was also injured and whose car was damaged in the same collision, answers without pleading a counterclaim. The case ends in a judgment for the plaintiff. The defendant later sues for her own damages from the collision. Result?
(A) She may proceed; counterclaims are always optional. (B) She may proceed because the first case was decided on the merits for the plaintiff. (C) **Her claim is barred because it arose out of the same transaction or occurrence and was a compulsory counterclaim that she did not plead.** (D) She may proceed if filed within the limitations period.
*Best: C.*

**JN-2 (crossclaim is optional).** Two defendants are sued for the same crash. Defendant 1 believes Defendant 2 is liable to her for contribution but asserts no crossclaim. After judgment, she sues Defendant 2 for contribution. Result?
(A) Barred; crossclaims are compulsory. (B) **Not barred by Rule 13; crossclaims against a coparty are permissive.** (C) Barred because she was a party to the first action. (D) Barred unless she moves under Rule 60.
*Best: B.*

**JN-3 (impleader is for derivative liability).** A shopper sues a store for a slip on a wet floor. The store (a) wants to implead its janitorial contractor, which agreed by contract to indemnify the store for such claims; and (b) argues that a different customer, who spilled the liquid, is the only person at fault. Which is proper impleader?
(A) Both. (B) Neither. (C) **Only (a), because the contractor may be liable to the store for all or part of the plaintiff's claim; (b) is a defense on causation, not derivative liability.** (D) Only (b).
*Best: C.* (If the State allows contribution among tortfeasors, (b) might be proper as a contribution claim; the stem must say that State law gives the store no right of contribution.)

**JN-4 (misjoinder).** A plaintiff sues Defendant 1 for a contract claim and joins Defendant 2 on an unrelated tort claim from a different occasion. Defendant 2 moves to dismiss for misjoinder. The court should:
(A) Dismiss the entire case. (B) Dismiss Defendant 2 with prejudice. (C) **Deny dismissal for misjoinder but drop or sever the claims against Defendant 2, because Rule 20(a)(2) requires the same transaction or series and a common question.** (D) Allow the joinder because Rule 18 permits unrelated claims against any defendant.
*Best: C.* D: Rule 18 applies only to claims against an **opposing party already properly joined**.

**JN-5 (Rule 19, joint tortfeasor).** A plaintiff sues one of three joint tortfeasors. The defendant moves to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(7) for failure to join the other two. The court should:
(A) Grant; all joint tortfeasors are required parties. (B) **Deny; joint tortfeasors are not required parties merely because they are jointly and severally liable, and the defendant may implead them for contribution.** (C) Grant; the case is stayed pending joinder. (D) Deny because Rule 19 applies only to contracts.
*Best: B.* [K: Temple v. Synthes]

**JN-6 (Rule 19(b)).** A State A plaintiff sues a State B partner in federal diversity over ownership of property. A co-owner, a State A citizen, claims an interest in the property, and a judgment in his absence would impair his ability to protect it. Joining the co-owner would destroy complete diversity. The court should:
(A) Join the co-owner anyway and keep the case. (B) **Weigh the Rule 19(b) factors, and, if the case cannot proceed in equity and good conscience without the co-owner (and the plaintiff has an adequate remedy in state court), dismiss without prejudice.** (C) Dismiss with prejudice. (D) Ignore the co-owner and enter judgment.
*Best: B.*

**JN-7 (intervention of right).** After a landowner sues a city over a zoning ordinance, a neighbor who would lose a valuable view if the ordinance is struck down moves to intervene as a defendant. The city is defending vigorously, but only on the ground of preserving public revenue. The motion is timely. Should the court allow intervention?
(A) No; the city adequately represents all interests. (B) No; intervention is available only before the answer. (C) **Yes; the neighbor claims an interest in the subject, disposition may impair it, and the existing parties may not adequately represent it.** (D) Yes, but only permissive, never as of right.
*Best: C.*

**JN-8 (class notice).** A (b)(3) class is certified. The court orders notice by a single posting at the courthouse; the class members' names and addresses are available from the defendant's records. A class member objects to the adequacy of notice. Result?
(A) Posting suffices if it is public. (B) **Inadequate; Rule 23(c)(2)(B) requires the best notice practicable, including individual notice to members who can be identified through reasonable effort, plus information on the right to opt out.** (C) Notice is not required in (b)(3) classes. (D) Notice is required only in (b)(1) classes.
*Best: B.*

**JN-9 (commonality).** Female employees at 3,000 stores sue the retailer, claiming discrimination in pay and promotion because local managers had discretion; there is no company-wide policy. They move to certify a nationwide class. Court should:
(A) Certify because they all work for the same employer. (B) **Deny; no common contention capable of classwide resolution is shown.** (C) Certify under (b)(2) because injunctive relief is sought. (D) Certify, because employer size shows numerosity.
*Best: B* (*Wal-Mart*). 

**JN-10 (Rule 23(f)).** A district court grants class certification on March 1. The defendant files a petition for permission to appeal on March 20. The court of appeals:
(A) Must hear the appeal as of right. (B) **Dismisses the petition as untimely; Rule 23(f) petitions are due within 14 days of the order.** (C) Hears it, because 30 days applies. (D) Hears it, because certification orders are final judgments.
*Best: B.*

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Same transaction or occurrence? You must counterclaim. Against a coparty? You may crossclaim. Someone owes you for it? Implead." / "Rule 19: needed, can they be joined, if not, proceed or dismiss?"

## 10. III.E-III.G Discovery, injunctions, ending without trial, pretrial conference (cp8, cp9)

### 10.1 Black-letter rules: discovery (cp8)

All rule text [V] unless marked.
- **Scope (26(b)(1)):** any **nonprivileged** matter **relevant to any party's claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case**, considering: importance of the issues, amount in controversy, parties' relative access to information, parties' resources, importance of the discovery in resolving the issues, and whether burden or expense outweighs likely benefit. Information **need not be admissible** to be discoverable.
- **Initial disclosures (26(a)(1)):** without waiting for a request: (i) names/contact info of individuals likely to have discoverable information the party may use (not solely impeachment) and the subjects; (ii) copies or description by category and location of documents, ESI and tangible things the party may use; (iii) a computation of each category of damages with supporting materials; (iv) any insurance agreement that may satisfy a judgment. Due **14 days after the Rule 26(f) conference** (30 days for parties served/joined later). Some case types exempt. A party is not excused because its investigation is incomplete.
- **Sequence and timing:** no discovery from any source **before the 26(f) conference** (26(d)(1)) except exempt proceedings, rule, stipulation or court order; **early Rule 34 requests** may be delivered more than **21 days** after service of the summons and complaint and are treated as served at the first 26(f) conference. Methods may otherwise be used in any sequence (26(d)(3)).
- **Rule 26(f) conference (NextGen names it):** parties must confer as soon as practicable and at least **21 days** before a scheduling conference or order is due to discuss claims and defenses, settlement, disclosures, preservation, and a proposed discovery plan; submit a written report within **14 days**; the plan addresses ESI, privilege-claim procedures (including a possible FRE 502 order), and limits.
- **Duty to supplement (26(e)):** timely supplement or correct disclosures and responses to interrogatories, RFPs and RFAs if materially incomplete or incorrect. Failure to disclose or supplement means the party **may not use** that information or witness on a motion, at a hearing or at trial **unless the failure was substantially justified or harmless** (37(c)(1)), plus possible expenses/instruction/other sanctions.
- **Experts (26(a)(2), 26(b)(4)):** disclose the identity of any witness who may present expert evidence; a **retained** expert must give a signed **report** (complete opinions and basis, data considered, exhibits, qualifications incl. 10 years of publications, a list of cases where the expert testified in the previous 4 years, compensation); disclosure at least **90 days before trial** (rebuttal: 30 days after the other side's). Draft reports and most attorney-expert communications are protected (exceptions: compensation, facts or data the lawyer provided and the expert considered, assumptions the lawyer supplied and the expert relied on). A **non-testifying consulting expert** is discoverable only under Rule 35(b) or on **exceptional circumstances**. The party seeking discovery from an expert pays a reasonable fee.
- **Privilege and work product:** attorney-client privilege (*Upjohn*, corporate employees [K]); **work product** (26(b)(3), *Hickman v. Taylor* [K]): documents prepared in anticipation of litigation by or for a party or its representative are discoverable only on a showing of **substantial need** and inability to get the substantial equivalent without **undue hardship**; **opinion work product** (mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal theories) is protected from compelled disclosure much more strongly; a person may obtain his own previous statement. Withholding privileged material requires an **express claim** and a description enabling assessment (26(b)(5)(A)); **clawback (26(b)(5)(B))**: after notice of a claim, the receiving party must promptly return, sequester or destroy, and not use the information until the claim is resolved. FRE 502(b),(d) limit waiver for inadvertent disclosure and allow protective orders that make disclosure non-waiving in other proceedings [K].
- **Protective orders (26(c)):** on motion with a certification of good-faith conferral, the court may for **good cause** protect a person from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense (forbid discovery, set terms, limit scope, seal, protect trade secrets...). 
- **ESI:** parties may withhold sources identified as **not reasonably accessible** because of undue burden or cost (the responding party must show it), but the court may order discovery for good cause (26(b)(2)(B)); form of production; proportionality; metadata. **Spoliation of ESI (37(e)):** if ESI that should have been preserved in anticipation or conduct of litigation is lost because a party failed to take reasonable steps and it cannot be restored or replaced: (1) on finding **prejudice**, measures no greater than necessary to cure; (2) only on finding of **intent to deprive**, the court may presume the information was unfavorable, instruct the jury to so presume, or dismiss/enter default. Negligence alone cannot support an adverse-inference instruction.
- **Tools:**
  - **Depositions (Rule 30)**: limit of **10 depositions per side**; one day of **7 hours**; leave required for more than 10, for re-deposing someone, or before 26(d); notice reasonable; **organization deponent (30(b)(6))** designates one or more persons to testify to information known or reasonably available to the organization on matters described with reasonable particularity; objections noted but examination proceeds, and a lawyer may instruct not to answer only to preserve a privilege, enforce a court-ordered limit, or present a 30(d)(3) motion; deponent may review and make changes within **30 days** if requested before the end of the deposition. Any party's deposition may be used by an adverse party for any purpose (Rule 32 [K]).
  - **Interrogatories (Rule 33)**: to parties only; **25** including discrete subparts; answered under oath within **30 days**; objections stated with specificity and waived if not timely; option to produce business records when the burden is the same; contention interrogatories allowed.
  - **Requests for production (Rule 34)**: to parties, for documents, ESI, tangible things, entry on land in the party's possession, custody or control; respond within **30 days**; objections must state specifics and whether materials are withheld; produce as kept in the usual course or organized to match requests. Nonparties: **Rule 45 subpoena**.
  - **Requests for admission (Rule 36)**: facts, application of law to fact, opinions on either, genuineness of documents; a matter is **admitted unless within 30 days** the party serves a signed answer or objection; lack-of-knowledge response only after reasonable inquiry; an admission is **conclusively established** for this action unless the court allows withdrawal or amendment (promotes merits, no prejudice); wrongfully denying later-proved matters can shift costs (37(c)(2)).
  - **Physical and mental exams (Rule 35)**: only by court order on motion for **good cause** when a party's physical or mental condition is **in controversy** (including a person in the party's custody or legal control); the order specifies time, place, manner, conditions, scope and the examiner; the examinee is entitled to the report on request.
  - **Subpoenas (Rule 45)**: compel nonparty attendance/production; served by any nonparty adult with one day's fee and mileage; compliance **within 100 miles** of where the person lives, works or regularly transacts business in person (trial: within the State in specified cases); recipient may object to document production within **14 days** (or before compliance if sooner); court **must** quash if it fails to allow reasonable time, violates the geographic limits, requires privileged matter, or imposes undue burden.
- **Discovery motions and sanctions:** motion to compel (Rule 37(a)) requires a **certification of good-faith conferral**; if granted (or material produced after the motion), the court **must** award reasonable expenses including fees unless the movant did not confer, the opposing position was substantially justified, or an award would be unjust; an evasive or incomplete answer is treated as a failure to answer. Violating a discovery **order** exposes the party to the Rule 37(b)(2)(A) menu (facts taken as established; preclusion; strike pleadings; stay; dismiss; default; contempt). Failure to attend one's own deposition or to serve any response to interrogatories/RFPs lets the court sanction directly under 37(d) without a prior order, and an objection does not excuse that failure unless a protective-order motion is pending. NextGen only requires knowing that sanctions exist, not their menu.
- **Certification (26(g))**: signing a discovery request, response or objection certifies a reasonable inquiry; a violation without substantial justification requires an appropriate sanction.

### 10.2 Fact patterns: discovery

**DS-1 (admissibility is not the test).** In a contract case, the plaintiff requests, and the defendant resists producing, an email in which a nonparty employee told her supervisor the defendant "knew the part was defective." The defendant says it is inadmissible hearsay. The court should:
(A) Deny the request; discoverable information must be admissible. (B) **Order production if the email is relevant and proportional, because discoverable information need not be admissible.** (C) Deny the request unless the plaintiff first proves the email is authentic. (D) Order production only if the email is a business record.
*Best: B* (26(b)(1)).

**DS-2 (work product).** After a fire, the defendant's insurer sends an investigator who photographs the scene the next day and takes written statements from two witnesses. A year later the building has been rebuilt. The plaintiff seeks the photographs and the statements. The court should:
(A) Deny; all documents prepared after an incident are privileged. (B) **Order production if the plaintiff shows substantial need and inability to obtain the substantial equivalent without undue hardship, because these are work product but not opinion work product.** (C) Order production without a showing because they are factual. (D) Deny because work product is absolutely protected.
*Best: B.* (The rebuilt building supports "undue hardship" for the photographs.) (A person may obtain his own earlier statement without that showing.)

**DS-3 (timing).** The day after serving the summons and complaint, the plaintiff serves interrogatories on the defendant; no stipulation or order exists. The defendant does not answer. The plaintiff moves to compel. The court should:
(A) Grant; interrogatories can be served at any time. (B) **Deny; absent exceptions, a party may not seek discovery before the Rule 26(f) conference.** (C) Grant; the defendant waived objections by not responding. (D) Grant but only after 30 days.
*Best: B* (26(d)(1)). (Early Rule 34 requests are the exception: delivered after 21 days.)

**DS-4 (limits).** A plaintiff has already deposed 10 witnesses. She wants to take an 11th deposition of a former employee. The defendant objects; no stipulation or order. She notices it anyway. The deposition:
(A) Is allowed; there is no numerical limit. (B) Is allowed if the witness is a nonparty. (C) **Requires leave of court (or stipulation) because it would exceed 10 depositions per side.** (D) Is barred absolutely.
*Best: C* (30(a)(2)(A)(i)).

**DS-5 (admissions by silence).** The plaintiff serves requests for admission on the defendant, including that the signature on a contract is the defendant's. The defendant does not respond for 45 days. At trial the defendant denies the signature. Result?
(A) The jury decides; RFAs are only evidence. (B) **The matter is deemed admitted and conclusively established, unless the court permits withdrawal or amendment on motion.** (C) The matter is admitted only if the court ordered a response. (D) The matter is admitted only for summary judgment.
*Best: B* (Rule 36(a)(3), (b)).

**DS-6 (nonparty documents).** A plaintiff in a personal-injury case wants hospital records held by a nonparty hospital. Which tool applies?
(A) Rule 34 request served on a party. (B) **Rule 45 subpoena commanding the hospital to produce.** (C) Rule 33 interrogatories to the hospital. (D) Rule 36 request for admission.
*Best: B.* (Rules 33, 34, 36 apply to parties only; depositions of non-parties by subpoena.)

**DS-7 (Rule 35).** In a negligence case, the plaintiff claims a permanent back injury. The defendant wants its own physician to examine her. The plaintiff refuses. The defendant should:
(A) Serve a Rule 34 request for entry. (B) Notice a deposition. (C) **Move for a court order under Rule 35 showing good cause that her physical condition is in controversy.** (D) Do nothing; physicians can examine anyone.
*Best: C.*

**DS-8 (spoliation of ESI).** A company's routine auto-delete wipes employee text messages after the company reasonably anticipates litigation; the failure to suspend it was careless but not intentional; the messages cannot be recovered and are somewhat helpful to the plaintiff. The plaintiff seeks an instruction telling the jury to presume the messages were unfavorable. The court:
(A) May give it because the company was negligent. (B) May give it only if the plaintiff shows bad faith. (C) **May not give an adverse-inference instruction without a finding of intent to deprive; it may impose curative measures no greater than necessary to cure the prejudice.** (D) Must dismiss the defense.
*Best: C* (37(e)(1)-(2)). B is close but "bad faith" is not the rule's language; the rule says "intent to deprive."

**DS-9 (clawback).** In responding to document requests, a defendant's paralegal accidentally produces an email with its lawyer's advice. Two days later the defendant notifies the plaintiff and asserts privilege. What must the plaintiff's lawyer do?
(A) Keep using it; production waives privilege. (B) **Promptly return, sequester or destroy the email and any copies, and not use or disclose it until the court resolves the claim.** (C) Use it but only for impeachment. (D) File it publicly to test the claim.
*Best: B* (26(b)(5)(B)).

**DS-10 (undisclosed witness).** A defendant fails to disclose, in its initial disclosures or by supplementation, a witness it now wants to call at trial. There is no justification and the plaintiff would be surprised. The plaintiff objects. The court:
(A) Must allow the witness; witnesses can always be called. (B) **May exclude the witness's testimony, because failure to disclose bars use unless substantially justified or harmless.** (C) Must declare a mistrial. (D) Must allow the witness if the plaintiff can depose him tonight.
*Best: B* (37(c)(1)).

**DS-11 (30(b)(6)).** A plaintiff wants testimony of the corporate defendant about its record-keeping. She names the corporation as deponent and lists topics. Who testifies, and what must they know?
(A) The CEO, from personal knowledge only. (B) Any lawyer the corporation selects. (C) **One or more persons the corporation designates, who must testify to information known or reasonably available to the organization.** (D) Whoever the plaintiff selects.
*Best: C.*

### 10.3 Black-letter rules: injunctions (III.A)

- **TRO and preliminary injunction (Rule 65)** [V]: a **preliminary injunction** requires **notice** to the adverse party; the court may consolidate the PI hearing with a trial on the merits (65(a)(2)) (so a PI "can become a permanent injunction" per NextGen); a **TRO may issue without notice** only if (A) specific facts in an affidavit or verified complaint clearly show **immediate and irreparable injury** before the adverse party can be heard, and (B) the movant's attorney **certifies in writing** efforts to give notice and why it should not be required. A no-notice TRO expires on its terms **not to exceed 14 days**, extendable once for good cause for a like period (or longer on consent); the PI motion is then heard at the earliest possible time; the adverse party may move to dissolve on 2 days' notice. **Security:** the court may issue a PI or TRO "only if the movant gives security" in an amount the court considers proper (the US is exempt) (65(c)). **Contents:** state reasons, terms specifically, describe acts restrained in reasonable detail without referring to the complaint (65(d)(1)); binds the parties, their officers/agents/servants/employees/attorneys, and others **in active concert or participation** who receive **actual notice** (65(d)(2)).
- **PI standard** [K, Winter v. NRDC (2008)]: the movant must show (1) a **likelihood of success on the merits**, (2) likelihood of **irreparable harm** absent preliminary relief, (3) the **balance of equities** tips in its favor, (4) an injunction is in the **public interest**. (Some circuits use a sliding scale; the exam will follow the four-factor description.) Purpose: preserve the status quo pending trial.
- **Appeal:** an order granting, continuing, modifying, refusing or dissolving a **PI** is immediately appealable (28 USC 1292(a)(1)) [V]; a **TRO** generally is not (unless it operates like a PI, e.g., extended well beyond 14 days) [K, *Sampson v. Murray*, *Granny Goose*].

### 10.4 Fact patterns: injunctions

**IN-1 (TRO duration).** A court issues a TRO without notice on March 1 at 9:00 a.m. No hearing on a PI has been set and no one has consented to extension. On March 17, the TRO:
(A) Remains effective until a PI hearing. (B) Remains effective for 28 days. (C) **Has expired, because a TRO without notice lasts at most 14 days unless extended for good cause or with consent.** (D) Has become a permanent injunction.
*Best: C.*

**IN-2 (ex parte).** A landlord's lawyer asks for a TRO without notice and supplies an affidavit showing a hazard that may injure tenants within hours. No certification of efforts to give notice is filed. The court:
(A) May issue it because immediate harm is shown. (B) **May not issue it without notice because the attorney's written certification of efforts to give notice (or why notice should not be required) is missing.** (C) May issue it only if the movant posts no security. (D) Must hold a jury trial first.
*Best: B* (65(b)(1)(B)). A is the trap (right on harm, misses the second requirement).

**IN-3 (PI factors).** A plaintiff shows a strong likelihood of success on a contract claim but the only harm is monetary loss, fully compensable by damages after trial. Result on a PI motion?
(A) Granted; likelihood of success suffices. (B) Granted; money damages can never be adequate. (C) **Denied; the movant has not shown irreparable harm.** (D) Denied because PIs are available only in equity cases with jury demand.
*Best: C.* [Winter; K]

**IN-4 (who is bound).** A court enjoins a company from dumping waste. A contractor that is not a party but knows of the order and works with the company's officers dumps the waste for them. Is the contractor bound?
(A) No; only named parties are bound. (B) No; contractors are never bound. (C) **Yes; persons in active concert or participation with the party who receive actual notice are bound.** (D) Yes, but only if it was served.
*Best: C* (65(d)(2)).

### 10.5 Black-letter rules: ending without trial and pretrial conference (III.F, III.G) [MBE only]

- **Voluntary dismissal (Rule 41(a))** [V]: the plaintiff may dismiss **without a court order** by filing a notice **before the opposing party serves either an answer or a motion for summary judgment** (a Rule 12(b) motion does **not** cut off the right), or by stipulation signed by all parties who have appeared; it is **without prejudice** unless the notice says otherwise; but **the two-dismissal rule**: a notice of dismissal operates as an adjudication on the merits if the plaintiff previously dismissed **any** federal- or state-court action based on or including the same claim. Otherwise only by court order on terms the court considers proper (a counterclaim pleaded earlier may block it unless it can be adjudicated independently); such a dismissal is without prejudice unless the order says otherwise. Class actions: 23(e) court approval. Costs of a previously dismissed action may be required before refiling (41(d)).
- **Involuntary dismissal (41(b))** [V]: for failure to prosecute or comply with rules/orders; unless the order says otherwise, it **operates as an adjudication on the merits** except for dismissals for lack of jurisdiction, improper venue, or failure to join a party under Rule 19.
- **Offer of judgment (Rule 68)** [V]: only a **defending party** may serve an offer on specified terms **at least 14 days before trial**; the opposing party has **14 days** to accept in writing, and then the clerk enters judgment; an unaccepted offer is deemed withdrawn and inadmissible except to determine costs; if the final judgment obtained is **not more favorable** than the unaccepted offer, the offeree **must pay the costs incurred after the offer** (costs, not ordinarily attorney fees unless the underlying statute defines costs to include fees, *Marek v. Chesny*, 1985 [K]; Rule 68 does not apply if the defendant wins, *Delta Air Lines v. August*, 1981 [K]).
- **Other:** summary judgment and Rule 12(c) (cp10); default (cp13); settlement and ADR (16(c)(2)(I)) [V]; court-ordered stays, arbitration (FAA) not tested in depth.
- **Pretrial conferences and orders (Rule 16)** [V]: the court may require conferences to expedite disposition, discourage wasteful pretrial activities, improve trial preparation, and facilitate settlement; a **scheduling order** is required in most cases and issued by the **earlier of 90 days after any defendant has been served or 60 days after any defendant has appeared**, limiting time to join parties, amend pleadings, complete discovery, and file motions; it may be modified **only for good cause and with the judge's consent**; the court may discuss many subjects (narrowing issues, stipulations, discovery plans, ESI, summary judgment timing, settlement, separate trials, etc.); attorneys must be authorized to make stipulations and admissions; the **final pretrial conference** is attended by the trial attorney and the **final pretrial order may be modified only to prevent manifest injustice**; sanctions under 16(f) for failure to appear, being substantially unprepared, or violating an order (including Rule 37(b)(2)(A)(ii)-(vii) sanctions and mandatory fee shifting unless substantially justified).

### 10.6 Fact patterns: ending without trial and Rule 16

**ET-1 (voluntary dismissal).** A plaintiff sues in federal court. The defendant files a Rule 12(b)(6) motion but no answer. The next day the plaintiff files a notice of voluntary dismissal. The defendant objects that a motion to dismiss was already pending. Result?
(A) Ineffective; court order is needed once any motion is filed. (B) Ineffective; the defendant must consent. (C) **Effective without prejudice (barring a prior dismissal), because the defendant has served neither an answer nor a summary-judgment motion.** (D) Effective with prejudice.
*Best: C.*

**ET-2 (two-dismissal rule).** A plaintiff voluntarily dismisses, by notice, a state-court breach-of-contract suit against a defendant. She refiles the same claim in federal court, then voluntarily dismisses again by notice before an answer. She files a third suit on the same claim. The defendant moves to dismiss. Result?
(A) The third suit is allowed; dismissals are without prejudice. (B) **The third suit is barred; the second notice of dismissal operates as an adjudication on the merits because the plaintiff previously dismissed a state-court action on the same claim.** (C) Allowed only if filed in state court. (D) Barred only if the court dismissed after an answer.
*Best: B.* 

**ET-3 (Rule 68).** The defendant offers to settle for $20,000 plus costs 20 days before trial. The plaintiff rejects it and wins $15,000. Consequence?
(A) Plaintiff recovers all costs. (B) **Plaintiff cannot recover post-offer costs and must pay the defendant's post-offer costs, because the judgment was not more favorable than the offer.** (C) Plaintiff must pay the defendant's attorney fees in every case. (D) Offer is irrelevant because it was rejected.
*Best: B* (fees only if the underlying statute defines costs to include fees).

**ET-4 (Rule 41(b) effect).** A court dismisses a plaintiff's case for failure to prosecute without specifying prejudice. Later the plaintiff refiles the same claim. Result?
(A) Allowed; dismissal for non-prosecution is never on the merits. (B) **Barred; unless the dismissal order states otherwise, it operates as an adjudication on the merits (the exceptions are for lack of jurisdiction, improper venue, and failure to join a Rule 19 party).** (C) Allowed if refiled within 30 days. (D) Barred only if there was a trial.
*Best: B.*

**PC-1 (amending after the scheduling-order deadline).** A scheduling order set a deadline for amending pleadings; it passes. Three months later, the plaintiff moves to add a new claim, saying only that justice requires it. The court should apply:
(A) Rule 15(a)(2)'s liberal standard alone. (B) **Rule 16(b)(4)'s requirement of good cause first, and only then Rule 15(a)(2).** (C) Rule 11. (D) No standard; the deadline is absolute.
*Best: B* (majority view). Tag status "general".

**PC-2 (final pretrial order).** The final pretrial order lists only two claims. At trial the plaintiff wants to add a third, unlisted claim. The court:
(A) Must permit it if the claim is in the complaint. (B) **May permit it only to prevent manifest injustice, because the final pretrial order controls the course of the trial.** (C) May always permit it. (D) May permit it only if the jury consents.
*Best: B* (16(e)).

## 11. V.A Rule 12, judgment on the pleadings, summary judgment (cp10)

### 11.1 Black-letter rules

- **Rule 12(b) defenses by motion** [V]: (1) lack of **subject-matter jurisdiction**; (2) lack of **personal jurisdiction**; (3) **improper venue**; (4) **insufficient process**; (5) **insufficient service of process**; (6) **failure to state a claim** upon which relief can be granted; (7) **failure to join a party** under Rule 19. Mnemonic: "1-2-3 jurisdiction/venue, 4-5 process/service, 6 claim, 7 party". A motion asserting these **must be made before pleading** if a responsive pleading is allowed. Defenses may be combined in the same pleading or motion without waiver. Raising defenses by motion **tolls the time to answer**: the answer is due **14 days** after notice of the court's action if the motion is denied or postponed (12(a)(4)).
- **Consolidation and waiver (12(g)(2), 12(h))** [V]: a party that makes a Rule 12 motion **cannot make another** Rule 12 motion raising a defense **that was available but omitted**, except under 12(h)(2)-(3). **12(b)(2)-(5)** defenses (PJ, venue, process, service) are **waived** if omitted from the first Rule 12 motion or, when no motion is made, if not included in the **responsive pleading** (or an amendment as of course). **12(b)(6), 12(b)(7) and failure to state a legal defense** are **not** waived by omission: they may be raised in any pleading permitted by Rule 7(a), by a 12(c) motion, or at trial. **12(b)(1)** SMJ cannot be waived: the court must dismiss "at any time" it finds SMJ lacking (12(h)(3)).
- **12(b)(6) standard** [K]: the court accepts well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draws reasonable inferences for the plaintiff, but not legal conclusions (*Iqbal*); dismisses if the complaint does not state a plausible claim (*Twombly*); may consider the complaint, documents attached or incorporated by reference, and matters subject to judicial notice. It tests the **legal sufficiency**, not the evidence. Dismissal on this ground is "on the merits" for preclusion (41(b)). Often granted with leave to amend.
- **Conversion (12(d))** [V]: if **matters outside the pleadings** are presented to and **not excluded** by the court on a 12(b)(6) or 12(c) motion, it **must be treated as a Rule 56 motion**, and all parties must be given a **reasonable opportunity to present all the material that is pertinent** to the motion. (NextGen explicitly lists this.)
- **Judgment on the pleadings (12(c))** [V]: after the pleadings are closed, but early enough not to delay trial; same standard as 12(b)(6); can be used to raise failure to state a claim, Rule 19 failure to join, and failure to state a legal defense (12(h)(2)); also for the defendant's affirmative defenses that appear on the face of the pleadings.
- **12(e) more definite statement** (pleading so vague or ambiguous that a party cannot reasonably prepare a response; before responding) and **12(f) motion to strike** (insufficient defense or redundant, immaterial, impertinent, scandalous matter; on motion made before responding or **21 days** after service if no response is allowed, or on the court's own initiative) [V].
- **Rule 56 summary judgment** [V text; K cases]:
  - Standard: movant shows **no genuine dispute as to any material fact** and is **entitled to judgment as a matter of law**; the court states its reasons. A "genuine" dispute exists if a **reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmovant**; "material" means it might affect the outcome under the governing law (*Anderson v. Liberty Lobby*, 1986 [K]).
  - **Burdens:** movant bears the initial burden of showing the basis; a movant who **will not bear the burden of proof at trial** may discharge it by pointing to the **absence of evidence** supporting the nonmovant's case, without negating the claim with its own affidavits (*Celotex v. Catrett*, 1986 [K]); the nonmovant must then cite **specific facts** in the record creating a genuine dispute and cannot rest on pleadings; a movant who bears the burden at trial must show the evidence is such that no reasonable jury could find for the nonmovant. A nonmovant who offers only **metaphysical doubt or implausible theories** loses (*Matsushita*, 1986 [K]).
  - **Evidence** viewed in the **light most favorable to the nonmovant** with reasonable inferences drawn for them; the court does not weigh evidence or decide credibility (*Tolan v. Cotton*, 2014 [K]); but a version "blatantly contradicted" by an unchallenged video need not be credited (*Scott v. Harris*, 2007 [K]). Facts must be supported by citation to the record; affidavits/declarations on **personal knowledge**, setting out facts admissible in evidence, from a competent declarant (56(c)(4)).
  - **Timing:** a party may move **at any time until 30 days after the close of all discovery** unless the court sets another time. If the nonmovant shows by affidavit or declaration that **it cannot present facts essential to its opposition**, the court may defer or deny, allow discovery, or issue another order (56(d)). The court may grant summary judgment **for the nonmovant**, on grounds not raised, or on its own initiative after notice and reasonable time to respond (56(f)), and may enter an order stating facts not genuinely in dispute (56(g)) when it doesn't grant all relief. Bad-faith affidavits: fees (56(h)).
  - **Appeal:** denial of summary judgment is **not appealable** until final judgment and, after a full trial on the merits, is generally **not reviewable** on sufficiency of the evidence (the party must preserve it through Rule 50 motions): *Ortiz v. Jordan* (2011) [K]; but a purely legal issue rejected at summary judgment is preserved for appeal without a post-trial Rule 50(b) motion: *Dupree v. Younger* (2023, unanimous) [V].

### 11.2 Fact patterns

**MO-1 (conversion).** A defendant moves to dismiss under 12(b)(6) and attaches an affidavit from its manager contradicting the complaint's key allegation. The court reads the affidavit and relies on it. The court must:
(A) Disregard it automatically. (B) Rely on it as part of the pleadings. (C) **Treat the motion as one for summary judgment and give all parties a reasonable opportunity to present pertinent material.** (D) Treat it as a Rule 12(c) motion only.
*Best: C* (12(d)). The trap is B: matters outside the pleadings cannot be treated as pleadings.

**MO-2 (waiver).** A defendant served in a federal action files a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, which is denied, then files an answer that includes defenses of lack of venue and failure to state a claim. The plaintiff moves to strike the venue defense. The court should:
(A) Deny; venue may be raised at any time. (B) **Grant as to venue (waived by omission from the first Rule 12 motion), but keep the failure-to-state-a-claim defense, which is preserved.** (C) Grant as to both. (D) Deny as to both.
*Best: B.*

**MO-3 (12(c)).** After the defendant answers, the defendant moves for judgment on the pleadings, arguing that the complaint fails to state a claim. The court:
(A) Must deny; failure to state a claim was waived by answering. (B) **May grant it, under the same standard as 12(b)(6); the defense is preserved and may be raised by a 12(c) motion.** (C) Must treat the motion as one for summary judgment. (D) May consider only the answer.
*Best: B.*

**MO-4 (Celotex).** A plaintiff alleges the defendant's chemical caused her illness. After discovery, the defendant moves for summary judgment, pointing out that the plaintiff has identified no evidence of exposure; the defendant submits no affidavits. The plaintiff responds only with the allegations of her complaint. The court should:
(A) Deny; the defendant must disprove exposure with its own evidence. (B) Deny; credibility is for the jury. (C) **Grant; the movant may satisfy its burden by showing an absence of evidence, and the nonmovant must then come forward with specific facts.** (D) Deny; summary judgment is unavailable before trial in tort cases.
*Best: C.*

**MO-5 (credibility).** In a contract dispute the plaintiff's affidavit says the parties agreed to a $50,000 price; the defendant's affidavit says $40,000. Each affiant has personal knowledge. The defendant moves for summary judgment, arguing the plaintiff is not credible. The court should:
(A) Grant, because the defendant's account is more plausible. (B) **Deny; conflicting sworn accounts present a genuine dispute that turns on credibility, which the court cannot resolve at summary judgment.** (C) Grant, because the plaintiff bears the burden. (D) Deny, but only if a jury demand was filed.
*Best: B.*

**MO-6 (56(d)).** The defendant moves for summary judgment on day 30 of a case before any discovery. The plaintiff files an affidavit explaining that the facts on the defendant's state of mind are in the defendant's files and that discovery is needed. The court may:
(A) Only grant the motion. (B) Deny the motion as untimely. (C) **Defer or deny the motion and allow time for discovery.** (D) Convert the motion to a Rule 12(c) motion.
*Best: C* (56(d)). B: a party may move for summary judgment at any time.

**MO-7 (denial not appealable; preserved legal issue).** The court denies summary judgment on a purely legal question (whether a statute applies). The case goes to trial; the defendant loses and files no Rule 50(b) motion. On appeal from the final judgment, the defendant argues the court misinterpreted the statute. The court of appeals:
(A) Cannot review because no Rule 50(b) motion was made. (B) **May review, because a purely legal issue decided at summary judgment is preserved for appeal without a post-trial motion.** (C) May review only if the issue is plain error. (D) May review any factual sufficiency argument too.
*Best: B* (*Dupree v. Younger*). D is wrong: a sufficiency challenge requires Rule 50 motions.

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Venue, process, service, personal jurisdiction: use them or lose them in your first Rule 12 move." / "Bring in outside evidence on a 12(b)(6)? It's now summary judgment, and everyone gets a fair chance." / "Summary judgment asks one question: could a reasonable jury find for the other side?"

## 12. IV Jury trials (cp11)

### 12.1 Black-letter rules

- **Right (Seventh Amendment; Rule 38(a))** [K]: "In Suits at common law, where the value in controversy shall exceed twenty dollars, the right of trial by jury shall be preserved." Applies in **federal** court only (not incorporated against the States). Jury available for **legal** claims (damages) and **legal issues**; no jury for **equitable** claims (injunction, specific performance, equitable restitution). Test: historical (18th-century English law/equity) and, more important, the nature of the **remedy** (*Tull v. United States*, 1987; *Curtis v. Loether*, 1974; Markman: a jury is not required for patent claim construction). **Beacon Theatres v. Westover** (1959) and **Dairy Queen v. Wood** (1962): where legal and equitable claims share common fact issues, the **jury decides the common legal issues first**, to protect the right; equitable claims go to the judge afterward. Statutory claims: depends on remedy. A declaratory judgment action takes the character of the coercive action it replaces. Derivative suits: *Ross v. Bernhard* (1970) [K].
- **Demand (Rule 38)** [V]: serve a written demand (may be in a pleading) **no later than 14 days after the last pleading directed to the issue is served** and file it; a demand can name specific issues (otherwise all triable issues); if only some issues demanded, any other party may demand others within **14 days** of service; **waived** unless properly served and filed; a proper demand can be withdrawn only with the parties' consent.
- **Court discretion (Rule 39(b))** [K]: even if no demand was made, the court may, **on motion**, order a jury trial on any issue for which a jury might have been demanded (inadvertence alone often insufficient). 39(a): when a demand is made, trial is by jury unless the parties file consent to a bench trial or the court finds no federal right to a jury. 39(c): advisory jury on motion or on the court's own initiative in non-jury cases.
- **Removed cases (Rule 81(c)(3))** [V]: a party who expressly demanded a jury under state law before removal need not renew; if state law did not require an express demand and all pleadings were served at the time of removal, a party entitled to a jury under Rule 38 must serve a demand within **14 days** after the notice of removal is filed or served.
- **Composition (Rule 48)** [V]: starts with **at least 6 and not more than 12**; each juror participates in the verdict unless excused; the verdict must be **unanimous** and returned by at least 6 jurors **unless the parties stipulate otherwise**; polling on request. (*Colgrove v. Battin*, 1973: a six-person civil jury is constitutional [K].)
- **Selection:** voir dire by the judge or with lawyers' participation (47(a) [K]); challenges **for cause** unlimited; **peremptory challenges: three per party** in civil cases, multiple parties may be treated as one (28 USC 1870 [V]); **no discrimination on race** (Batson applies to civil cases: *Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete*, 1991) or **sex** (*J.E.B. v. Alabama*, 1994) [K]; a fair cross-section of the community (28 USC 1861) [K]; alternates (47(c)) [K].
- **Instructions (Rule 51)** [V]: a party may file **written requests** before or at the close of evidence (or at an earlier time the court sets); the court must inform the parties of its proposed instructions and its action on requests **before instructing the jury and before final jury arguments**, and give the parties an opportunity to object **on the record and outside the jury's hearing**; an objection must state **distinctly the matter objected to and the grounds**; it is timely if made at the opportunity given, or promptly after learning of the instruction. **Preserving error:** to challenge a **given** instruction the party must have properly objected; to challenge a **refused** instruction it must have properly requested it and also objected **unless the court made a definitive ruling on the record rejecting the request**; an unpreserved error may be reviewed only as **plain error** affecting substantial rights (51(d)(2)).
- **Verdict forms (Rule 49)** [V]: **special verdict** (written findings on each fact issue; a party waives a jury on an issue not submitted unless it demands submission before the jury retires); **general verdict with interrogatories**: if the answers are consistent with the verdict, judgment; if answers are consistent with each other but one or more is inconsistent with the general verdict, the court may enter judgment per the answers, send the jury back, or order a new trial; if answers are inconsistent with each other and the verdict, no judgment: the jury is sent back or a new trial is ordered.
- **Reexamination Clause** (Seventh Amendment): a jury's findings can be reviewed only as the common law allowed (via JMOL, new trial, remittitur) [K]. **Juror testimony** to impeach a verdict is largely barred (FRE 606(b)) except extraneous prejudicial information, outside influence, or a clerical mistake on the verdict form [K].

### 12.2 Fact patterns

**JT-1 (demand timing).** A plaintiff files a complaint seeking damages on January 2, with no jury demand. The defendant serves an answer on February 1 (no counterclaim). On February 20 the plaintiff files a written jury demand. The defendant moves to strike. How should the court rule?
(A) Deny; a plaintiff may demand a jury any time before trial. (B) **Strike it as untimely (due 14 days after the answer, February 15), unless the court exercises discretion under Rule 39(b); mere inadvertence is usually not enough.** (C) Strike it; the plaintiff waived jury trial by filing a complaint. (D) Deny; the right is constitutional and cannot be waived.
*Best: B.*

**JT-2 (legal and equitable issues).** A plaintiff seeks damages for breach of a license and also an injunction to stop continued use. Both claims turn on whether the license was validly terminated. The plaintiff demanded a jury. The judge proposes to decide the termination issue herself in deciding the injunction, then let the jury address damages. Is that proper?
(A) Yes; equitable claims are tried first. (B) **No; the jury must decide the common legal question (validity of termination) first, because deciding it first by the judge would deprive the plaintiff of the jury right.** (C) Yes; the judge decides all issues when equitable relief is sought. (D) No, because equitable claims cannot be joined with legal claims.
*Best: B* (*Beacon Theatres*).

**JT-3 (Batson in civil).** In a civil damages case, the defendant uses all three of its peremptory challenges to remove the only three jurors of a particular race, offering no reason. The plaintiff objects. How should the judge proceed?
(A) Overrule; peremptory challenges need no reason in civil cases. (B) Overrule; Batson applies only in criminal cases. (C) **Require the defendant to give race-neutral reasons; if the strikes were race-based, they are impermissible.** (D) Sustain automatically.
*Best: C* (*Edmonson*). [K; verify]

**JT-4 (unanimity).** In a federal civil trial with a jury of 8 and no stipulation about the verdict, seven jurors vote for the plaintiff and one dissents. The court should:
(A) Enter judgment; a majority suffices. (B) Enter judgment; six votes suffice. (C) **Not accept the verdict; absent a stipulation the verdict must be unanimous, so the court may send the jury back or order a new trial.** (D) Enter judgment for the defendant.
*Best: C* (Rule 48(b)).

**JT-5 (instruction objection).** The judge tells counsel the proposed instructions and rules on requests before closing arguments, then instructs the jury. The defendant's lawyer says nothing until after the verdict, then argues the instruction on causation was erroneous. The defendant:
(A) Preserved the objection by submitting a proposed instruction. (B) **Failed to preserve the objection and may obtain review only for plain error.** (C) Preserved the objection by filing a post-verdict motion. (D) May object at any time.
*Best: B* (Rule 51(c), (d)). Variation: if the judge had definitively refused the defendant's written request on the record, no further objection would be needed to challenge the refusal.

**JT-6 (inconsistent answers).** A jury returns a general verdict for the plaintiff and, as the court asked under Rule 49(b), answers written questions: its answers are consistent with each other but one of them means the defendant was not negligent. The court may:
(A) Enter judgment on the verdict only. (B) **Enter judgment according to the answers, return the jury for further deliberation, or order a new trial.** (C) Declare a mistrial only. (D) Enter judgment for the plaintiff on the general verdict and ignore the answers.
*Best: B* (49(b)(3)).

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Demand the jury within 14 days of the last pleading on the issue, or lose it (the judge may rescue you)." / "Legal issue shared with an equitable claim? Jury first."

## 13. V.B-V.C Judgment as a matter of law, new trial, relief from judgment (cp12)

### 13.1 Black-letter rules

- **JMOL, Rule 50(a)** [V]: after a party has been **fully heard** on an issue during a jury trial, the court may grant JMOL if a **reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis** to find for that party; the motion may be made **any time before the case is submitted to the jury**, and must specify the judgment sought and the law and facts that entitle the movant. Standard: view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmovant, draw all reasonable inferences for them, **do not weigh the evidence or assess credibility**, disregard evidence favorable to the movant that the jury isn't required to believe (*Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing*, 2000) [K]. "Directed verdict" and "JNOV" are the old names.
- **Renewed JMOL, Rule 50(b)** [V]: if the court does not grant the motion, it is deemed reserved, and the movant may file a **renewed** motion **no later than 28 days after entry of judgment** (or after the jury was discharged if it didn't reach a verdict), and may include an **alternative new-trial motion**. It is available **only if the movant made a Rule 50(a) motion before submission to the jury**, and the renewed motion is limited to the grounds raised in the 50(a) motion [K]. If the party failed to move before the verdict, the court of appeals **cannot review** the sufficiency of the evidence, and a party cannot get JMOL by a post-verdict motion only (*Unitherm Food Systems v. Swift-Eckrich*, 2006 [K]). A district court **cannot extend** the 28-day period (6(b)(2)) [V].
- **Conditional ruling on a new trial (50(c))** [V]: when granting a renewed JMOL the court must also say whether it would grant a new trial if the judgment is later vacated or reversed. The prevailing party may urge a new trial on appeal as an alternative (50(e)).
- **New trial, Rule 59** [V]: for any party, on all or some issues; after a **jury** trial, for any reason for which a new trial has historically been granted in federal court (e.g., verdict **against the clear weight of the evidence**, excessive or inadequate damages, prejudicial error in evidence or instructions, juror misconduct, attorney misconduct, newly discovered evidence); after a **bench** trial, grounds for rehearing in equity, and the court may take more testimony, amend findings, and enter a new judgment. Motion **no later than 28 days after entry of judgment**; supporting affidavits filed with the motion, opposition affidavits within 14 days; the court may order a new trial on its own initiative within 28 days. **Unlike JMOL, the judge on a new-trial motion may weigh the evidence and assess credibility** [K]. **Remittitur:** the court may condition denial of a new trial on the plaintiff's acceptance of a reduced award; the plaintiff must be offered a **new trial** as the alternative (7th Amendment) (*Hetzel v. Prince William County*, 1998) [K]; **additur is unconstitutional in federal court** (*Dimick v. Schiedt*, 1935) [K]. The plaintiff who accepts a remittitur cannot appeal it (*Donovan v. Penn Shipping*, 1927) [K].
- **Motion to alter or amend a judgment (59(e))** [V]: within **28 days** after entry of judgment; typically to correct manifest errors of law or fact, present newly discovered evidence, or reflect an intervening change in law. **Amended findings (52(b))** [V]: within 28 days. A timely 50(b), 52(b), 59 (or 60 motion filed within 28 days) **restarts the time to appeal** (FRAP 4(a)(4)) [V].
- **Relief from judgment or order, Rule 60(b)** [V]: on motion and just terms, relief from a **final** judgment, order or proceeding for: (1) **mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect**; (2) **newly discovered evidence** that, with reasonable diligence, **could not have been discovered in time to move for a new trial under Rule 59(b)**; (3) **fraud** (intrinsic or extrinsic), misrepresentation, or misconduct by an opposing party; (4) the judgment is **void**; (5) the judgment has been satisfied, released, or discharged, is based on an earlier judgment that has been reversed or vacated, or applying it prospectively is no longer equitable; (6) **any other reason that justifies relief** (extraordinary circumstances, not available if grounds fit (1)-(5)) [K]. **Time:** within a **reasonable time**, and for (1), (2), (3) **no more than 1 year** after entry of the judgment; the motion doesn't affect finality or suspend the judgment. **Coney Island Auto Parts v. Burton** (Jan. 20, 2026): the "reasonable time" requirement applies to (b)(4) (void) motions too, including a default judgment attacked for lack of personal jurisdiction (the Court did not accept a rule that void judgments may be attacked "at any time") [V via CRS]. Rule 60(a) corrects clerical mistakes. Rule 60(d): an independent action, or setting aside a judgment for **fraud on the court**, is not time-limited [V].
- **Rule 52(c)** [V]: in a **bench trial**, after a party has been fully heard, the court may enter judgment on partial findings (the nonjury analogue of JMOL), with findings and conclusions.
- **Rule 62** [V]: enforcement of a judgment is automatically stayed for **30 days**; after that a stay requires a bond or other security (62(b)).
- **Costs and fees (Rule 54(d))** [V]: costs go to the prevailing party; fee motion within **14 days** after entry of judgment.

### 13.2 Fact patterns

**PT-1 (no pre-verdict motion).** At trial, the defendant does not move for JMOL at the close of evidence. The jury returns a verdict for the plaintiff. Ten days later the defendant moves under Rule 50(b) for JMOL, arguing the evidence was legally insufficient. The court should:
(A) Grant if the evidence was insufficient. (B) **Deny, because a renewed motion under 50(b) requires a Rule 50(a) motion before the case was submitted to the jury.** (C) Deny because the motion was premature. (D) Grant because 28 days had not yet run.
*Best: B.* D describes timing but misses the prerequisite. The defendant might still seek a new trial under Rule 59 (court weighing evidence).

**PT-2 (standard).** A defendant timely moves for JMOL; the plaintiff's only evidence of notice is a witness whom the defendant says is a liar. The judge personally finds the witness not credible. The court should:
(A) Grant the motion; the judge may weigh credibility. (B) **Deny the motion; on JMOL the court may not weigh the evidence or assess credibility and must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmovant.** (C) Grant because the defendant has the better case. (D) Deny because JMOL is available only to plaintiffs.
*Best: B.* (The credibility concern can be raised in a new-trial motion.)

**PT-3 (remittitur/additur).** A jury awards $1,000,000 for a minor injury; the judge finds the award excessive. Alternatively, the jury awards $1 and the judge finds it inadequate. Which is available in federal court?
(A) Both remittitur and additur. (B) **Remittitur, offering the plaintiff the choice of a reduced award or a new trial; additur is not allowed.** (C) Neither; the jury's award is final. (D) Additur only.
*Best: B.*

**PT-4 (28 days).** Judgment is entered on June 1. On June 30 (29 days later) the plaintiff moves under Rule 59(e) to alter the judgment. The court:
(A) May extend the time for good cause. (B) **Must deny the motion as untimely; the 28-day period cannot be extended by the court.** (C) Treats it as timely because mail adds 3 days. (D) May hear it because Rule 59(e) has no deadline.
*Best: B* (6(b)(2) forbids extending the Rule 59(e) period; Rule 6(d)'s three extra days apply only to periods that run from service, not from entry of judgment).

**PT-5 (Rule 60(b) timing).** A defendant moves under 60(b)(1) thirteen months after a judgment, claiming her lawyer's mistake caused her to miss the deadline. The court:
(A) Grants relief; mistake is a ground. (B) **Denies relief; a 60(b)(1) motion must be made within a reasonable time and no more than one year after entry of judgment.** (C) Grants relief under 60(b)(6). (D) Treats it as a Rule 59 motion.
*Best: B.* (C: Rule 60(b)(6) is unavailable when the reason falls within (1)-(5).)

**PT-6 (newly discovered evidence).** After judgment, a losing plaintiff finds a document that existed at trial and which, despite diligence, she could not have found in time to file a Rule 59 motion. It is more than 28 days after judgment, but 6 months. Which relief?
(A) Rule 59(a). (B) **Rule 60(b)(2), within a reasonable time and not more than one year.** (C) Rule 50(b). (D) None.
*Best: B.*

## 14. VI Verdicts and judgments: default, dismissals, findings (cp13)

### 14.1 Black-letter rules

- **Default (Rule 55)** [V]: two steps. (a) **Entry of default**: when a party against whom affirmative relief is sought has failed to plead or otherwise defend, shown by affidavit or otherwise, the **clerk must enter the default**. (b) **Default judgment**: by the **clerk** if the claim is for a **sum certain** (or one made certain by computation), on the plaintiff's request with an affidavit, and the defendant is **not a minor or incompetent** and has **not appeared**; otherwise, by the **court**, which may hold hearings (damages, accounting, jury right preserved by statute). If the defaulting party **has appeared personally or by a representative**, it must be served with written notice of the application **at least 7 days before the hearing**. A default judgment against a minor or incompetent requires an appearing guardian. Default judgments against the United States require evidence satisfying the court.
- **Limit on relief (54(c))** [V]: a default judgment **must not differ in kind from, or exceed in amount, what is demanded in the pleadings**. Other final judgments grant the relief to which each party is entitled even if not demanded.
- **Setting aside:** an **entry of default** may be set aside for **good cause** (more lenient: willfulness, prejudice to plaintiff, meritorious defense [K]); a **final default judgment** only under **Rule 60(b)** [V]. A default judgment entered without PJ over the defendant is void; the defendant who never appeared can attack it, but a 60(b)(4) motion must be made within a reasonable time (*Coney Island*, 2026 [V via CRS]).
- **What default does** [K]: it admits well-pleaded allegations of liability but **not** damages or conclusions of law. A default judgment is a judgment on the merits for **claim preclusion**, but issues are not "actually litigated" for **issue preclusion** (most courts).
- **Dismissals** [V]: voluntary (41(a)) and involuntary (41(b)) as in section 10.5; dismissals under Rule 12(b) for failure to state a claim operate as adjudications on the merits (41(b), final judgment). Dismissal for lack of jurisdiction, improper venue, or failure to join a Rule 19 party is **not** an adjudication on the merits (but may have issue-preclusive effect on the jurisdiction/venue issue).
- **Judgment on multiple claims or parties (54(b))** [V]: any order adjudicating fewer than all claims or the rights of fewer than all parties **does not end the action** and may be revised until the entry of a judgment adjudicating all claims, **unless** the court expressly determines "**there is no just reason for delay**" and directs entry of final judgment.
- **Findings and conclusions (Rule 52)** [V]: in a **bench trial** (or advisory jury), the court must find the facts specially and state its conclusions of law separately, on the record or in a written opinion; fact findings are reviewed for **clear error** with due regard for the trial court's credibility determinations (52(a)(6)); a party may move within **28 days** to amend or add findings (52(b)); the sufficiency of the evidence to support findings can be raised on appeal even if the party didn't object in the trial court (52(a)(5)).
- **Jury verdicts: types and challenges** [V Rule 49; K for challenges]: general verdict (one finding for a party), special verdict (answers on each fact issue), general verdict with interrogatories (Rule 49(b)); unanimity unless stipulated (Rule 48); polling (48(c)); challenges by JMOL, new trial, remittitur, juror impeachment limits (FRE 606(b)).
- **Entry of judgment (Rule 58)** [V]: every judgment must be set out in a **separate document**, with listed exceptions (e.g., orders disposing of 50(b), 52(b), 54 fees, 59, 60 motions); judgment is "entered" when entered in the civil docket (and, if a separate document is required, the earlier of the separate document or **150 days** after docket entry).
- **Harmless error** [K]: errors that do not affect substantial rights are disregarded (Rule 61; 28 USC 2111).

### 14.2 Fact patterns

**DF-1 (default judgment amount).** A plaintiff sues for $60,000 for breach of contract. The defendant never responds, and the clerk enters default. At the hearing, the plaintiff proves actual damages of $90,000 and asks for judgment in that amount. The court:
(A) May enter judgment for $90,000. (B) **May enter judgment of no more than $60,000, the amount demanded in the pleadings.** (C) Must dismiss for failure to prove damages. (D) Must set the matter for a jury trial.
*Best: B* (Rule 54(c)).

**DF-2 (clerk vs court).** A plaintiff sues for a sum certain of $12,000 on a promissory note. The adult defendant has not appeared in any manner. Who enters default judgment?
(A) Only the judge, after a hearing with 7 days' notice. (B) **The clerk, on the plaintiff's request supported by an affidavit of the amount due.** (C) Only a jury. (D) The clerk, but only if the defendant consents.
*Best: B* (55(b)(1)). Variation: if the defendant appeared (even informally), the 7-day notice rule applies to the court's judgment.

**DF-3 (setting aside).** The clerk enters a default against a defendant who missed the deadline by two days because of a clerical mistake at his insurer; he has a strong defense and the plaintiff has suffered no prejudice. No default judgment has been entered. The standard to set aside the entry of default is:
(A) Rule 60(b) relief standards only. (B) The one-year limit. (C) **"Good cause" under Rule 55(c), which is applied liberally.** (D) Entry of default can never be set aside.
*Best: C.*

**DF-4 (what's on the merits).** Which dismissal is an adjudication on the merits (for purposes of claim preclusion)? (A) Lack of personal jurisdiction. (B) Improper venue. (C) Failure to join a required party. (D) **Failure to state a claim.**
*Best: D.* (Rule 41(b) lists A-C as exceptions.) 

**DF-5 (54(b)).** A plaintiff sues two defendants. The court grants summary judgment dismissing all claims against Defendant 1 and enters no Rule 54(b) determination. The plaintiff tries to appeal immediately. The court of appeals:
(A) Must hear it; it is a final decision on Defendant 1. (B) **Must dismiss the appeal; without an express Rule 54(b) determination that there is no just reason for delay, the order is not final.** (C) May hear it because it is a collateral order. (D) Must hear it because the plaintiff objected.
*Best: B.*

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Default: clerk for a sum certain and no appearance; judge for everything else; never more than the complaint demanded." / "Dismissed for jurisdiction, venue or a missing Rule 19 party? Not the merits. Everything else? The merits."

## 15. VI.D Preclusion: claim and issue (cp14)

### 15.1 Black-letter rules (Restatement (Second) of Judgments; Supreme Court) [K unless noted]

**Claim preclusion (res judicata; "merger and bar")**. A **valid, final judgment on the merits** in the first action bars a second action between the **same parties or their privies** on the **same claim** (all claims that were or **could have been raised** in the first action arising from the same transaction/series (Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 24, the "transactional test")). 
- **Elements** (the three classic elements; NextGen asks for the "elements"): (1) a **final judgment on the merits** by a court of competent jurisdiction; (2) **identity of claims** (same transaction or occurrence, regardless of legal theory or remedy); (3) **identity of parties or privity**.
- **A judgment on the merits** includes: judgment after trial, summary judgment, **dismissal for failure to state a claim**, involuntary dismissal under 41(b) (except jurisdiction, venue, Rule 19), a second voluntary dismissal (41(a)(1)(B)), a **default judgment**, a consent judgment (for claim preclusion). NOT: a dismissal for lack of SMJ, PJ, improper venue, failure to join a party, ripeness/standing, or voluntary dismissal without prejudice (first). [V Rule 41(b)]
- **Effect on a plaintiff who lost or won:** if the plaintiff **wins**, the claim **merges** into the judgment and cannot be sued on again (and the judgment is enforceable); if the plaintiff **loses**, the claim is **barred**. Claim-splitting is not allowed: P who recovers for property damage from a collision cannot later sue for personal injury from the same collision (modern view).
- **Exceptions:** the first court lacked power to hear the claim (e.g., claims within exclusive federal jurisdiction not heard in state court) [K]; the parties agreed to split or the defendant acquiesced; the court expressly reserved the right to bring the other claim; the claim had **not accrued** at the time (e.g., ongoing or new harm arising after the first suit) (*Lawlor v. National Screen Service*, 1955); a different remedy was unavailable.
- **Counterclaims:** a defendant who fails to plead a compulsory counterclaim (Rule 13(a)) is usually barred from suing on it later (by Rule 13 principles/claim preclusion) [K].
- **Finality:** a federal-court judgment is final for preclusion purposes even if erroneous, and **while an appeal is pending** (majority federal rule; reversal later undoes preclusion); the failure to appeal an error does not undermine preclusion (*Federated Department Stores v. Moitie*, 1981).

**Issue preclusion (collateral estoppel)**. A party is precluded from relitigating an **issue of fact or law** that was **actually litigated and determined** in a prior action. 
- **Elements:** (1) the issue is **identical** to the issue in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was **actually litigated** (genuinely contested, not defaulted or stipulated unless the parties intended to be bound) and **actually decided**; (3) the determination was **essential (necessary) to the judgment**; (4) there is a **valid and final judgment** (on the merits in the prior case); (5) the party **against whom** preclusion is asserted was a party (or in privity) with a **full and fair opportunity to litigate**. [Restatement § 27]
- **No mutuality requirement**: a nonparty to the first action may use the first judgment against a party who lost the issue: **defensive** nonmutual issue preclusion is allowed (*Blonder-Tongue Laboratories v. University of Illinois Foundation*, 1971); **offensive** nonmutual issue preclusion is allowed at the court's **discretion** (*Parklane Hosiery v. Shore*, 1979), considering: whether the plaintiff **could easily have joined** the earlier action (wait-and-see plaintiffs are disfavored), whether the defendant had **incentive** to litigate vigorously in the first (small stakes, unforeseeable later suits), whether the judgment is **inconsistent** with other prior judgments, and whether the second action offers the defendant **procedural opportunities unavailable in the first** (e.g., a jury). Against the **US**, nonmutual offensive issue preclusion is not allowed (*United States v. Mendoza*, 1984).
- **Exceptions** (Restatement § 28): no preclusion if appellate review was unavailable; the issue is one of law and the two actions involve **unrelated claims** (or there has been a change in law); a significantly different **burden of persuasion** (e.g., a criminal acquittal under beyond a reasonable doubt does not preclude a civil plaintiff under preponderance; a prior civil finding does not bind in a criminal case); the party lacked an adequate opportunity or incentive; a **clear and convincing need** for a new determination. A **criminal conviction** may be used against the convicted defendant in a later civil action [K].
- **Alternative determinations**: if the first court rested its judgment on two independent grounds, both are preclusive unless the loser's appeal affirmed only one [K, Restatement § 27 cmt. i (modern rule)]; a finding not necessary to the judgment is not preclusive.
- **Nonparty preclusion - general rule: a person not a party is not bound** (*Taylor v. Sturgell*, 2008, unanimous): rejecting "virtual representation"; the established exceptions: (1) agreement; (2) a **pre-existing substantive legal relationship** (e.g., assignee-assignor, successor, bailee-bailor); (3) **adequate representation** by a party in limited circumstances (class actions, trustees, guardians); (4) **assumption of control** over the earlier litigation; (5) litigation by a **proxy** or agent of the bound party; (6) **special statutory schemes** (bankruptcy, probate). [V]
- **Choice of law for preclusive effect** [K]: 
  - A **state-court judgment** in a **federal** court (or another state's court): the full-faith-and-credit statute, **28 USC 1738**, requires giving it the same preclusive effect it would have in the courts of the rendering State (*Migra v. Warren City School District*, 1984; *Kremer v. Chemical Construction*, 1982).
  - A **federal-question** judgment of a federal court: **federal common law** (and federal preclusion rules) governs its effect (*Taylor*; *Semtek*).
  - A **diversity** judgment of a federal court: **federal common law** governs, which adopts the preclusion law the courts of the **State in which the federal court sits** would apply, unless that law is incompatible with federal interests (*Semtek International v. Lockheed Martin*, 2001): a federal court's Rule 41(b) "on the merits" dismissal on limitations grounds does not automatically bar refiling in a state court with a longer limitations period. This is a favorite NCBE pairing (Klaxon ≠ Semtek).

### 15.2 Fact patterns

**PR-1 (same transaction).** A driver sues another driver for property damage from a collision and wins a judgment. The next year, she sues the same driver for personal injuries from the same collision. The defendant moves to dismiss. Result?
(A) Denied; different damages are different claims. (B) Denied; personal injury claims could not have been joined. (C) **Granted; both claims arise from the same transaction, so claim preclusion bars the second action.** (D) Granted, but only if the first judgment was after a jury trial.
*Best: C.*

**PR-2 (jurisdictional dismissal).** A plaintiff's federal suit is dismissed for lack of personal jurisdiction over the defendant. She refiles the same claim in the defendant's home State. The defendant argues claim preclusion. Result?
(A) Barred; any dismissal is a final judgment. (B) **Not barred; a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction is not an adjudication on the merits.** (C) Barred because she could have filed there first. (D) Barred unless she appeals.
*Best: B.*

**PR-3 (12(b)(6) preclusion).** A plaintiff's claim for breach of contract against a defendant is dismissed under 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim; she does not appeal or amend. A year later she files a new suit for the same breach, adding facts omitted earlier. The court should:
(A) Allow it; a 12(b)(6) dismissal is not on the merits. (B) **Dismiss it; a dismissal for failure to state a claim is a judgment on the merits, and the same claim cannot be relitigated.** (C) Allow it because new facts. (D) Allow if she pays costs.
*Best: B.*

**PR-4 (offensive non-mutual, Parklane).** A plane crash kills one passenger; the airline is found negligent in a $50,000 suit by his estate. Later, 200 other plaintiffs, each claiming millions, sue the airline and seek to preclude it from relitigating negligence. Many did not join the first case, though they could have. Is offensive nonmutual issue preclusion proper?
(A) Yes; issue preclusion is automatic. (B) Yes; the airline lost fully. (C) **Not necessarily; the court has discretion and should deny preclusion where the defendant had little incentive to litigate vigorously in the first small suit and where plaintiffs "wait and see".** (D) No; nonmutual preclusion is always barred.
*Best: C.* [Parklane factors]

**PR-5 (defensive non-mutual).** A plaintiff sues Company A for patent infringement and loses after full litigation because the court holds the patent invalid. She then sues Company B on the same patent. Company B asserts issue preclusion. Result?
(A) Rejected; B was not a party to the first case. (B) **Allowed; defensive nonmutual issue preclusion is permitted where the plaintiff had a full and fair opportunity to litigate patent validity.** (C) Rejected; patents are exclusively federal. (D) Allowed only if B participated.
*Best: B* (*Blonder-Tongue*).

**PR-6 (Semtek).** A federal court sitting in diversity in State X dismisses a plaintiff's state-law claim as time-barred; the dismissal order does not say "without prejudice." The plaintiff refiles the claim in State Y, whose statute of limitations is longer. Which law decides the preclusive effect of the federal dismissal?
(A) State Y preclusion law. (B) Uniform federal preclusion rule that Rule 41(b) dismissals are always preclusive. (C) **Federal common law, which incorporates the preclusion law of State X, where the federal court sits, unless incompatible with federal interests.** (D) The law of the State whose substantive law governed the claim.
*Best: C* (*Semtek*). Trap: B. 

**PR-7 (criminal acquittal).** A defendant is acquitted of assault in a criminal trial. The victim sues him for battery in a civil action and wants to prove the assault. The defendant invokes issue preclusion based on the acquittal. Result?
(A) Precluded; he was found not guilty of the same act. (B) **Not precluded; the burden of proof in the criminal case (beyond a reasonable doubt) was higher than in the civil case.** (C) Precluded; mutuality is unnecessary. (D) Precluded only if there was a jury.
*Best: B.* (Inverse: a criminal conviction can be used against the defendant in a later civil suit.)

**PR-8 (nonparty, Taylor).** A group member sues a federal agency under the FOIA and loses. A second member with the same goals who knew of the first suit but had no control over it and did not agree to be bound brings a materially identical FOIA claim. The agency invokes preclusion on "virtual representation". Result?
(A) Precluded because the first litigant adequately represented the group. (B) Precluded because of same interests. (C) **Not precluded merely because of shared interests or a relationship; none of the established nonparty exceptions applies, and virtual representation is rejected.** (D) Precluded because both are members.
*Best: C.* [V]

**PR-9 (necessary to the judgment).** In a first action on a contract, the jury returns a general verdict for the defendant. The court's opinion also says, in passing, that the defendant had been negligent in an unrelated respect, an issue that was neither argued nor necessary to the decision. In a second action, the plaintiff seeks to use that statement against the defendant. Result?
(A) Precluded; the court said so. (B) **Not precluded; the issue was not actually litigated and was not essential to the judgment.** (C) Precluded; judges' statements bind. (D) Precluded if the defendant lost on appeal.
*Best: B.*

**PR-10 (state judgment in federal court).** A state court enters a judgment that, under that State's law, bars a later suit on the same claim. The plaintiff files the same claim in federal court under a federal statute within the court's jurisdiction. Which preclusion law governs?
(A) Federal common law preclusion rules. (B) **28 USC 1738 requires the federal court to give the judgment the same preclusive effect as the State's own courts would.** (C) None; federal courts are not bound by state judgments. (D) Whatever the federal judge decides is fair.
*Best: B.* [K; exceptions where Congress has expressly provided otherwise]

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Claim preclusion: same parties, same transaction, final merits judgment: you can't bring it again, or what you could have brought." / "Issue preclusion: same issue, really fought, really decided, really needed. Non-mutual is allowed, but offensive is the judge's call."

## 16. VII Appealability and review (cp15)

### 16.1 Black-letter rules

- **Final judgment rule (28 USC 1291)** [K; 1291 text not opened; V for 1292]: courts of appeals have jurisdiction over **final decisions** of district courts: a decision that ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment (*Catlin v. United States*, 1945). Interlocutory orders (denial of a motion to dismiss, denial of summary judgment, discovery rulings, a ruling on a motion to compel, denial of class certification (but 23(f)), transfer, a TRO, rulings on motions in limine, denial of a motion to disqualify counsel) are not appealable until final judgment; they can be reviewed with the final judgment. Orders that dispose of **fewer than all claims or parties** are not final without a **Rule 54(b)** determination [V].
- **Exceptions (interlocutory review)**:
  - **28 USC 1292(a)(1)**: orders **granting, continuing, modifying, refusing or dissolving injunctions**, or refusing to dissolve or modify them (also (a)(2) receivers; (a)(3) admiralty). [V]
  - **28 USC 1292(b) certification**: the district judge states in writing in the order that it involves a **controlling question of law** as to which there is **substantial ground for difference of opinion** and that an **immediate appeal may materially advance the ultimate termination of the litigation**; the court of appeals **may, in its discretion**, permit the appeal if application is made within **10 days** after entry of the order; the application does not stay proceedings unless ordered. [V]
  - **Rule 54(b) partial final judgment** [V]; **Rule 23(f)** class-certification petition within 14 days [V].
  - **Collateral order doctrine** (*Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan*, 1949) [K]: an order that (1) conclusively determines a disputed question, (2) resolves an important issue completely separate from the merits, and (3) would be effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment. Examples: denial of absolute or qualified immunity on a question of law (*Mitchell v. Forsyth*), denial of Eleventh Amendment immunity, certain bond orders. Not collateral orders: discovery orders on privilege (*Mohawk Industries v. Carpenter*, 2009), denial of a motion to disqualify counsel, denial of motion to dismiss on forum-selection grounds, *derivative sovereign (Yearsley) immunity denials* (*GEO Group v. Menocal*, Feb. 25, 2026, Kagan) [V via CRS]. 
  - **Mandamus (28 USC 1651)**: extraordinary; requires clear right and no other adequate means [K].
  - **Arbitration**: denial of a motion to compel arbitration is appealable (9 USC 16(a)), and the district court proceedings are automatically stayed pending appeal (*Coinbase v. Bielski*, 2023) [K].
  - **Contempt**: a non-party or party may disobey a discovery order and appeal the resulting contempt [K].
  - **Remand orders** from removal are **unreviewable** (28 USC 1447(d)) [V]. Orders of **transfer** under 1404(a) are not appealable [K].
  - **Pendent appellate jurisdiction** is very narrow (*Swint v. Chambers County Commission*, 1995) [K]. A plaintiff cannot **manufacture finality** by voluntarily dismissing remaining claims after losing a class certification ruling (*Microsoft v. Baker*, 2017) [K].
- **Timing** [V]: notice of appeal within **30 days** after entry of judgment (60 if the US is a party); **timely** Rule 50(b), 52(b), 59 (and 60 if filed within 28 days) motions restart the 30 days from the order disposing of the last such motion; an extension of up to 30 days is available for excusable neglect or good cause if sought within 30 days after the original deadline; reopening for 14 days if no notice of entry (FRAP 4(a)(6)); the time limit in a civil case is jurisdictional because it is statutory (28 USC 2107; *Bowles v. Russell*, 2007) [K].
- **Standards of review (NextGen lists; MBE "scope of review")** [V list; K examples]:
  - **De novo** (no deference): conclusions of law; interpretation of statutes/rules/contracts (usually); grant or denial of **summary judgment**; **JMOL** (same standard as the trial court); 12(b)(6) dismissals; dismissals for lack of SMJ or PJ (jurisdictional facts: findings reviewed for clear error); Seventh Amendment issues; constitutional excessiveness of punitive damages (*Cooper Industries v. Leatherman*, 2001) [K].
  - **Clearly erroneous**: a **trial judge's findings of fact** after a bench trial (Rule 52(a)(6) [V]): reversal only if the reviewing court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake was made; where there are two permissible views of the evidence, the factfinder's choice between them cannot be clearly erroneous (*Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 1985) [K]; special deference to credibility findings.
  - **Abuse of discretion**: evidentiary rulings; discovery rulings and sanctions; **grant or denial of new trial** (and remittitur); class certification; leave to amend; 1404(a) transfer; forum non conveniens; case-management orders; PI decisions (legal conclusions de novo, facts for clear error, balance for abuse of discretion) [K].
  - **Jury verdict**: reviewed only through the post-verdict JMOL record (sufficiency requires a Rule 50(a)/(b) record, *Unitherm* [K]); the reviewing court views the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner.
  - **Plain error**: for **unpreserved** errors (e.g., jury instructions not objected to under Rule 51(d)(2) [V]; Rule 103(e) in evidence [K]); **harmless error**: errors not affecting substantial rights are disregarded (Rule 61; 28 USC 2111) [K].
  - **Preservation**: arguments not raised below are generally forfeited; new theories on appeal are heard only in exceptional circumstances [K].
  - **Mixed questions:** law-fact mixed questions get deference depending on whether the inquiry is primarily legal or factual (*U.S. Bank v. Village at Lakeridge*, 2018) [K].
- **Seventh Amendment limit**: an appellate court cannot reexamine facts tried by a jury other than as the common law permitted [K].

**AP-1 (final judgment).** A district court denies a defendant's motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. The defendant wants to appeal immediately. The court of appeals:
(A) Must hear it as of right. (B) Must hear it as a collateral order. (A) The court of appeals must hear it. (B) **The court of appeals may, in its discretion, accept the appeal; the application was timely (10 days) and the district court's certification does not guarantee review.** (C) The district court proceedings are automatically stayed. (D) The application is untimely.

**AP-5 (collateral order, privilege).** A district court orders a party to turn over documents the party claims are attorney-client privileged. The party seeks an immediate appeal. Under the collateral-order doctrine:
(A) The order is immediately appealable; privilege rulings are collateral. (B) **The order is not immediately appealable; effective review is available after final judgment (and a party can seek mandamus or resist and appeal a contempt order).** (C) Appealable only if the privilege is constitutional. (D) Appealable under Rule 23(f).
*Best: B* (*Mohawk*).

**AP-6 (standards of review).** Match the ruling to the standard on appeal:
- Findings of fact after a bench trial → **clearly erroneous**.
- Summary judgment grant → **de novo**.
- Denial of a motion for a new trial → **abuse of discretion**.
- Instruction error not objected to → **plain error**.
- Exclusion of evidence → **abuse of discretion** (and harmless error).
- Decision on a pure question of law → **de novo**.
- JMOL → **de novo**.
(Use as a match-pairs item.)

**AP-7 (clearly erroneous).** After a bench trial, the judge finds that the plaintiff, not the defendant, signed the contract, based mostly on conflicting testimony. On appeal, the defendant says the appellate court should weigh the evidence itself. The standard is:
(A) De novo. (B) **Clearly erroneous, with due regard for the trial judge's opportunity to judge credibility.** (C) Abuse of discretion. (D) Plain error.
*Best: B.*

**AP-8 (appeal clock).** Judgment is entered on September 1. On September 20 the losing party files a Rule 59(e) motion; on October 5 the court denies it. By what date must notice of appeal be filed?
(A) October 1. (B) **November 4 (30 days from October 5), because the timely Rule 59(e) motion restarted the clock.** (C) October 20. (D) December 5.
*Best: B.* (October 5 + 30 days = November 4.)

**AP-9 (remand orders).** A federal court remands a removed case to state court because it concludes the removal notice was filed late. The defendant tries to appeal. The court of appeals:
(A) Reviews for abuse of discretion. (B) Reviews de novo. (C) **Lacks jurisdiction; remand orders based on non-jurisdictional or jurisdictional grounds listed in 1447(c) are not reviewable on appeal or otherwise (except 1442/1443 removals).** (D) Must hear it as a collateral order.
*Best: C.* [V]

**AP-10 (summary judgment denial after trial).** The court denies the defendant's motion for summary judgment on a factual issue. After a full trial, the jury finds for the plaintiff. The defendant, who never moved for JMOL, appeals arguing the evidence was insufficient and that summary judgment should have been granted. The court of appeals:
(A) Reviews the denial of summary judgment de novo. (B) **Will not review the denial of summary judgment on a factual sufficiency ground after a full trial on the merits, and without a Rule 50 record cannot review sufficiency.** (C) Remands for a new trial. (D) Reverses automatically.
*Best: B* (*Ortiz v. Jordan*; *Unitherm*). Contrast MO-7.

*Teach-it-in-one-breath drafts:* "Final judgment or an exception: injunctions, a 1292(b) green light, 54(b), the collateral-order trio." / "Facts after a bench trial: clearly erroneous. Law: de novo. Judge's discretion: abuse of discretion."

## 18. Cross-cutting trap catalog (for "mistakes notebook" tags)

1. **Right result, wrong reason.** MBE options often pair the correct disposition with a legally wrong reason. (Most items above include one.)
2. **Federal defense / federal counterclaim does not create federal-question jurisdiction.** Removal needs the plaintiff's complaint to arise under federal law.
3. **LLCs and partnerships take every member's citizenship**; corporations take two (incorporation + nerve center).
4. **Forum-defendant rule is waivable; SMJ is not.**
5. **1367(b) restricts only plaintiffs' claims in diversity-only cases** (defendants' counterclaims, crossclaims, impleader claims are fine).
6. **A Rule 12(b) motion does not cut off a Rule 41(a)(1) voluntary dismissal.** Only an answer or a summary-judgment motion does.
7. **Waived by omission:** 12(b)(2), (3), (4), (5). **Never waived:** 12(b)(1); preserved to trial: 12(b)(6), (7).
8. **Rule 4(d) waiver of service does not waive PJ or venue objections.**
9. **Rule 11 safe harbor: serve, don't file, for 21 days.** Rule 11 does not reach discovery papers.
10. **50(b) needs a prior 50(a).** JMOL: no weighing. New trial: may weigh.
11. **28 days is not extendable** for 50(b), 52(b), 59(b), (d), (e), 60(b)'s one-year limit.
12. **Additur: never in federal court. Remittitur: with a new-trial option.**
13. **1404(a) transfer carries the transferor's law (except forum-selection-clause transfers); 1406(a)/1631 transfers use the transferee's law.**
14. **Klaxon (choice of law for claims) vs Semtek (preclusive effect of a federal diversity judgment).** Different rules, different States.
15. **Rule 14 impleader requires derivative liability**, not an "it was really him" defense.
16. **Rule 19 joint tortfeasors are not required parties.**
17. **Offer of judgment (Rule 68):** only defendants; costs, not fees (unless statute); not if defendant wins.
18. **Remand orders are not appealable** (except 1442/1443); **denials of motions to dismiss are not appealable**; **PI grants/denials are.**
19. **Standard of review:** facts after a bench trial = clearly erroneous; law = de novo; discretion = abuse of discretion; unobjected instruction = plain error.
20. **Preclusion:** a 12(b)(6) dismissal IS on the merits; jurisdiction/venue/Rule 19 dismissals are NOT; a second notice dismissal IS.
