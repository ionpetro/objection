# Constitutional Law on the MBE: research notes for "Objection"

Researched 2026-11-02. Scope: what the MBE Constitutional Law section tests, what changes under NextGen, current law as of Nov 2026 (post-Dobbs, Bruen, 303 Creative, Loper Bright, and the October Term 2025 decisions), traps, and our OWN exam-style fact patterns.

**Copyright rule:** every fact pattern below is original (written for this project). Do not paste NCBE sample or released questions or any commercial outline text into the app. The NCBE outline and sample PDF were downloaded only to `research/source-data/` (gitignored) to learn scope and phrasing.

**Confidence tags used below**
- `[P]` = I read the primary text (NCBE PDF, Supreme Court slip opinion or syllabus, LII text) this session.
- `[S]` = confirmed in a reliable secondary source this session (SCOTUSblog, Oyez, CRS/Constitution Annotated, LII summaries) but I did not read the opinion itself.
- `[M]` = from my own knowledge of settled law (well known, not re-fetched this session). Re-check before shipping if a card depends on a fine detail.
- `[?]` = uncertain or sources disagree. Do not ship without checking.

## 1. What the exam actually tests

- MBE = 200 multiple-choice questions, 6 hours, 100 morning + 100 afternoon. **175 scored + 25 unscored pretest** questions. 7 subjects, **25 scored questions each**, Constitutional Law is one of them. [P] (https://www.ncbex.org/exams/mbe/preparing ; fact sheet https://www.ncbex.org/sites/default/files/2025-11/NextGen%20UBE%20Fact%20Sheet%20111825.pdf)
- NCBE's own subject-matter outline (file dated 2023-01, "Copyright 2020"): https://www.ncbex.org/sites/default/files/2023-01/MBE_Subject_Matter_Outline.pdf [P]. Key official statements:
  - "The terms 'Constitution,' 'constitutional,' and 'unconstitutional' refer to the federal Constitution unless indicated otherwise." So never teach state constitutions as tested.
  - **"Approximately half of the Constitutional Law questions on the MBE will be based on category IV [individual rights], and approximately half on the remaining categories I, II and III."** So about 12-13 of 25 scored questions are individual rights.
  - "The outline is not intended to list every aspect of a topic... each topic is not necessarily tested on each examination." NCBE publishes no per-topic frequency numbers. **Do not invent frequencies.** (Third-party frequency charts exist, e.g. JD Advising; I did not read or verify them. Do not cite them.)
- Official outline structure (this is the skeleton the units should follow):
  - **I. Nature of judicial review:** A. organization and relationship of state and federal courts in a federal system; B. jurisdiction (1. congressional power to define and limit; 2. the Eleventh Amendment and state sovereign immunity); C. judicial review in operation (1. "case or controversy": no advisory opinions, standing, ripeness, mootness; 2. the "adequate and independent state ground"; 3. political questions and justiciability).
  - **II. Separation of powers:** A. Congress (1. commerce, taxing, spending; 2. war, defense, foreign affairs; 3. power to enforce the 13th, 14th, 15th Amendments; 4. other powers); B. President (1. chief executive incl. "take care"; 2. commander in chief; 3. treaty and foreign affairs; 4. appointment and removal); C. federal interbranch relationships (1. congressional limits on the executive; 2. presentment, veto, withholding action; 3. nondelegation; 4. executive, legislative, judicial immunities).
  - **III. Nation and states:** A. intergovernmental immunities (1. federal immunity from state law; 2. state immunity from federal law incl. 10th Amendment); B. federalism-based limits on state authority (1. negative implications of the commerce clause; 2. supremacy clause and preemption; 3. authorization of otherwise invalid state action).
  - **IV. Individual rights:** A. state action; B. due process (substantive: fundamental rights, other rights and interests; procedural); C. equal protection (fundamental rights, heightened scrutiny classifications, rational basis); D. takings; E. other protections: privileges and immunities clauses, contracts clause, unconstitutional conditions, bills of attainder, ex post facto; F. First Amendment (1. religion: free exercise, establishment; 2. expression: content-based, content-neutral, unprotected expression, commercial speech, public school students/public employment/licenses/benefits, expressive conduct, prior restraint/vagueness/overbreadth; 3. press; 4. association).
- Question phrasing seen in the NCBE free sample (https://www.ncbex.org/sites/default/files/2025-06/MBE%20Sample%20Test%20Questions.pdf) [P], for stem style only: "Is this law constitutional?"; "Which of the following statements about this law is most accurate as a matter of constitutional law?"; "Does the Court have jurisdiction to review the merits of this case?"; answer choices start "Yes, because..." / "No, because..." and the tempting wrong ones state a true-sounding rule that does not apply (e.g. "does not affect a fundamental right or suspect class", "Tenth Amendment reserves this to states"). Our cards should mimic that format and the "best answer" idea.
- **Lag before new cases are tested:** NCBE says its questions "typically are drafted, revised, and finalized over an approximately three-year period" and are pretested on live exams (https://www.ncbex.org/news/ncbe-statement-on-scotus-decisions-and-the-bar-exam/ [P]). For July 2022 NCBE told examinees they would not need that term's decisions (secondary report; exact current wording of the NCBE timeline article not retrievable, 403) [S]. **Implication: for Feb and July 2027 sittings, decisions from OT2024-OT2025 (2025-2026) are very unlikely to be scored; OT2022-OT2023 decisions (SFFA, 303 Creative, Counterman, Tyler, etc.) are plausible; Dobbs (2022) and Bruen (2022) are in range.** Mark every post-mid-2024 card `recent: true` and keep it in a "Keeping current" side unit, not the core MBE path. Rules that older prep books teach and 2026 changed (Humphrey's Executor) need a flag, because the exam may still reflect the older rule while "best answer" under today's law differs.

So **Feb 2027 and July 2027: most candidates (New York, California, Texas, Florida, etc.) still sit the legacy UBE/MBE; a minority sit NextGen.** The exact MBE dates for 2027 were not verified (normally the last Wednesday of Feb and July; do not hard-code).
- NextGen does not have a standalone 200-question MBE. It tests 8 "foundational concepts" (business associations, civil procedure, **constitutional law**, contracts, criminal law and constitutional protections of accused persons, evidence, real property, torts; family law added July 2028) through multiple-choice, integrated question sets and performance tasks, over 1.5 days (two 3-hour sessions day 1, one on day 2). [P, fact sheet]
- **NextGen Constitutional Law outline (Aug 2025, valid July 2026-Feb 2027) differs from the MBE outline.** [P] Structure: I. Federal judicial power (standing/case or controversy, ripeness, mootness, advisory opinions, Eleventh Amendment, congressional power over federal jurisdiction); II. Legislative powers (commerce, taxing, spending; enforcing 13th-15th Amendments); III. Executive powers (commander in chief; appointment and removal; **powers of federal administrative agencies as enforcers, rule-makers and adjudicators and Congress's power to delegate**); IV. Nation and states (intergovernmental immunities incl. state taxation of federal entities and 10th Amendment anticommandeering; supremacy/preemption incl. presumption against preemption; **dormant commerce clause incl. congressional authorization and the market participant doctrine**); V. Individual rights (state action **and the exclusive-government-functions exception**; substantive due process: privacy (medical care refusal, educate children, live with whom one chooses, marriage, contraception), **right to vote, right to travel, right to bear arms**, standards of review; procedural due process; equal protection (strict/intermediate/rational with the listed classes); takings; ex post facto; religion clauses; speech (content-based, content-neutral and forum designations, expressive conduct, unprotected expression, commercial speech, public school students/public employees/unconstitutional conditions, prior restraint/vagueness/overbreadth); press; association).
- **On the MBE outline but NOT listed in the NextGen Constitutional Law outline (text search of the Aug 2025 PDF found no mention):** political question doctrine, adequate and independent state ground, war/defense/foreign affairs powers, treaty power, presentment/veto, the contracts clause, bills of attainder, privileges and immunities clauses, congressional power to enforce beyond 13/14/15 is kept. **On NextGen but not on the MBE outline:** right to vote, right to travel and right to bear arms as named substantive-due-process topics; the market participant doctrine by name; state-action "exclusive government functions"; agency powers; "right to educate one's children / live with whomever one chooses" named. (Stars marking "recall-only, no resources provided" topics were lost in PDF text extraction; check the PDF.)
- **Recommendation for the app:** build the core path on the **MBE outline** (that is what most 2027 takers sit) and tag every unit/card `mbe`, `nextgen`, or both. Add small NextGen-only cards (agency powers, right to vote/travel/arms, market participant) and flag MBE-only cards (political question, A&I state ground, war/treaty, presentment, contracts clause, attainder, P&I) so a NextGen candidate can toggle them off. Keep a "Jurisdiction" setting (MBE or NextGen) like Carpe's exam-date setting.

## 4. Teaching conventions (for the unit files)

- Rule statements below are written as the best-answer rule. Each "item" in the app should carry `status`: `book` (settled, in every outline), `exam` (tested style), `general`, or `unsure` (see `[?]`), plus `src` (case or constitutional text). Cite the clause or case in `src`, not a commercial outline.
- "Majority rule vs minority rule" barely appears in Con Law; the equivalent is **"the best answer"** among options that each state a real rule. The usual trap: a true rule that belongs to a different test (strict scrutiny where only rational basis applies; Tenth Amendment where the real issue is preemption; Eleventh Amendment where the defendant is a county).
- Tests that make good **build-from-tiles** cards: standing (injury in fact, traceable, redressable), *Dole* spending conditions, *Pike*, *Complete Auto*, *Central Hudson*, *Brandenburg*, *Miller*, *Mathews*, *Penn Central*, *Lucas*, *Nollan/Dolan*, *Ward* TPM, *O'Brien*, *Pickering*, *Youngstown* categories, *Baker v. Carr* factors, Michigan v. Long presumption.
- **Yes/no sorting cards:** state action? (private mall guard no, company town yes, state athletic association with public-school entwinement yes); Eleventh Amendment bars it? (state agency yes, county no, official for prospective relief no); which scrutiny? (race strict, alienage by state strict but political-function exception, gender intermediate, age/wealth/disability rational basis).
- **Match pairs:** case to holding (one line each, as below); also "overruled/abandoned" pairs: *Lemon* to *Kennedy*; *Roe/Casey* to *Dobbs*; *Bowers* to *Lawrence*; *Korematsu* to *Trump v. Hawaii*; *Humphrey's Executor* to *Slaughter*; *Colorado II* to *NRSC*; *National League of Cities* to *Garcia*.
- **Mascot hooks:** use "Objection!" moods sparingly: happy on correct, cheer on streaks, sad on a miss with a one-line "teach-it-in-one-breath" rule.

## 5. I. THE NATURE OF JUDICIAL REVIEW (MBE category I)

### 5.1 Jurisdiction and structure
- **Judicial review of federal acts:** *Marbury v. Madison* (1803). Review of state-court judgments on federal questions: *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee*, *Cohens v. Virginia*. Federal courts' constitutional interpretation binds the states: *Cooper v. Aaron*. [M]
- **Congress controls jurisdiction:** Congress creates lower federal courts and sets their jurisdiction; Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction is "with such Exceptions... as the Congress shall make" (Art. III, §2) (*Ex parte McCardle*). Limits: Congress cannot dictate how a court must decide a pending case under existing law (*United States v. Klein*), cannot violate other constitutional provisions, cannot reopen final judgments (*Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm*), and **cannot add to the Supreme Court's constitutionally fixed original jurisdiction** (*Marbury*). Original jurisdiction: cases affecting ambassadors, public ministers and consuls, and cases in which a State is a party. [M]
- **Article I (legislative) courts / public rights:** Congress can assign some matters to non-Article III adjudicators (public rights); *Stern v. Marshall* (counterclaim in bankruptcy) and *SEC v. Jarkesy* (2024: civil penalties for securities fraud need a jury; public-rights exception narrow) limit this. [M]

### 5.2 Eleventh Amendment and state sovereign immunity (outline I.B.2)
Rule: a State cannot be sued by a private party (including its own citizen) in federal court (*Hans v. Louisiana*), in its own courts (*Alden v. Maine*), or before a federal agency adjudication (*FMC v. South Carolina Ports Authority*) without consent or valid abrogation. [M]
Exceptions (memory aid: **C-A-Y**): **Consent/waiver** (must be unequivocal; removal to federal court waives immunity from suit in federal court for claims the State has waived in its own courts: *Lapides*); **Abrogation** by Congress only under §5 of the 14th Amendment with congruence and proportionality (*Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer*; *Seminole Tribe v. Florida*: no abrogation under the Commerce Clause; *Kimel* (ADEA) and *Board of Trustees v. Garrett* (ADA Title I damages) fail; *Tennessee v. Lane* (Title II access to courts) and *Nevada v. Hibbs* (FMLA) pass; *Katz*, *PennEast* and *Torres v. Texas DPS* (2022: war powers) recognize "plan of the Convention" waivers); **Young**: *Ex parte Young* allows suits against a state officer in official capacity for **prospective** injunctive relief against ongoing violation of **federal** law. Not allowed: retroactive money from state treasury (*Edelman v. Jordan*), state-law claims (*Pennhurst*). [M]
Traps: the Amendment does **not** protect counties, cities, school districts or other local governments (*Mt. Healthy*; *Lincoln County*), and does not bar damages against an official in their **personal** capacity (*Hafer v. Melo*), or suits by the United States or by another State. "Arm of the state" decides state agencies (state universities usually yes). A State is not a "person" under 42 U.S.C. 1983 (*Will*) - statutory, not constitutional. *Galette* (2026) is about immunity in *other states' courts*, and is not the Eleventh Amendment test. [S]

### 5.3 "Case or controversy": no advisory opinions, standing, ripeness, mootness (outline I.C.1)
- **No advisory opinions** (*Hayburn's Case*, the 1793 Jay letter, *Muskrat v. United States*). A declaratory judgment is fine if there is a real adverse dispute. [M]
- **Standing (burden on the plaintiff, at each stage):** (1) **injury in fact**: concrete and particularized, actual or imminent (not conjectural); (2) **causation**: fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct; (3) **redressability** by the requested relief (*Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*). [M]
  - Future harm for injunctions: real and immediate threat (*City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*); "certainly impending" (*Clapper*); pre-enforcement challenge OK if intention to engage in conduct arguably protected and a credible threat of enforcement (*Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*). Statutory violation alone is not enough; harm must be concrete (*TransUnion v. Ramirez*, 2021). Nominal damages redress a past completed violation (*Uzuegbunam*). [M]
  - **Generalized grievances / citizen standing:** no (*Richardson*, *Schlesinger*). **Federal taxpayer:** no, except the narrow *Flast v. Cohen* exception for Establishment Clause challenges to **congressional** exercises of the taxing and spending power (confined by *Hein* (executive discretionary spending) and *Arizona Christian School Tuition Org. v. Winn* (tax credits)). State/municipal taxpayers: municipal taxpayers usually have standing to challenge illegal local spending. [M]
  - **Third-party standing (jus tertii):** only with a close relation to the right-holder and a hindrance to the right-holder suing (*Powers v. Ohio*; *Singleton v. Wulff*); overbreadth in 1st Amendment cases is a separate exception. **Organizations:** members would have standing, interests germane, no need for individual members' participation (*Hunt v. Washington Apple*). **States:** no parens patriae suit against the federal government (*Mellon*), but "special solicitude" (*Massachusetts v. EPA*); *United States v. Texas* (2023): no standing to challenge federal arrest-priorities policy; *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023): Missouri had standing through MOHELA; *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* (2024): doctors lacked standing; *Murthy v. Missouri* (2024): no standing where traceability to the government's conduct was not shown. [M]
  - Recent: *Bost* (2026) candidate standing; *First Choice v. Davenport* (2026) present associational injury from a subpoena. [S]
- **Ripeness:** fitness of the issues + hardship of withholding review (*Abbott Labs*). Pre-enforcement challenge ripe when threat of enforcement is credible. [M]
- **Mootness:** the controversy must live through all stages. Exceptions: **capable of repetition yet evading review** (same party reasonably expected to face it again + duration too short to litigate); **voluntary cessation** (defendant bears a heavy burden to show conduct cannot reasonably recur: *Friends of the Earth*, *FBI v. Fikre* (2024)); **class actions** certified before mootness (*Sosna*); **collateral consequences** of criminal convictions (*Sibron*, but not parole revocation expiry in *Spencer v. Kemna*). [M]
- **Political questions (outline I.C.3)** (*Baker v. Carr* factors): textually demonstrable commitment to another branch; lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards; need for an initial nonjudicial policy decision; and respect/embarrassment factors. Examples: not justiciable: partisan gerrymandering (*Rucho v. Common Cause*, 2019); Guaranty Clause "republican form" (*Luther v. Borden*); Senate impeachment trial procedure (*Nixon v. United States*, 1993); much of foreign affairs (*Goldwater v. Carter*). Justiciable: whether the House can exclude a member who meets the Art. I qualifications (*Powell v. McCormack*); racial gerrymandering; one person one vote (*Baker v. Carr*, *Reynolds*); the President's seizure of steel mills (*Youngstown*); a passport birthplace statute (*Zivotofsky v. Clinton*, 2012). [M]
- **Adequate and independent state ground (outline I.C.2):** the Supreme Court will not review a state-court judgment that rests on a state-law ground that is **independent** of the federal question and **adequate** to support the judgment, since reversing on federal law would change nothing (*Fox Film*; *Herb v. Pitcairn*). **Michigan v. Long:** if the state court's decision appears to rest primarily on federal law or is interwoven with it, and does not clearly and expressly state an adequate and independent state ground, the Court presumes jurisdiction. A State may give **more** protection than the federal floor (*PruneYard*). A state procedural default is "adequate" unless unusual or discriminatory in application (*Lee v. Kemna*, *Henry v. Mississippi*). Only the **highest state court** (that can hear the case) judgment is reviewable (28 U.S.C. 1257). [M]

**Traps:** (1) Calling a county "the State" for Eleventh Amendment. (2) Treating *Ex parte Young* as allowing money damages. (3) Saying "taxpayer standing" without the *Flast* conditions. (4) Mixing ripeness with standing in pre-enforcement challenges. (5) Mootness with "voluntary cessation" (not moot). (6) State court "relies on federal cases for guidance" does not make a state ground independent.

### 5.4 Own fact patterns: Judicial review (all original)

**JR-1 (Flast).** A federal statute directs the Secretary of Education to distribute $40 million in annual grants to church-run schools to buy religious-instruction materials. A federal income-tax payer, objecting to the use of tax dollars for religion, sues the Secretary in federal court on Establishment Clause grounds. She alleges no other injury.
Will the court dismiss for lack of standing?
(A) Yes, because a taxpayer's interest in federal spending is a generalized grievance shared by all taxpayers.
(B) Yes, because the Establishment Clause protects only against coercion, and she alleges none.
(C) No, because a federal taxpayer may challenge an exercise of Congress's taxing and spending power as violating the Establishment Clause.
(D) No, because an injunction would redress any injury she suffers as a citizen.
**Best: C** (*Flast*, still good for congressional spending). A states the general rule that the narrow *Flast* exception displaces. B confuses the merits with standing. D: citizenship alone is not an injury.

**JR-2 (Lyons).** Two years ago a city police officer used a prohibited chokehold on a man during a traffic stop. The man was not seriously hurt. He now sues the city in federal court for an injunction barring the department from authorizing chokeholds, alleging the department's policy allows them. He does not allege he expects to be stopped again.
Should the court dismiss?
(A) No, because a past constitutional violation is enough for injunctive relief.
(B) No, because the policy is capable of repetition yet evading review.
(C) Yes, because he has not shown a real and immediate threat that he personally will be subjected to a chokehold again.
(D) Yes, because federal courts may never enjoin local police policies.
**Best: C** (*Lyons*). A: past harm supports damages, not forward-looking relief. B: the mootness exception is not standing. D: federal courts may enjoin unconstitutional police practices.

**JR-3 (pre-enforcement).** A state statute makes it a misdemeanor to distribute leaflets "urging the boycott of any licensed business" within 100 feet of a store. The Attorney General has publicly said he will prosecute violators. A union organizer who plans to leaflet at a store next week sues in federal court for a declaration that the law violates the First Amendment. She has not leafleted and has not been charged.
Is the claim justiciable?
(A) Yes, because she intends conduct arguably protected by the First Amendment and faces a credible threat of prosecution.
(B) No, because she must first violate the law and be prosecuted.
(C) No, because the claim is an impermissible request for an advisory opinion.
(D) Yes, but only if she can show that other persons have already been prosecuted.
**Best: A.** B and C ignore pre-enforcement review. D adds a requirement the cases do not impose.

**JR-4 (voluntary cessation).** After a church sued a city in federal court challenging a zoning ordinance, the city council repealed the ordinance. The council spokesperson said the council "may well bring something similar back next year." The church still seeks an injunction against future similar ordinances.
How should the court rule on the city's motion to dismiss as moot?
(A) Grant it, because the ordinance no longer exists.
(B) Deny it, because a defendant who stops challenged conduct after suit bears a heavy burden to show it cannot reasonably be expected to recur.
(C) Grant it, because mootness can be defeated only by a class action.
(D) Deny it, because any religious claim is automatically capable of repetition.
**Best: B.** A ignores voluntary cessation. C and D invent rules.

**JR-5 (political question).** Voters in a state allege that the legislature drew congressional districts to give one major party an overwhelming advantage, though the districts are equal in population and not drawn on racial lines. They sue in federal court under the Equal Protection Clause.
What is the likely result?
(A) The claim is justiciable and strict scrutiny applies, because voting is a fundamental right.
(B) The claim presents a nonjusticiable political question, because there are no judicially manageable standards for deciding how much partisan advantage is too much.
(C) The claim fails on the merits, because rational basis review applies to every districting plan.
(D) The claim is nonjusticiable only if the plan was drawn by the state legislature rather than a commission.
**Best: B** (*Rucho*, 2019). Racial gerrymanders and population deviations remain justiciable; that is why A is tempting.

**JR-6 (Powell).** The House of Representatives votes by majority to refuse to seat a member-elect who is 35, a citizen for 12 years, and a resident of the state she represents, because a majority considers her "unfit." She sues in federal court.
May the court consider her claim?
(A) No, because the House is the sole judge of its members' qualifications, a political question.
(B) No, because members of Congress lack standing.
(C) Yes, because the Constitution lists the qualifications, and the House may not add to them.
(D) Yes, but only after the House has had 90 days to reconsider.
**Best: C** (*Powell v. McCormack*). A is the textual-commitment trap. Expulsion (2/3) differs from exclusion.

**JR-7 (A&I state ground, plain).** A state supreme court holds a city ordinance invalid under the state constitution's free expression clause, stating that the holding rests "solely and independently" on the state constitution as interpreted in the state's own precedent and that the court does not reach the First Amendment. The city petitions for certiorari.
Does the Supreme Court have jurisdiction?
(A) Yes, because the state court invalidated a local law.
(B) Yes, because the state clause is textually similar to the First Amendment.
(C) No, because the judgment rests on an adequate and independent state ground.
(D) No, because the Supreme Court may review only decisions of federal courts.
**Best: C.** D is false: it reviews state high courts on federal questions. B: similarity is irrelevant when the state court says it is independent.

**JR-8 (Michigan v. Long).** A state supreme court holds a search unconstitutional, citing the Fourth Amendment and federal cases throughout and mentioning the state constitution once in a footnote "for completeness". It says nothing about independent grounds. The State petitions for certiorari.
Does the Supreme Court have jurisdiction?
(A) No, because the state constitution also supports the result.
(B) Yes, because the state court did not clearly and expressly state that its holding rested on an adequate and independent state ground.
(C) No, because search questions are state law matters.
(D) Yes, but only if the defendant consents.
**Best: B** (*Long* presumption).

**JR-9 (Garrett).** A state university employee, fired after a stroke, sues the university (an arm of the State) in federal court for money damages under Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act. The State has not consented.
How should the court rule?
(A) Dismiss, because Congress's attempt to abrogate state immunity for Title I damages exceeded its §5 enforcement power.
(B) Hear the case, because Congress validly abrogated state immunity under the Commerce Clause.
(C) Hear the case, because the claim arises under federal law.
(D) Dismiss, because the Eleventh Amendment bars every suit against a State, including for prospective injunctive relief.
**Best: A** (*Garrett*; *Seminole Tribe*). B is the old pre-1996 view. D ignores *Ex parte Young*.

**JR-10 (Young/Edelman).** A state health agency wrongly terminated Medicaid benefits under a policy that conflicts with federal law. Recipients sue the agency director in his official capacity in federal court, seeking (1) an injunction against continuing the policy and (2) an order requiring payment of the benefits wrongly withheld for the past two years.
What relief may the court grant, if the claims succeed?
(A) Both, because the suit is under federal law.
(B) Neither, because the Eleventh Amendment bars all suits against state officials.
(C) The injunction only; retroactive payment from the state treasury is barred.
(D) The payment only, because injunctions against state officials are barred.
**Best: C** (*Ex parte Young*; *Edelman*).

**JR-11 (Marbury).** Congress enacts a statute giving the Supreme Court original jurisdiction over all petitions for mandamus against federal officials.
Is the statute valid?
(A) Yes, because Congress controls the Supreme Court's jurisdiction.
(B) Yes, because mandamus is a traditional remedy.
(C) No, because Congress may not expand the Supreme Court's original jurisdiction beyond what Article III specifies.
(D) No, because mandamus cases are political questions.
**Best: C** (*Marbury*). A is the McCardle trap: Congress controls *appellate* jurisdiction.

## 6. II. THE SEPARATION OF POWERS (MBE category II)

### 6.1 Powers of Congress (outline II.A)
- **Enumerated powers + Necessary and Proper** (*McCulloch v. Maryland*; *United States v. Comstock*: a statute is valid if rationally related to implementing an enumerated power). Congress has no general police power. [M]
- **Commerce power (Art. I, §8, cl. 3)** - three categories (*United States v. Lopez*): (1) channels of interstate commerce; (2) instrumentalities, persons or things in interstate commerce; (3) activities with a **substantial effect** on interstate commerce. Economic activity may be aggregated (*Wickard*; *Gonzales v. Raich*: local home-grown marijuana within a comprehensive scheme). **Noneconomic, non-aggregable** activity: *Lopez* (gun possession near schools, no jurisdictional element) and *Morrison* (VAWA civil remedy) were invalid. Congress cannot **compel** individuals to buy something (*NFIB v. Sebelius*, 2012). Rational-basis review of congressional findings that the activity substantially affects commerce. Heart of Atlanta/McClung: private discrimination reachable under the commerce power. [M]
- **Taxing power:** valid if it raises revenue even with a regulatory motive (*Sonzinsky*, *Kahriger*); a "penalty" is not a tax when it is a pure punishment (*Bailey v. Drexel Furniture*, child-labor excise). *NFIB*: the individual mandate's payment was a tax (modest amount, IRS-collected, no scienter). Direct taxes must be apportioned (Pollock; *Moore v. United States*, 2024, upheld the MITT without deciding whether realization is required). Export taxes are barred (Art. I, §9). [M]
- **Spending power** (*South Dakota v. Dole*): spending for the general welfare (not limited to enumerated powers: *Butler*); conditions must be (1) in pursuit of the general welfare, (2) **unambiguous**, (3) **related** to a federal interest in the funded program, (4) not barred by another constitutional provision, and (5) **not coercive** ("gun to the head": *NFIB* struck the Medicaid-expansion threat to withdraw **all** existing Medicaid funds). Dole upheld 5% highway-fund loss tied to 21 drinking age; the Twenty-first Amendment did not bar it because the condition itself was not independently unconstitutional. Spending Clause statutes bind individuals only through consent: *Landor* (2026). [M/S]
- **War, defense, foreign affairs:** Congress declares war, raises and supports armies and navy, regulates the military and calls up the militia (Art. I, §8). Foreign commerce, naturalization, immigration (plenary), territories, property (*Kleppe*). Treaties: President + 2/3 of Senate. A valid treaty is supreme law and may let Congress legislate on matters otherwise reserved to the States (*Missouri v. Holland*); a treaty cannot violate the Constitution (*Reid v. Covert*); self-executing vs non-self-executing (*Medellín v. Texas*: ICJ judgment not domestic law without legislation); later-in-time rule between treaty and statute. [M]
- **Power to enforce the 13th, 14th, 15th Amendments:** **13th Amendment §2:** Congress may abolish "badges and incidents of slavery," reaches **private** conduct (*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*). **14th Amendment §5:** remedial, not substantive; legislation must show **congruence and proportionality** between the violation and the remedy (*City of Boerne v. Flores*: RFRA invalid as applied to states); reaches only **state action** (*United States v. Morrison*; *Civil Rights Cases*). Valid: *Katzenbach v. Morgan* (pre-Boerne), *Hibbs* (FMLA family leave), *Tennessee v. Lane*. Invalid: *Kimel*, *Garrett*. **15th Amendment §2:** *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* upheld VRA preclearance; *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) invalidated the §4(b) coverage formula. [M]
- **Other powers:** bankruptcy, patents/copyright, post office, District of Columbia and territories, investigations/contempt in aid of legislation (*McGrain v. Daugherty*). Limits on Congress (Art. I, §9): no bill of attainder, no ex post facto law, habeas suspension only in rebellion/invasion, no export taxes, no titles of nobility. [M]
- **Recent:** *Learning Resources v. Trump* (2026): IEEPA does not authorize tariffs; tariff/tax power is Congress's (6-3). [S]

### 6.2 Powers of the President (outline II.B)
- **Chief executive / "Take Care":** executive power vested in the President (Art. II, §1); the President must faithfully execute the laws (Art. II, §3) and cannot ignore valid statutes. **Youngstown (Jackson's three zones):** (1) with congressional authorization: authority at its maximum; (2) silence: "twilight zone," concurrent/uncertain; (3) against Congress's expressed or implied will: authority at lowest ebb, valid only if Congress lacks power over the matter. The President is not a lawmaker: *Youngstown* invalidated the seizure of steel mills (Congress had considered and rejected a seizure power). [M]
- **Commander in chief:** directs military operations, but Congress funds, declares war and makes rules for the military. Detainees: *Hamdi* (citizen enemy combatant gets due process), *Hamdan* (military commissions need statutory authority), *Boumediene* (Suspension Clause reaches Guantanamo). *Korematsu* "has been overruled in the court of history" (*Trump v. Hawaii*, 2018). [M]
- **Treaty and foreign affairs:** President negotiates treaties (Senate 2/3 consent), sole recognition power (*Zivotofsky v. Kerry*, 2015), executive agreements valid in some settings (*Dames & Moore*, *Garamendi*) but cannot override a statute or the Constitution. [M]
- **Appointment:** **Appointments Clause:** principal officers by the President with Senate consent; inferior officers by the President alone, the courts of law or heads of departments **if Congress so provides**. Congress cannot appoint officers who execute the laws (*Buckley v. Valeo*). ALJs of the SEC are inferior officers needing proper appointment (*Lucia*, 2018). Recess appointments: a recess shorter than 10 days is presumptively too short; pro forma sessions count as sessions (*NLRB v. Noel Canning*). [M]
- **Removal:** President may remove executive officers; Congress cannot reserve removal for itself (*Bowsher v. Synar*; *Myers*). Congress may limit removal of **inferior** officers (*Morrison v. Olson*). *Free Enterprise Fund* (2010): two layers of for-cause protection unconstitutional. *Seila Law* (2020): single-director CFPB with for-cause protection invalid. **2026 change: *Trump v. Slaughter*** (see table: Humphrey's Executor overruled to the extent it allowed for-cause protection for multi-member bodies exercising executive power); **Federal Reserve left open**; *Trump v. Cook* (interim): process-protected for-cause. **Warning for cards:** prep books written before June 2026 say "Congress may restrict removal of members of multimember independent agencies (*Humphrey's*)." Mark that card `deprecated: true, replaced_by: Slaughter`. Expect the 2027 MBE to still be drafted on older law; the app should show both: "Old rule / Current law". [P/S]
- **Pardon:** offenses against the United States only (not state crimes), not impeachment; may be before conviction. [M]
- **Immunities (outline II.C.4):** President: absolute immunity from civil damages for official acts (*Nixon v. Fitzgerald*); **no** immunity from civil suit over unofficial pre-office conduct (*Clinton v. Jones*); executive privilege is qualified (*United States v. Nixon*: yields to criminal subpoena); state grand jury subpoena OK (*Trump v. Vance*); *Trump v. United States* (2024): absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for acts within core constitutional powers, presumptive immunity for other official acts, none for unofficial acts. Aides: qualified immunity (*Harlow*). **Legislators:** Speech or Debate Clause (Art. I, §6) protects legislative acts (votes, committee work) (*Gravel*, *Eastland*), not press releases/newsletters (*Hutchinson v. Proxmire*) nor bribery (*Brewster*). Judges: absolute immunity for judicial acts (*Stump v. Sparkman*). [M]
- **Domestic military use (recent):** *Trump v. Illinois* (Dec 2025): federalizing the Guard under 10 U.S.C. 12406(3) likely requires that the President be "unable with the regular forces [the military]" to execute the laws. Interim order; keep out of core path. [P]

### 6.3 Federal interbranch relationships (outline II.C)
- **Bicameralism and presentment** (Art. I, §7): a legislative act altering legal rights must pass both Houses and be presented to the President; one-house legislative veto invalid (*INS v. Chadha*); line-item veto invalid (*Clinton v. City of New York*). Veto override: 2/3 of each House. Pocket veto: Congress adjourned and the President does not sign within 10 days (Sundays excepted). [M]
- **Nondelegation:** Congress may delegate if it supplies an **intelligible principle** (*J.W. Hampton*; *Mistretta*; *Whitman v. American Trucking*); only two cases ever struck (1935: *Panama Refining*, *Schechter Poultry*). *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (2025) reaffirmed the test, 6-3. Separately, the **major questions doctrine** is a statutory-interpretation canon requiring clear congressional authorization for decisions of vast economic and political significance (*West Virginia v. EPA*, *Biden v. Nebraska*). *Loper Bright* (2024) ended *Chevron* deference (administrative law, relevant to NextGen "powers of agencies"). [M/S]
- **Congressional limits on executive:** control through appropriations, creating/abolishing offices, advice and consent, impeachment (non-justiciable procedure: *Nixon v. US*), oversight. Congress cannot execute the laws through its own agents (*Bowsher*, *Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority v. CAAN*). [M]

**Traps:** (1) Using "commerce clause" to justify compelled purchases (*NFIB*). (2) Applying §5 to private conduct. (3) Treating a treaty as limited by Tenth Amendment. (4) Forgetting Congress can't get around *Dole* coercion by calling it a "condition". (5) Old *Humphrey's* rule vs *Slaughter*. (6) "Executive order has force of law" - only if authorized by the Constitution or a statute. (7) Speech or Debate does not cover press releases. (8) The pocket veto trap: the President's failure to sign within 10 days becomes law if Congress is in session.

### 6.4 Own fact patterns: Separation of powers

**SP-1 (Raich).** Congress enacts a comprehensive regulatory scheme for the national market in a controlled substance. A provision bans home cultivation for personal use, even when the plants never leave the state, because unregulated home production could be diverted into the interstate market. A grower challenges the provision as applied to her.
(A) Valid, because Congress rationally could conclude that in the aggregate home cultivation substantially affects the interstate market.
(B) Valid, because the Tenth Amendment gives Congress police power over drugs.
(C) Invalid, because the activity is wholly intrastate.
(D) Invalid, because Congress may regulate only the channels of interstate commerce.
**Best: A** (*Raich*). C ignores aggregation. B: Congress has no general police power. D: channels is one of three categories.

**SP-2 (Morrison).** After finding that violence against women costs the economy billions, Congress creates a federal civil damages remedy against any person who commits a gender-motivated violent crime. A defendant sued under the provision challenges its constitutionality.
(A) Valid under the commerce power because Congress made extensive findings.
(B) Valid under §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment because it enforces equal protection.
(C) Invalid, because the activity is noneconomic, and Congress cannot use §5 to regulate private conduct.
(D) Invalid, because Congress cannot create private causes of action.
**Best: C** (*Morrison*). A: findings do not convert noneconomic violence into economic activity. B: §5 reaches state action. D is false.

**SP-3 (NFIB).** Congress requires adults to maintain health insurance or pay a payment to the IRS, calculated on income and collected with tax returns, producing substantial revenue. A taxpayer challenges the requirement as beyond Congress's power.
(A) Valid, as a regulation of commerce because inactivity affects the insurance market.
(B) Valid, as an exercise of the taxing power, because the payment functions as a tax.
(C) Invalid, because Congress cannot tax inactivity.
(D) Invalid, because the Necessary and Proper Clause forbids compelled purchases.
**Best: B** (*NFIB*). A is what the Court rejected. C and D misstate the taxing holding.

**SP-4 (Dole).** To reduce traffic deaths, Congress directs that 5% of a state's federal highway funds be withheld unless the state sets 21 as the minimum drinking age. A state that wants 18 argues the Twenty-first Amendment reserves liquor regulation to the states.
(A) The condition is valid, because it is unambiguous, related to highway safety, in pursuit of the general welfare, and not unduly coercive.
(B) Invalid, because Congress may not regulate liquor.
(C) Invalid, because the Tenth Amendment forbids conditions on federal grants.
(D) Valid only if the funds are unrelated to the condition.
**Best: A** (*Dole*). D reverses the relatedness test.

**SP-5 (13th Amendment).** Congress enacts a statute that forbids any person from refusing, on the basis of race, to sell real property, and creates a damages remedy. A private seller challenges it.
(A) Invalid, because the Fourteenth Amendment reaches only state action.
(B) Valid, as an exercise of Congress's power to eliminate the badges and incidents of slavery under the Thirteenth Amendment.
(C) Invalid, because land sales are local.
(D) Valid only if the seller operates an interstate business.
**Best: B** (*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer*). A is true but irrelevant; the 13th Amendment reaches private conduct.

**SP-6 (Boerne).** Citing §5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, Congress enacts a statute providing that no state or local law may substantially burden the exercise of religion unless it satisfies strict scrutiny. A city's zoning board denies a church a permit and relies on the Constitution's limits on §5 power.
(A) The statute is valid, because Congress defines the content of Fourteenth Amendment rights.
(B) Invalid as applied to the city, because the statute is not congruent and proportional to any pattern of unconstitutional state action; it changes rather than enforces the Free Exercise right.
(C) Invalid, because Congress has no power over zoning.
(D) Valid, because Congress's spending power supports it.
**Best: B** (*City of Boerne*). D: nothing in the facts invokes spending.

**SP-7 (treaty).** The President, with 2/3 of the Senate, ratifies a treaty with a neighboring country to protect migratory waterfowl. Congress passes a statute implementing it, which a state argues invades powers reserved to the states by the Tenth Amendment.
(A) Valid, because the treaty power supports implementing legislation, even in areas Congress could not otherwise reach.
(B) Invalid, because only the states may regulate wildlife.
(C) Valid only if the birds travel across state lines.
(D) Invalid, because treaties require House approval.
**Best: A** (*Missouri v. Holland*). D: House has no role in treaty ratification.

**SP-8 (Youngstown).** During a nationwide strike that threatens production of military supplies, the President, without statutory authority and after Congress has considered and rejected giving him such power, orders the Secretary of Commerce to seize and run the nation's steel mills. The mill owners sue.
(A) The order is valid, because the President is commander in chief.
(B) Valid, because the President must execute the laws and protect the national interest.
(C) Invalid, because the order is not authorized by the Constitution or Congress, and Congress had rejected such power.
(D) Invalid, because it presents a political question.
**Best: C** (*Youngstown*). D: the Court decided it.

**SP-9 (Buckley appointments).** Congress creates a commission with power to bring enforcement actions in court and issue binding rules. Its six voting members are chosen as follows: two by the President pro tempore of the Senate, two by the Speaker of the House, and two by the President, each subject to confirmation by both Houses.
(A) Valid, because Congress can create agencies as it chooses.
(B) Invalid, because officers exercising executive power must be appointed according to the Appointments Clause, and legislative leaders cannot appoint them.
(C) Valid, because the President appoints some members.
(D) Invalid, because the commission's rules need presidential approval.
**Best: B** (*Buckley*). C does not cure the defect.

**SP-10 (Bowsher).** Congress gives the Comptroller General, an officer removable by joint resolution of Congress, power to determine budget cuts that the President must then carry out.
(A) Valid, because Congress may delegate to its own officer.
(B) Valid, because budget decisions are a core legislative power.
(C) Invalid, because Congress cannot retain removal control over an officer who executes the laws.
(D) Invalid, because only the President may propose budgets.
**Best: C** (*Bowsher*).

**SP-11 (Chadha; Humphrey's flag).** Statute authorizes the Attorney General to suspend deportation of certain noncitizens, but allows either House of Congress, by simple resolution, to veto each suspension.
(A) Valid, because Congress delegated narrowly and can recapture power.
(B) Invalid, because it is a legislative act with the force of law taken without bicameral passage and presentment.
(C) Invalid, because immigration is exclusively executive.
(D) Valid, because immigration is plenary in Congress.
**Best: B** (*Chadha*).

**SP-12 (Slaughter: current law).** A federal statute creates a five-member agency enforcing a consumer-protection law, with rulemaking, adjudication and civil enforcement powers. Members serve fixed terms and are removable by the President only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance." The President removes a member solely because of a policy disagreement. The member sues.
(A) The removal is unlawful, because *Humphrey's Executor* protects multimember independent agencies. *(Pre-2026 answer.)*
(B) The removal is lawful, because under *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) statutory limits on the President's power to remove officers of an agency exercising executive power violate Article II.
(C) The removal is unlawful, because the Senate must consent to removal.
(D) Nonjusticiable.
**Best (current law): B. Flag in app: `recent`, pre-June 2026 outlines say A.** Do not include as a core scored card until it is likely on the exam; use the "Old rule / Current law" two-step.

**SP-13 (Clinton v. Jones).** Before taking office, a person made defamatory statements about a private individual in a private conversation. After becoming President, the defendant is sued for damages in federal court. He asserts presidential immunity.
(A) The suit must be dismissed, because the President has absolute immunity from all civil suits while in office.
(B) May proceed, because there is no immunity from civil suit for unofficial acts done before taking office.
(C) Must be postponed, because separation of powers always requires a stay.
(D) May proceed only in state court.
**Best: B** (*Clinton v. Jones*; contrast *Nixon v. Fitzgerald* for official acts).

**SP-14 (Speech or Debate).** A senator issues a press release repeating defamatory statements that she made on the Senate floor. The target sues her for defamation over the press release.
(A) Barred, because Speech or Debate protects everything a legislator says.
(B) Barred, because it republishes protected statements.
(C) Not barred, because the press release is not part of the legislative process.
(D) Barred only if the Senate consents.
**Best: C** (*Hutchinson v. Proxmire*). The floor speech itself is absolutely protected.

**SP-15 (nondelegation).** Congress directs the EPA to set national ambient air-quality standards "requisite to protect the public health" with "an adequate margin of safety." A trade group says the delegation is unconstitutional.
(A) Valid, because the statute supplies an intelligible principle.
(B) Invalid, because Congress may never delegate rulemaking power.
(C) Invalid, because costs must be considered.
(D) Valid only if the President approves each standard.
**Best: A** (*Whitman*).

## 7. III. THE RELATION OF NATION AND STATES (MBE category III)

### 7.1 Intergovernmental immunities (outline III.A)
- **Federal immunity from state law:** a State may not tax or regulate the federal government directly (*McCulloch*), and may not **discriminate** against the federal government, its employees or its contractors (*Dawson v. Steager*, 2019; *United States v. Washington*, 2022: state workers' compensation rule applying only to federal-site workers invalid). Nondiscriminatory taxes/regulation of private parties who deal with the federal government are generally valid (*North Dakota v. United States*; *Graves v. New York ex rel. O'Keefe*). Federal officers acting under federal authority and doing what is "necessary and proper" are immune from state prosecution (*In re Neagle*, Supremacy Clause immunity). [M]
- **State immunity from federal law / Tenth Amendment (anticommandeering):** Congress may regulate **private actors** and may regulate states under generally applicable laws (*Garcia v. SAMTA*, overruling *National League of Cities*; *Reno v. Condon*; *South Carolina v. Baker*), but may **not commandeer** state legislatures or executives to enact or enforce federal programs (*New York v. United States*: take-title provision; *Printz v. United States*: background-check duty for local sheriffs; *Murphy v. NCAA*, 2018: a federal ban on state authorization of sports gambling regulates the state, not private actors). Conditions on **spending** are allowed within *Dole* (not coercive). Federal law may require state courts to hear federal claims on the same terms as analogous state claims (*Testa v. Katt*). *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023): ICWA placement preferences regulate state agencies only incidentally to regulating private custody and were not commandeering; the case was resolved partly on standing. Clear statement rule for federal laws that alter the federal-state balance (*Gregory v. Ashcroft*). [M]
- **State sovereign immunity** is in §5.2. [M]

### 7.2 Federalism-based limits on state authority (outline III.B)
**A. Dormant (negative) commerce clause** - the Commerce Clause implicitly restricts state laws that burden interstate commerce when Congress has not acted.
1. **Discrimination** (facial, purpose or effect): **virtually per se invalid**; upheld only if necessary to achieve an important local purpose that cannot be served by nondiscriminatory alternatives (*Philadelphia v. New Jersey*: ban on out-of-state landfill waste; *Hunt v. Washington Apple*; *Dean Milk*; *C & A Carbone v. Clarkstown*: flow-control to a private facility invalid; *United Haulers*: flow control to a public facility valid; *Tennessee Wine & Spirits v. Thomas*, 2019: durational residency requirement for liquor retailers invalid, 21st Amendment does not save it; *Granholm v. Heald*). [M]
2. **Nondiscriminatory but burdensome:** *Pike v. Bruce Church* balancing: valid unless the burden on interstate commerce is **clearly excessive** relative to the putative local benefits (*Kassel*, *Bibb*). *National Pork Producers v. Ross* (2023): the Court unanimously rejected an "almost per se" extraterritoriality rule; fractured on how *Pike* applies; a state law is not invalid merely because it has out-of-state effects (California's Prop 12 upheld). [M/S]
3. **Exceptions:** (a) **Market participant:** a state buying or selling goods or services in the market may prefer its own citizens (*Reeves v. Stake*; *White v. Massachusetts Council of Constr. Employers*: city contracts require employing city residents), but not impose conditions that reach downstream, beyond the market it participates in (*South-Central Timber v. Wunnicke*). (b) **Congressional authorization:** Congress may authorize state laws that would otherwise violate the dormant clause if its intent is clear (*Western & Southern Life v. Board of Equalization*; *Prudential v. Benjamin*, McCarran-Ferguson). **But Congress cannot authorize states to violate individual rights** (equal protection, *Shapiro v. Thompson* context). [M]
4. **State taxes:** *Complete Auto Transit* four parts: substantial nexus, fair apportionment, no discrimination, fair relation to services provided. *South Dakota v. Wayfair* (2018): physical presence not required (economic nexus). [M]

**B. Privileges and Immunities Clause of Art. IV, §2** - protects **citizens of other states** from discrimination regarding fundamental rights and important economic interests (livelihood); applies only to **natural persons who are US citizens** (not corporations or aliens). Test: discrimination permitted only if there is a substantial reason and the discrimination bears a substantial relation to it (*Supreme Court of New Hampshire v. Piper*: nonresident attorney admission; *Hicklin v. Orbeck*; *Toomer v. Witsell*; but *Baldwin v. Fish & Game Comm'n*: recreational elk hunting not fundamental). **14th Amendment P&I Clause** protects only the rights of **national** citizenship (*Slaughter-House Cases*); *Saenz v. Roe*: a new resident is entitled to the same benefits as long-term residents (right to travel). Right to travel interstate is fundamental (strict scrutiny): *Shapiro*, *Dunn v. Blumstein*, *Memorial Hospital*. [M]

**C. Supremacy and preemption (Art. VI)** - federal law (Constitution, valid statutes, treaties, valid regulations) prevails over conflicting state law.
- **Express preemption** by text; **field preemption** (federal scheme so pervasive/dominant: *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator*; *Arizona v. United States*, 2012: alien registration field); **conflict preemption**: impossibility (*Florida Lime*; *PLIVA v. Mensing*, generic drugs) or **obstacle** (*Hines v. Davidowitz*; *Geier*). [M]
- **Presumption against preemption** in fields traditionally occupied by the states (health, safety) (*Medtronic v. Lohr*; *Wyeth v. Levine*: brand-name drug failure-to-warn claims not preempted); express clause is read narrowly (*Puerto Rico v. Franklin California Tax-Free Trust*). Federal regulations preempt too (*Fidelity Federal v. de la Cuesta*). Preemption provisions must regulate private conduct, not commandeer (*Murphy*). [M]
- **Trap pair:** brand-name (*Wyeth*: not preempted) vs generic (*Mensing*, *Bartlett*: preempted because of the duty of sameness).

### 7.3 Own fact patterns: Nation and states

**FED-1 (Printz).** Congress requires each county's chief law-enforcement officer to run background checks on handgun applicants until a national system is available.
(A) Valid under the commerce power.
(B) Valid, because the federal government may command state officials in matters affecting commerce.
(C) Invalid, because Congress may not commandeer state officers to administer a federal regulatory program.
(D) Invalid, because Congress may not regulate firearms.
**Best: C** (*Printz*).

**FED-2 (Murphy).** Congress enacts two provisions: one prohibits private individuals from running sports lotteries; another prohibits any state from authorizing sports gambling by law.
(A) Both valid under the commerce power.
(B) Only the provision aimed at private individuals is valid; the one aimed at states unconstitutionally commandeers state legislative power.
(C) Both invalid under the Tenth Amendment.
(D) Only the provision directed at states is valid.
**Best: B** (*Murphy v. NCAA*).

**FED-3 (Condon).** Congress forbids both state motor-vehicle departments and private resellers from disclosing drivers' personal information obtained from DMV records. The State sues, arguing a Tenth Amendment violation.
(A) Valid, because the statute regulates the State as a participant in interstate commerce along with private actors and does not commandeer it to administer a federal program.
(B) Invalid, because it regulates states.
(C) Invalid, because personal information is not commerce.
(D) Valid, because the Tenth Amendment does not apply to the federal commerce power.
**Best: A** (*Reno v. Condon*). B is the overbroad Tenth Amendment trap. D overstates.

**FED-4 (intergovernmental).** A state imposes a workers' compensation rule that applies only to employees at a federal facility, and makes federal contractors, but not other employers, bear a special cost.
(A) Valid, because states may regulate within their territory.
(B) Invalid, because it discriminates against the federal government and those it deals with.
(C) Valid, because contractors are private.
(D) Invalid only if Congress has legislated on workers' compensation.
**Best: B** (*United States v. Washington*, 2022). D adds a preemption requirement discrimination doctrine does not have.

**FED-5 (discrimination).** A state bans importing solid waste from other states into its landfills, stating a goal of conserving landfill space and protecting the environment. Congress has not legislated on the subject.
(A) Valid, because it protects health and the environment.
(B) Valid, because states control their own land.
(C) Invalid, because it discriminates against interstate commerce and there are nondiscriminatory alternatives (such as limiting total volume).
(D) Invalid, because only Congress may regulate waste.
**Best: C** (*Philadelphia v. New Jersey*).

**FED-6 (Pike).** A state requires every truck to have a particular contoured mudguard that no other state requires. The rule applies equally to in-state and out-of-state trucks; evidence shows the safety benefit is trivial, and trucks must stop at the border to swap guards, imposing large costs.
(A) Valid, because it is nondiscriminatory.
(B) Valid, because states may regulate highway safety.
(C) Invalid, because the burden on interstate commerce is clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits.
(D) Invalid, because states cannot regulate trucks.
**Best: C** (*Bibb*; *Pike*).

**FED-7 (market participant).** A state owns and operates a cement plant. During a shortage, it sells cement only to state residents.
(A) Invalid under the dormant commerce clause, because it favors in-state buyers.
(B) Valid, because the state is acting as a market participant.
(C) Invalid under the Equal Protection Clause, because out-of-state buyers are a suspect class.
(D) Valid only if Congress has authorized it.
**Best: B** (*Reeves*). A is the trap: the dormant commerce clause does not apply to a state acting as a market participant. C: nonresidency is not a suspect class. D: no congressional authorization is needed. (Note for authors: the Art. IV Privileges and Immunities Clause is *not* displaced by the market participant doctrine - *Camden*; do not write a card that pits the two without making that clear.)

**FED-8 (P&I).** A state limits admission to its bar to state residents.
(A) Valid, because states control law practice.
(B) Valid, because nonresidents may practice in their home state.
(C) Invalid under the Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article IV, because it discriminates against nonresident citizens in the pursuit of livelihood without a substantial reason.
(D) Invalid under the Equal Protection Clause because residence is a suspect class.
**Best: C** (*Piper*). D: residency is not a suspect classification.

**FED-9 (preemption: obstacle).** Federal law authorizes national banks to sell insurance in towns with fewer than 5,000 people. A state law forbids national banks to sell insurance anywhere in the state.
(A) Valid, because states regulate insurance.
(B) Invalid, because Congress has exclusive power over banking.
(C) Invalid, because it stands as an obstacle/conflicts with the federal grant of authority.
(D) Valid, because there is a presumption against preemption.
**Best: C** (*Barnett Bank v. Nelson*). The presumption is overcome where federal law expressly grants a power the state law forbids.

**FED-10 (Wyeth).** A consumer sues the manufacturer of an FDA-approved brand-name drug under state tort law for failure to warn, claiming the manufacturer could have strengthened the warning without prior FDA approval.
(A) Preempted, because FDA approved the label.
(B) Not preempted, because the manufacturer could have strengthened the warning under federal regulation, and there is a presumption against preemption of state tort law.
(C) Preempted, because drug safety is a field occupied by federal law.
(D) Preempted only if the drug is generic.
**Best: B** (*Wyeth v. Levine*). D is a trap: generic manufacturers are preempted in design-defect/failure-to-warn claims where they cannot change labels (*Mensing*). Under the app: show D as "partially true statement attached to the wrong claim".

## 8. IV. INDIVIDUAL RIGHTS, PART 1: state action, due process, equal protection, takings, other protections (about half of all Con Law questions are category IV)

### 8.1 State action (outline IV.A)
Rule: the Constitution (14th Amendment, 1st Amendment as incorporated, etc.) restrains **government** only. Exceptions where private conduct is attributed to the state: **(1) public function:** a private entity exercising a power traditionally and **exclusively** reserved to the state (company town: *Marsh v. Alabama*; running a primary election: *Terry v. Adams*, *Smith v. Allwright*). Narrow: not a shopping center (*Hudgens*), private utility (*Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison*), private school for troubled students (*Rendell-Baker*), private operator of public-access cable channels (*Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck*, 2019). **(2) Entanglement/significant state involvement:** judicial enforcement of racially restrictive covenants (*Shelley v. Kraemer*), mutually beneficial lease of space in a public parking garage (*Burton*), state officials entwined with a nominally private athletic association (*Brentwood Academy*), private party using state attachment procedures (*Lugar*). Not enough: licensing/regulation/funding alone (*Moose Lodge*, *Blum v. Yaretsky*, *Jackson*). **Officials' personal accounts:** state action only if the official had actual authority to speak for the state and purported to exercise it (*Lindke v. Freed*, 2024). **No affirmative duty to protect:** *DeShaney v. Winnebago County* (no duty absent custody). **Private conduct can still be reached by Congress** under the commerce power and the 13th Amendment. Under NextGen: "exclusive government functions" exception named. [M]

### 8.2 Due process (outline IV.B)
**Substantive due process (SDP):** "No State shall deprive any person of life, liberty, or property without due process" (14th; federal govt via 5th).
- **Fundamental rights → strict scrutiny:** government must show the law is **necessary to achieve a compelling interest**. Fundamental rights under SDP: those "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and carefully described (*Washington v. Glucksberg*): marry (*Loving*; *Zablocki v. Redhail*; *Obergefell*), procreation/contraception (*Skinner*; *Griswold*; *Eisenstadt*), direct upbringing and education of children (*Meyer*, *Pierce*, *Troxel*), keep family together (*Moore v. East Cleveland*), refuse medical treatment (*Cruzan*), bodily integrity, intimate relations (*Lawrence v. Texas*), interstate travel, **vote** (usually analyzed under EP), **bear arms** (*Heller*, *McDonald*; *Bruen*; *Rahimi*; *Wolford*, 2026). **Abortion is not a fundamental right** (*Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Org.*, 2022: *Roe* and *Casey* overruled; abortion regulations get **rational-basis** review). No fundamental right to assisted suicide (*Glucksberg*, *Vacco v. Quill*). [M]
- **Other rights and interests (non-fundamental):** economic and social legislation gets **rational-basis** review: legitimate government purpose + rationally related; challenger bears the burden (*Williamson v. Lee Optical*; *Ferguson v. Skrupa*; *Lochner* is dead). Punitive damages can violate due process if grossly excessive (*BMW v. Gore*; *State Farm*). [M]
- **Second Amendment test (*Bruen*, as refined by *Rahimi*, *Wolford*, *Hemani*):** (1) does the plain text cover the conduct ("the people", "keep"/"bear", "Arms")? If so, presumptively protected; (2) the government must show the regulation is consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation by "relevantly similar" historical analogues (*how* and *why*); no means-end interest balancing. Applies equally to the states (*McDonald*). Recent: *Wolford* (private-property default rule invalid), *Hemani* (as applied to marijuana user, 922(g)(3)). Flag: lower courts' work on 922(g) and "unusually dangerous" arms; OT2026 AR-15 grant. [P/S]

**Procedural due process (PDP):** government must provide **notice and an opportunity to be heard at a meaningful time and manner** before depriving a person of **life, liberty or property** (requires: state action; a protected interest; a deprivation; inadequate process).
- **Property:** a legitimate claim of entitlement created by state law or rules (*Board of Regents v. Roth*, *Perry v. Sindermann*): tenured public employee has a property interest (*Cleveland Bd. of Ed. v. Loudermill*: pre-termination notice and chance to respond, full post-termination hearing); welfare benefits (*Goldberg v. Kelly*: pre-termination evidentiary hearing), but disability benefits can be terminated after paper review with a post-termination hearing (*Mathews v. Eldridge*). At-will or probationary employees without entitlement: no property interest (*Roth*). No property interest in police enforcement of a restraining order (*Town of Castle Rock v. Gonzales*). [M]
- **Liberty:** physical freedom; fundamental rights; reputation alone is not enough (*Paul v. Davis*: "stigma plus"); public school suspension of 10 days or less requires notice and an informal hearing (*Goss v. Lopez*); corporal punishment needs no pre-hearing (*Ingraham v. Wright*); prisoners (*Sandin v. Conner*, atypical and significant hardship); parole revocation (*Morrissey*). [M]
- ***Mathews* balancing:** (1) private interest affected; (2) risk of erroneous deprivation and probable value of additional safeguards; (3) government's interest, including administrative burden. [M]
- **Not a deprivation:** mere **negligence** (*Daniels v. Williams*); random unauthorized acts of state employees where an adequate post-deprivation remedy exists (*Parratt v. Taylor*; *Hudson v. Palmer*). Impartial decision-maker: a judge must recuse where the probability of actual bias is too high (*Caperton*). No automatic right to appointed counsel in civil cases (*Lassiter*; *Turner v. Rogers*); fees barring indigent access to certain fundamental proceedings (*Boddie*; *M.L.B. v. S.L.J.*). [M]
- Recent note: the 2025 Alien Enemies Act emergency-docket orders (*Trump v. J.G.G.*; *A.A.R.P. v. Trump*) required notice and a meaningful chance to seek habeas relief: `[?]` details not re-fetched; do not teach until verified.

### 8.3 Equal protection (outline IV.C)
Applies to states (14th) and, through the 5th Amendment Due Process Clause, to the federal government (*Bolling v. Sharpe*). Elements: a **classification** + **state action** + for facially neutral laws, **discriminatory purpose** (disparate impact alone is not enough: *Washington v. Davis*; *Arlington Heights*; *Personnel Adm'r v. Feeney*).
- **Strict scrutiny** (necessary to a compelling interest; burden on government): **race, national origin, ethnicity**, **alienage by states** (*Graham v. Richardson*), **fundamental rights** (voting, interstate travel, access to courts in some settings, marriage/procreation). Benign/remedial racial classifications also get strict scrutiny (*Croson*; *Adarand*). **Compelling interests accepted in the race context are very few:** remedying specific, identified past discrimination by the governmental unit itself, and avoiding imminent serious risks to prison safety; *SFFA v. Harvard* (2023) held race-conscious admissions programs invalid and reaffirmed these categories; *Callais* (2026) repeats "only two compelling interests". [P/M]
  - **Alienage exceptions:** state laws excluding aliens from jobs that are "political functions" (police, teachers, probation officers) get only rational-basis review (*Foley v. Connelie*, *Ambach v. Norwick*); Congress has plenary power over aliens (federal alienage classifications get rational basis: *Mathews v. Diaz*). *Plyler v. Doe*: states may not deny free public education to children not lawfully present (applied more than rational basis). [M]
- **Intermediate scrutiny** (substantially related to an important interest; for gender, "exceedingly persuasive justification" and no overbroad generalizations): **sex/gender** (*Craig v. Boren*; *Mississippi Univ. for Women v. Hogan*; *United States v. Virginia* (VMI); *Frontiero*; *Orr*), **nonmarital children** (*Clark v. Jeter*; *Trimble*). Male-only draft registration upheld on military deference (*Rostker*). *B.P.J./Hecox* (2026): intermediate scrutiny applied to sex-based team eligibility; transgender status as a class not decided; *Skrmetti* (2025): age/medical-use classification gets rational basis. [P/S]
- **Rational basis** (legitimate purpose; rationally related; any conceivable basis; challenger bears burden; under- and over-inclusion tolerated): everything else, including **age** (*Murgia*), **wealth** (*San Antonio v. Rodriguez*: no fundamental right to education), **disability** (*Cleburne*), economic regulation (*Railway Express*, *New Orleans v. Dukes*, *FCC v. Beach*), sexual orientation as such (the Court has struck laws based on **animus** under rational basis: *Romer*, *Moreno*, *Windsor*; marriage in *Obergefell* rests on both DP and EP). "Rational basis with bite" arises where the only purpose is animus (*Cleburne*, *Romer*). [M]
- **Voting and districting:** poll taxes invalid (*Harper*); denial of the vote to non-property owners (*Kramer*); ballot access burdens: *Anderson-Burdick* sliding scale; voter ID upheld (*Crawford*); **one person one vote**: congressional districts nearly mathematically equal (*Wesberry*, *Karcher*); state legislative districts within about 10% total deviation presumptively valid (*Reynolds*, *Brown v. Thomson*) (population base: total population, *Evenwel*). **Racial gerrymandering:** race the predominant factor → strict scrutiny (*Shaw v. Reno*; *Miller v. Johnson*; *Cooper v. Harris*); partisan gerrymanders nonjusticiable (*Rucho*); *Callais* (2026) above. [M/P]
- Felon disenfranchisement allowed (14th Amend. §2: *Richardson v. Ramirez*). [M]

### 8.4 Takings (outline IV.D)
5th Amendment (applied to states through 14th): private property shall not be taken for **public use** without **just compensation** (fair market value at the time of taking).
- **Public use** is broad: any rational conceivable public purpose, including economic development (*Kelo*; *Midkiff*). [M]
- **Per se takings:** **permanent physical occupation or appropriation** (*Loretto*: cable box; *Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid*, 2021: union access easement; *Horne*: raisins - personal property; *Tyler v. Hennepin County*, 2023: county kept equity surplus from tax foreclosure); **total loss of all economically beneficial use** (*Lucas*), unless background principles of state property/nuisance law already forbid the use. [M]
- **Penn Central** balancing for other regulatory takings: (1) economic impact; (2) interference with distinct investment-backed expectations; (3) character of the government action. Landmark designation not a taking. Temporary moratoria are analyzed under *Penn Central* (*Tahoe-Sierra*). The "parcel as a whole" (*Murr*). Later purchaser can still claim (*Palazzolo*). [M]
- **Exactions:** conditioning a permit on dedication of property requires an **essential nexus** and **rough proportionality** (*Nollan*; *Dolan*); also applies to monetary exactions (*Koontz*) and to **legislatively imposed** conditions (*Sheetz v. County of El Dorado*, 2024, unanimous). [M/S]
- **Remedy/access:** property owner may sue in federal court immediately under §1983 for a taking by a local government; no exhaustion of state remedies (*Knick*, 2019). Temporary takings need compensation (*First English*). *Devillier v. Texas* (2024) `[?]`: states may require takings claims to proceed through available state-law causes of action; verify holding wording before card. [M/?]
- Not takings: nuisance abatement; ordinary zoning. [M]

### 8.5 Other protections (outline IV.E)
- **Contracts Clause (Art. I, §10, states only):** no state law "impairing the obligation of contracts." Test: (1) does the law substantially impair a contractual relationship? (2) if so, is it a reasonable and necessary means of serving a significant and legitimate public purpose? Private contracts: deference to legislature (*Energy Reserves Group v. Kansas Power & Light*; *Sveen v. Melin*, 2018); **the State's own contracts**: no deference when state's financial self-interest (*United States Trust Co. v. New Jersey*); emergency measures can be valid (*Home Building & Loan v. Blaisdell*). Does not apply to federal laws (federal analog: Due Process/Takings), nor to prospective contracts. [M]
- **Bills of attainder (Art. I, §§9-10):** legislative act that **inflicts punishment on an identifiable individual or group without a judicial trial** (*Cummings*, *Ex parte Garland*, *United States v. Brown*: ban on Communist Party members serving as union officers). Punishment tests: historical, functional (non-punitive purposes), motivational. *Nixon v. Administrator of General Services* (special statute about Nixon papers not attainder); *Selective Service System v. Minnesota PIRG*. [M]
- **Ex post facto (Art. I, §§9-10):** applies only to **criminal** laws (*Calder v. Bull*) that (1) criminalize conduct innocent when done, (2) increase the punishment, (3) aggravate a crime, or (4) alter evidence rules to lower the proof needed to convict (*Carmell*). Extension of an *unexpired* limitations period valid; revival of an *expired* one invalid (*Stogner v. California*). Civil sex-offender registration not punishment (*Smith v. Doe*). **Ellingburg v. United States (2026):** MVRA restitution is criminal punishment for the Ex Post Facto Clause. Retroactive civil laws only face DP rational basis (*Landgraf* presumption is statutory). [M/S]
- **Unconstitutional conditions:** government cannot condition a public benefit/job on waiving a constitutional right if it could not directly demand that waiver (*Perry v. Sindermann*, *Speiser v. Randall*, *Koontz*; *AOSI* (2013): funding conditions that leverage the program to regulate speech outside it invalid; *Rust v. Sullivan*: government may define the program it funds). [M]
- **Privileges and immunities:** see §7.2.B.

### 8.6 Traps for category IV
(1) Treating "private person" actions as state action without an exception. (2) Calling abortion a fundamental right after *Dobbs*; the best answer is rational basis. (3) Using strict scrutiny for a facially neutral law with only disparate impact. (4) Applying *Lemon* in an Establishment question (see 9.4). (5) Alienage: strict scrutiny for state laws, except political functions. (6) Gender is intermediate, not rational basis, even when the law "favors" women. (7) *Penn Central* vs *Loretto*/*Lucas* mix-up: physical occupation and total wipe-out are per se; everything else balances. (8) Using Contracts Clause against federal law. (9) Ex post facto is only criminal. (10) State officials' negligence is not a due process "deprivation."

### 8.7 Own fact patterns: State action, due process, equal protection, takings, other protections

**SA-1.** A privately owned shopping mall posts a no-leafleting rule and has security guards eject a group distributing political leaflets in the common areas. The group sues for violation of the First Amendment.
(A) Mall owner violated the group's rights because the mall functions as a public square.
(B) No violation, because the mall owner is a private party and is not performing an exclusive public function.
(C) Violation, because the mall holds itself open to the public.
(D) No violation only if the leaflets were commercial.
**Best: B** (*Hudgens*). A and C are the *Marsh* trap.

**SA-2.** A corporation owns an entire town, including its streets, sidewalks and shops, and operates it like any municipality. It arrests a resident who distributes religious literature on the sidewalk.
(A) No First Amendment issue, because the corporation is private.
(B) The corporation's action is state action because it performs the functions of a municipality.
(C) State action only if the state licensed the corporation.
(D) No state action, because the residents agreed to rules.
**Best: B** (*Marsh*).

**SA-3 (Lindke).** A city manager keeps a personal Facebook page, posts mostly family photos, but occasionally posts about city projects and sometimes deletes critical comments. A resident whose comments were deleted sues under the First Amendment.
(A) State action exists, because the manager is a public official.
(B) State action exists only if the manager had actual authority to speak for the city on the subject and purported to exercise that authority in the posts at issue.
(C) No state action, because social media is private property.
(D) State action exists whenever a government job is mentioned on the page.
**Best: B** (*Lindke v. Freed*).

**SA-4 (DeShaney).** County social workers received repeated reports that a child's father was beating the child, took no action, and the father later beat the child into permanent disability. The child sues the county under the Due Process Clause.
(A) Liable, because the state knew of the danger.
(B) Not liable, because the Due Process Clause does not generally impose an affirmative duty on the state to protect individuals from private violence when the child was not in state custody.
(C) Liable, because the county created the danger by being an agency of the state.
(D) Not liable only if the social workers acted in good faith.
**Best: B** (*DeShaney*).

**SDP-1 (Dobbs).** A state enacts a statute banning abortions after 15 weeks, except for medical emergencies, and states a purpose of protecting fetal life. A clinic challenges it under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause.
(A) Invalid under *Roe v. Wade*'s trimester framework.
(B) Invalid under the "undue burden" standard.
(C) Valid, because abortion is not a fundamental right and the law is rationally related to legitimate state interests.
(D) Valid only if it survives strict scrutiny.
**Best: C** (*Dobbs*, 2022). A, B, D are pre-*Dobbs* or wrong standards.

**SDP-2 (Pierce).** A state requires all children aged 8-16 to attend public schools. A religious parents' group challenges the law.
(A) Valid, because states may regulate education.
(B) Invalid, because it unreasonably interferes with the fundamental right of parents to direct their children's education.
(C) Valid, because there is no fundamental right regarding education.
(D) Invalid, but only as to religious schools.
**Best: B** (*Pierce*). C confuses a right to receive education (*Rodriguez*) with the parental right.

**SDP-3 (Moore).** A city zoning ordinance limits single-family housing occupancy to a nuclear family. A grandmother who lives with two grandsons who are cousins is cited.
(A) Valid, because zoning is a local matter.
(B) Valid, because rational basis review applies to zoning.
(C) Invalid, because the ordinance intrudes on the fundamental right of extended family members to live together and cannot survive heightened scrutiny.
(D) Invalid, because the grandmother is a suspect class.
**Best: C** (*Moore v. East Cleveland*). Contrast *Belle Terre* (unrelated persons; upheld).

**SDP-4 (Glucksberg).** A terminally ill adult asks a court to hold unconstitutional a state ban on physician-assisted suicide.
(A) Invalid, because the right to die is part of liberty.
(B) Invalid under equal protection, because the state permits withdrawal of life support.
(C) Valid, because there is no fundamental right to assisted suicide and the ban is rationally related to legitimate state interests.
(D) Valid only if the state shows a compelling interest.
**Best: C** (*Glucksberg*; *Vacco*).

**SDP-5 (economic).** A state law forbids anyone but licensed optometrists from fitting lenses to a face without a written prescription, even to duplicate lenses. Opticians challenge the law under the Due Process Clause.
(A) Invalid, because it burdens economic liberty.
(B) Invalid, because the legislature could have used less restrictive means.
(C) Valid if the legislature could rationally have believed it advances health interests.
(D) Valid only if the state proves a compelling health interest.
**Best: C** (*Lee Optical*).

**SDP-6 (Zablocki).** A state forbids any resident with unpaid child support from marrying.
(A) Valid, since marriage is a state-regulated privilege.
(B) Valid, if rationally related to collecting support.
(C) Invalid unless necessary (narrowly tailored) to serve a compelling interest, because it significantly interferes with the fundamental right to marry.
(D) Invalid, because poverty is a suspect class.
**Best: C** (*Zablocki*). D: wealth is not suspect.

**SDP-7 (Bruen).** A state allows residents to carry handguns in public only after proving a special need to the licensing official.
(A) Valid, if substantially related to public safety under intermediate scrutiny.
(B) Valid, because states may regulate firearms.
(C) Invalid, because the regulated conduct is covered by the Second Amendment's plain text and the state cannot show a historical tradition of comparable restriction.
(D) Invalid under rational basis.
**Best: C** (*Bruen*). A: interest balancing was rejected.

**PDP-1 (Loudermill).** A state hospital fires a tenured nurse for cause, after sending notice that she is dismissed, effective immediately; a hearing is available in three months.
(A) No violation, because a post-termination hearing exists.
(B) Violation, because she is entitled to some form of pre-termination notice and opportunity to respond.
(C) No violation, because public employment is a privilege.
(D) Violation, because she must receive a full adversarial trial before termination.
**Best: B** (*Loudermill*). D overstates; A understates.

**PDP-2 (Mathews).** A federal agency terminates a worker's Social Security disability benefits after a paper review of medical records, offering a full evidentiary hearing after termination.
(A) Due process requires a pre-termination oral hearing, as for welfare.
(B) Due process is satisfied, based on balancing the private interest, the risk of error, and the government's interest.
(C) No due process applies to federal benefits.
(D) Due process is violated unless counsel is appointed.
**Best: B** (*Mathews v. Eldridge*). A is the *Goldberg* trap.

**PDP-3 (Roth).** A state college hires an assistant professor for a one-year term with no tenure or expectation of renewal. It declines to rehire her without giving reasons or a hearing.
(A) Due process violated, because she lost a job.
(B) No violation, because she had no property interest in renewal and the state did not stigmatize her.
(C) Violation, because all public employees have property rights.
(D) No violation only if she was paid through the term.
**Best: B** (*Roth*).

**PDP-4 (Daniels).** A prison guard negligently leaves a pillow on a stairway and an inmate slips and is injured.
(A) Violation, because the guard acted under color of law.
(B) No due process violation, because negligence does not "deprive" a person of liberty or property.
(C) Violation, because the prison is liable for all injuries.
(D) No violation, because inmates have no rights.
**Best: B** (*Daniels v. Williams*).

**EP-1 (Washington v. Davis).** A city fire department's written entrance exam is passed by far fewer applicants from one racial group. There is no evidence the exam was designed or used with discriminatory purpose, and it is job-related.
(A) Violates equal protection because of disparate impact.
(B) Does not violate equal protection absent proof of discriminatory purpose.
(C) Violates, because race-based effects trigger strict scrutiny.
(D) Does not violate, because the Constitution does not reach employment.
**Best: B.** (Title VII has a different, impact-based standard; do not mix.)

**EP-2 (alienage).** A state denies public assistance benefits to lawful permanent residents who are not citizens.
(A) Valid under rational basis.
(B) Valid because Congress controls immigration.
(C) Invalid unless the state shows the classification is necessary to a compelling interest; none is shown.
(D) Valid because aliens are not "persons."
**Best: C** (*Graham v. Richardson*). D: aliens are "persons."

**EP-3 (political function).** A state requires all state police officers to be U.S. citizens.
(A) Invalid, because alienage classifications get strict scrutiny.
(B) Valid if rationally related to a legitimate interest, since police officers perform a political function.
(C) Invalid, because Congress occupies the field.
(D) Valid only if there is a compelling interest.
**Best: B** (*Foley v. Connelie*).

**EP-4 (Hogan).** A state-supported nursing school admits only women.
(A) Valid because women historically dominate nursing and the policy compensates for discrimination.
(B) Invalid unless the state shows an exceedingly persuasive justification, which it cannot.
(C) Valid under rational basis.
(D) Invalid, because gender is a suspect class under strict scrutiny.
**Best: B** (*Mississippi University for Women v. Hogan*). D: gender is intermediate scrutiny.

**EP-5 (Dukes).** A city bans pushcart food vendors in its historic district but grandfathers vendors who have operated there for at least eight years. A newer vendor challenges the exception.
(A) Invalid, because it treats similar vendors differently.
(B) Valid, if rationally related to a legitimate interest, such as preserving the district's character while protecting reliance interests.
(C) Invalid, because economic regulation requires intermediate scrutiny.
(D) Valid only if the city proves a compelling interest.
**Best: B** (*New Orleans v. Dukes*).

**EP-6 (Cleburne).** A city requires a special-use permit for homes for people with intellectual disabilities but not for boarding houses or apartment buildings. The city's stated reasons rest on neighbors' fears.
(A) Valid under rational basis.
(B) Invalid under strict scrutiny, because disability is a suspect class.
(C) Invalid under rational basis, because the permit requirement rests on irrational prejudice.
(D) Invalid under intermediate scrutiny.
**Best: C** (*Cleburne*). B and D misidentify the standard.

**EP-7 (SFFA).** A public university considers an applicant's race as one factor, among others, in holistic admissions to obtain educational benefits of diversity.
(A) Valid under *Grutter*.
(B) Valid under rational basis.
(C) Invalid, because race-conscious admissions cannot satisfy strict scrutiny.
(D) Valid, because race is only one factor.
**Best: C** (*Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard*, 2023).

**EP-8 (Reynolds).** The apportionment for a state's lower house has district populations varying by 30%.
(A) Valid, because states may weigh geography.
(B) Valid, because the Senate model applies.
(C) Invalid unless the state shows legitimate justification, since districts must be roughly equal in population.
(D) Nonjusticiable.
**Best: C** (*Reynolds*; *Brown v. Thomson* ≤10% presumptively valid).

**EP-9 (Harper).** A state requires voters to pay a $2 fee to vote in state elections.
(A) Valid, since the amount is modest.
(B) Valid under rational basis because voting isn't mentioned in the Constitution.
(C) Invalid, since a poll tax burdens a fundamental right and is not necessary to a compelling interest.
(D) Valid if fee proceeds fund elections.
**Best: C** (*Harper*).

**EP-10 (Callais; recent).** After a census, a state legislature draws a congressional district so that Black voters are 55% of the voting-age population, placing race as the predominant factor. The state says the district is needed to avoid VRA liability.
(A) The district is subject to rational basis.
(B) Strict scrutiny applies; the district is valid only if the state had a compelling interest, and, after *Callais*, VRA §2 does not supply that interest unless properly construed §2 actually required the district.
(C) Nonjusticiable partisan gerrymander.
(D) Valid whenever race is considered for any benign reason.
**Best: B.** Tag `recent`.

**EP-11 (B.P.J.; recent).** A state requires interscholastic girls' teams to be limited to students of the female sex. A transgender girl challenges the law under equal protection.
(A) Rational basis review applies, because transgender status is not a protected class.
(B) The Court applied intermediate scrutiny to the sex-based line and upheld the law without deciding whether transgender status is a quasi-suspect class.
(C) Strict scrutiny applies.
(D) Invalid, because Title IX prohibits sex-separated teams.
**Best: B** (*West Virginia v. B.P.J.*, 2026). Tag `recent`.

**T-1 (Lucas).** After a landowner buys a beachfront lot, the state passes a law banning all construction on it to protect the dunes; the lot's value is reduced to near zero. State law of nuisance would not have prohibited building.
(A) No taking, because police power regulation.
(B) A taking, requiring compensation, because the law deprives the owner of all economically beneficial use.
(C) A taking only if the owner also proves the state acted in bad faith.
(D) No taking if the owner can sell to the state.
**Best: B** (*Lucas*). Background principles exception does not apply.

**T-2 (Loretto).** A city requires landlords to allow cable companies to install a small cable box on the roof of any apartment building.
(A) No taking, because the intrusion is minor.
(B) A per se taking, because it is a permanent physical occupation.
(C) A taking only if it reduces property value.
(D) No taking, because regulation of landlord-tenant is within police power.
**Best: B** (*Loretto*).

**T-3 (Penn Central).** A city designates a historic building a landmark and forbids altering the facade, but the owner can keep operating the building profitably and may transfer development rights.
(A) A taking, because it limits use.
(B) Not a taking, as applying the Penn Central factors.
(C) A taking only if the city acts in bad faith.
(D) A per se taking.
**Best: B.**

**T-4 (Nollan/Dolan).** A city conditions a permit to expand a store on the owner dedicating a strip of land for a public bike path. Studies show the expansion will modestly increase traffic, but the bike path would not offset it, and the strip is larger than any impact justifies.
(A) Valid, since the owner can decline the permit.
(B) Valid, because bike paths serve a public purpose.
(C) Invalid as a taking, because the condition lacks an essential nexus and rough proportionality.
(D) Valid, because it was imposed by city ordinance rather than ad hoc.
**Best: C** (*Dolan*; *Sheetz* kills D).

**T-5 (Tyler).** A county forecloses on a home for $15,000 in unpaid taxes, sells it for $45,000, and keeps the $30,000 surplus.
(A) No taking, because the owner defaulted.
(B) A taking, because the owner has a property right in the surplus.
(C) A taking only if the county was acting in bad faith.
(D) No taking, because tax collection is within police power.
**Best: B** (*Tyler v. Hennepin County*).

**T-6 (Kelo).** A city condemns private homes as part of an integrated economic development plan to be built by private developers, to create jobs and revenue.
(A) Invalid, because condemned property must be put to public use.
(B) Valid, if the taking is rationally related to a conceivable public purpose and the owner receives just compensation.
(C) Invalid, because takings for private development violate the 14th Amendment.
(D) Valid, without compensation.
**Best: B** (*Kelo*).

**O-1 (Contracts Clause).** A state, short of money, passes a law retroactively reducing the interest it must pay on state bonds it had issued with a promise to pay a specified rate.
(A) Valid, because states can alter their finances in emergency.
(B) Invalid, because the law impairs the State's own contractual obligation and is not reasonable and necessary to serve an important public purpose, with less deference to the State.
(C) Valid, because the Contracts Clause protects only private contracts.
(D) Valid, because the federal government can't enforce it.
**Best: B** (*U.S. Trust*).

**O-2 (attainder).** Congress passes a statute naming five particular union officers and barring them from holding union office because of their past membership in a political party.
(A) Valid, because Congress has broad power over unions.
(B) Invalid as a bill of attainder.
(C) Valid, because it imposes civil, not criminal, penalties.
(D) Invalid as an ex post facto law.
**Best: B** (*United States v. Brown*). D is incorrect: the Ex Post Facto Clause is for criminal laws.

**O-3 (Stogner).** After the statute of limitations on a sex offense expired, a state extends the limitations period and prosecutes a defendant for an offense for which the old period had run.
(A) Valid, since procedural.
(B) Valid, because the legislature may change laws.
(C) Invalid under the Ex Post Facto Clause as a revival of an expired limitations period.
(D) Invalid under the Contracts Clause.
**Best: C.** If the old period had not yet run, the extension would be valid.

**O-4 (unconstitutional conditions).** A state offers a property-tax exemption to veterans but requires each applicant to sign an oath that he does not advocate overthrowing the government by force and is not a member of a certain political party.
(A) Valid, because the exemption is a gift.
(B) Invalid, because the state cannot condition a benefit on surrender of constitutional rights it could not directly infringe.
(C) Valid, because oaths are traditional.
(D) Valid if the exemption is small.
**Best: B** (*Speiser v. Randall*).

**O-5 (Saenz).** A state limits welfare payments to new residents in their first year to the benefit level of their prior state of residence.
(A) Valid, because it prevents welfare migration.
(B) Valid, because states choose benefit levels.
(C) Invalid, because it penalizes interstate travel and denies new citizens the same privileges as other state citizens.
(D) Invalid only under the Art. IV Privileges and Immunities Clause.
**Best: C** (*Saenz v. Roe*, 14th Amend. P or I and right to travel).

## 9. IV. INDIVIDUAL RIGHTS, PART 2: First Amendment (outline IV.F)

### 9.1 Free speech framework (expression)
Step 1: Is it **government action** restricting **speech or expressive conduct**? Step 2: **Content-based or content-neutral?** Step 3: **Which forum?** Step 4: Is the speech in an **unprotected category**?
- **Content-based** (regulates by subject matter, function or purpose, or viewpoint) → **strict scrutiny** (necessary to a compelling interest; least restrictive means) (*Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 2015: sign code treating "ideological", "political", "temporary directional" signs differently is content-based on its face, no matter the benign motive). *City of Austin v. Reagan National Advertising* (2022): on-premises/off-premises distinction is content-neutral location-based (does not single out topics). **Viewpoint discrimination** is the most disfavored form, invalid even in limited and nonpublic forums (*Rosenberger*; *Matal v. Tam*, 2017; *Iancu v. Brunetti*, 2019; but *Vidal v. Elster*, 2024, upheld trademark "names clause" as content-based but viewpoint-neutral). *Chiles v. Salazar* (2026): talk therapy ban is viewpoint-based speech regulation; strict scrutiny. [M/S]
- **Content-neutral time, place, manner (TPM)** → intermediate scrutiny (*Ward v. Rock Against Racism*): narrowly tailored to serve a **significant** government interest and leave open **ample alternative channels**; need not be the least restrictive means (*Clark v. CCNV*; *Frisby v. Schultz*: residential picketing ban; *McCullen v. Coakley*: buffer zone not narrowly tailored). [M]
- **Public forum doctrine:** *Traditional public forums* (streets, parks) and *designated public forums*: content-based restrictions get strict scrutiny; content-neutral TPM must satisfy *Ward*. *Limited public forums* (e.g., university facilities open to student groups): viewpoint neutral and reasonable (*Good News Club*; *Christian Legal Society v. Martinez*). *Nonpublic forums* (military bases, airport terminals, jails): viewpoint-neutral and **reasonable** (*Int'l Society for Krishna Consciousness v. Lee*: airport solicitation ban valid; *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. Mansky*, 2018: polling-place political-apparel ban too indeterminate to be reasonable). [M]
- **Government speech** is not subject to the Free Speech Clause (*Pleasant Grove v. Summum*: monuments in park; *Walker*: specialty plates), but a flag-raising program that took all comers was not (*Shurtleff v. Boston*, 2022). [M]
- **Compelled speech and professional speech:** *West Virginia v. Barnette* (no compelled flag salute); *Wooley v. Maynard* (license plate); *Hurley* (parade); *303 Creative LLC v. Elenis* (2023): Colorado may not compel a web designer to create custom expressive websites celebrating same-sex marriages; *NIFLA v. Becerra* (2018): "professional speech" not a lower-protection category; *Janus v. AFSCME* (2018): nonmember agency fees violate the First Amendment. [M]
- **Online/platform cases:** *Moody v. NetChoice* (2024): curating content is expressive; facial challenges require analysis of the whole range of applications; *NRA v. Vullo* (2024): officials may not coerce third parties to punish disfavored speech; *Murthy v. Missouri* (2024) standing; *Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton* (2025): age-verification for sexually explicit sites gets intermediate scrutiny; *TikTok v. Garland* (2025): upheld without settling content-neutrality. [M/S]
- **Campaign finance:** contribution limits permissible if closely drawn to prevent quid pro quo corruption or its appearance (*Buckley*; *McCutcheon* struck aggregate limits); independent expenditures cannot be limited, including by corporations (*Citizens United*); *Cruz* (2022) struck loan-repayment cap; **NRSC v. FEC (2026)**: limits on party coordinated expenditures invalid, overruling *Colorado II*. [M/S]
- **Expressive conduct:** conduct is speech if there is an intent to convey a particularized message and a great likelihood it will be understood (*Spence*). Regulation aimed at the message is content-based: strict scrutiny (*Texas v. Johnson*, *United States v. Eichman*: flag burning protected). Regulation of conduct with only incidental burden: *United States v. O'Brien* (draft-card burning): important/substantial interest unrelated to suppressing expression; incidental restriction no greater than essential (same as TPM). Schools: *Tinker* (black armbands). [M]

### 9.2 Unprotected (or less protected) categories
- **Incitement** - *Brandenburg v. Ohio*: advocacy of force or lawbreaking is unprotected only if (1) directed to inciting or producing **imminent** lawless action **and** (2) **likely** to produce it. Mere abstract advocacy protected.
- **True threats** - *Virginia v. Black*; **Counterman v. Colorado (2023):** prosecution requires proof the speaker had a subjective understanding of the threatening nature; **recklessness** is enough (not mere negligence/objective standard).
- **Fighting words** - face-to-face personally abusive epithets likely to provoke immediate violence (*Chaplinsky*); very narrow; *Cohen v. California* (jacket) protected.
- **Obscenity** - *Miller v. California*: (1) average person, applying **contemporary community standards**, would find the work, taken as a whole, appeals to the prurient interest; (2) it depicts or describes, in a patently offensive way, sexual conduct specifically defined by state law; (3) it, taken as a whole, **lacks serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value (judged by a reasonable-person standard, not community standards: *Pope v. Illinois*)**. Private possession at home protected (*Stanley v. Georgia*). Child pornography unprotected (*Ferber*); virtual child pornography protected (*Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*). Indecency protected but broadcast regulation permitted (*Pacifica*). Violent video games protected (*Brown v. EMA*).
- **Defamation** - *NYT v. Sullivan*: public official or public figure must prove **actual malice** (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard) by clear and convincing evidence; **private figure** + matter of public concern: state may set a fault standard (at least negligence); presumed or punitive damages require actual malice (*Gertz*); private figure + private concern: presumed and punitive damages allowed without actual malice (*Dun & Bradstreet*); plaintiff bears burden to prove falsity on public concern matters (*Hepps*); parody of public figure: *Hustler v. Falwell*; funeral picketing on public issues protected (*Snyder v. Phelps*); false statements are not categorically unprotected (*United States v. Alvarez*).
- **Commercial speech** - *Central Hudson*: (1) speech must concern lawful activity and not be misleading; (2) asserted governmental interest is **substantial**; (3) regulation **directly advances** the interest; (4) regulation is **not more extensive than necessary** (reasonable fit). Truthful price advertising cannot be banned to reduce consumption (*44 Liquormart*). Compelled factual, uncontroversial disclosures to prevent deception need only be reasonably related to a legitimate interest (*Zauderer*). Lawyer advertising protected but in-person solicitation for pecuniary gain may be banned (*Bates*; *Ohralik*). *Sorrell*: heightened scrutiny of content-/speaker-based burdens. [M]

### 9.3 Speech in special government roles
- **Public employees** (*Pickering* balancing): speech as a **citizen** on a **matter of public concern** is protected unless the employer's interest in efficient service outweighs it (*Connick*; *Rankin*); speech **pursuant to official duties** is not protected (*Garcetti v. Ceballos*); truthful sworn testimony outside job duties is protected (*Lane v. Franks*). Patronage dismissals of non-policymaking employees violate the First Amendment (*Elrod*, *Branti*, *Rutan*).
- **Public school students:** *Tinker* (substantial disruption); *Fraser* (lewd, vulgar on campus); *Hazelwood* (school-sponsored speech: reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns); *Morse v. Frederick* (pro-drug message at school event); *Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L.* (2021): off-campus speech gets less school authority; vulgar Snapchat post by cheerleader protected.
- **Licenses/benefits:** unconstitutional conditions (see §8.5); government may define the program it funds (*Rust*) but not leverage funds to restrict speech outside it (*AOSI*).

### 9.4 Prior restraint, vagueness, overbreadth
- **Prior restraint:** heavy presumption against validity (*Near v. Minnesota*; *New York Times v. United States* (Pentagon Papers); *Nebraska Press*). Licensing schemes that give officials unbridled discretion are facially invalid (*Shuttlesworth*; *Forsyth County*); content-neutral permit schemes need narrow, objective standards (*Thomas v. Chicago Park District*) and, for censorship of expression, procedural safeguards (*Freedman v. Maryland*).
- **Vagueness:** law fails to give fair notice or invites arbitrary enforcement (*Coates v. Cincinnati*; *Grayned*).
- **Overbreadth:** a law is facially invalid if a **substantial number** of its applications are unconstitutional **judged in relation to its plainly legitimate sweep** (*Stevens*; *Williams*; *Hicks*). Overbreadth is a standing exception; it is "strong medicine." After *Moody*, facial challenges require assessing the full scope. [M]

### 9.5 Press
No special First Amendment privilege for reporters to refuse grand jury testimony (*Branzburg*); promises of confidentiality enforceable under state law (*Cohen v. Cowles*). Press and public have a right of access to criminal trials; closure only upon findings of an overriding interest and narrow tailoring (*Richmond Newspapers*; *Globe Newspaper*; *Press-Enterprise*). No special right of access to prisons beyond the public's (*Pell*, *Houchins*). Government generally cannot punish publication of truthful information lawfully obtained on matters of public significance absent a need of the highest order (*Smith v. Daily Mail*; *Florida Star*; *Bartnicki*). Discriminatory taxes on the press invalid (*Minneapolis Star*; *Grosjean*). Government can't compel a right of reply for newspapers (*Miami Herald v. Tornillo*), but broadcast gets less protection (*Red Lion*). [M]

### 9.6 Association
Right to associate for expressive purposes: compelled disclosure of membership/donor lists is subject to **exacting scrutiny** (*NAACP v. Alabama*; *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta* (2021): narrow tailoring required); forced inclusion of unwanted members is unconstitutional when it significantly affects the group's expression (*Boy Scouts v. Dale*), but the State may bar discrimination by large, commercial, non-expressive associations (*Roberts v. U.S. Jaycees*); political party and ballot rules (*Anderson-Burdick*). Public employees' union dues: *Janus*. [M]

### 9.7 Religion: Establishment Clause
- **Current approach:** *Lemon v. Kurtzman* (purpose, effect, entanglement) was **abandoned** in *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022): the Clause is interpreted by "reference to historical practices and understandings" (*Town of Greece v. Galloway* (legislative prayer); *American Legion v. American Humanist Ass'n* (Bladensburg cross)). Coercion remains central in schools (*Lee v. Weisman*: clergy-led graduation prayer; *Santa Fe v. Doe*: student-led, school-sponsored prayer before football games; *Engel*; *Schempp*). Government may not prefer one denomination: strict scrutiny (*Larson v. Valente*; *Catholic Charities Bureau v. Wisconsin*, 2025).
- **Displays:** Ten Commandments posted on classroom walls violate the Clause (*Stone v. Graham*, 1980); outdoor monument on state grounds upheld (*Van Orden*); courthouse display with religious purpose invalid (*McCreary*); creche in a secular holiday display upheld (*Lynch*). `[?]` Ten Commandments litigation in Louisiana/Texas is live in the Fifth Circuit (en banc) post-*Kennedy*: mark the *Stone* card "settled for the exam, challenged in lower courts."
- **Aid:** neutral, true-private-choice vouchers are valid (*Zelman*); states may fund religious education neutrally and may not exclude religious schools solely because they are religious (*Trinity Lutheran*; *Espinoza*; *Carson v. Makin*, 2022: status **or** use), though a state may decline to fund devotional theology degrees (*Locke v. Davey*). *Oklahoma Statewide Charter School Bd. v. Drummond* (2025): 4-4 affirmance, no precedent. Tax exemptions for churches valid (*Walz*). Evolution/creation: *Epperson*, *Edwards v. Aguillard*.

### 9.8 Religion: Free Exercise
- **Neutral law of general applicability:** no exemption required, rational basis (*Employment Division v. Smith*: peyote). **Not neutral or not generally applicable** (targets religion, or allows secular exceptions comparable in risk) → **strict scrutiny**: *Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah* (animal sacrifice ordinance targeted Santeria); *Fulton v. City of Philadelphia* (2021: discretionary exceptions mean not generally applicable); *Tandon v. Newsom* (2021: any comparable secular exception triggers strict scrutiny); *Masterpiece Cakeshop* (2018: official hostility).
- **Exemptions by case:** unemployment benefits denied for refusing Saturday work (*Sherbert v. Verner*, *Thomas*, *Hobbie*: strict scrutiny); Amish school attendance (*Wisconsin v. Yoder*); ministerial exception bars employment suits by ministers (*Hosanna-Tabor*; *Our Lady of Guadalupe*); *Mahmoud v. Taylor* (2025): parental opt-out. Prison: RLUIPA (*Holt v. Hobbs*; *Landor*, 2026: no individual damages). Public employee prayer: *Kennedy v. Bremerton* (2022): coach's private post-game prayer protected under Free Exercise and Free Speech; the school's Establishment concern failed. A state may not bar clergy from public office (*McDaniel v. Paty*); no religious test (*Torcaso*). Sincerity, not truth, of belief matters (*Ballard*; *Thomas*).
- **Statutes, not constitutional:** RFRA (federal), RLUIPA; *Hobby Lobby*. Do not mix with Smith.

### 9.9 Traps for the First Amendment
(1) A true rule applied to the wrong forum (e.g., strict scrutiny to a content-neutral TPM). (2) "Offensive" speech is protected. (3) Incitement needs imminence. (4) True threats now need recklessness. (5) Government employee speech: duties vs citizen. (6) Commercial speech: it is not unprotected, only intermediate. (7) Establishment: do not answer with *Lemon* tests. (8) Smith vs Lukumi/Fulton/Tandon. (9) Prior restraint presumption, with licensing exceptions. (10) Schools: Mahanoy changed off-campus speech.

### 9.10 Own fact patterns: First Amendment

**FA-1 (Reed).** A city ordinance prohibits outdoor signs except three categories: "ideological signs" (up to 20 sq ft), "political signs" (up to 16 sq ft for a limited season), and "temporary directional signs" (up to 6 sq ft). The city says it has no animus toward any viewpoint. A church posting directional signs for its services challenges the size limits.
(A) Valid, because the ordinance does not favor any viewpoint.
(B) Valid, because it is a reasonable time, place, and manner rule.
(C) Subject to strict scrutiny, because it treats signs differently by the topic or purpose of the message.
(D) Subject to rational basis review, because it regulates signs.
**Best: C** (*Reed*). A ignores that content discrimination need not involve viewpoint or animus.

**FA-2 (TPM).** A city bans the use of amplified sound trucks in residential neighborhoods between 9 p.m. and 7 a.m., regardless of the message, in order to protect residents' sleep. A political candidate whose truck was stopped challenges the ordinance.
(A) Invalid, because it restrains political speech.
(B) Invalid, because it is not the least restrictive means.
(C) Valid, as a content-neutral regulation that is narrowly tailored to a significant interest and leaves open ample alternative channels.
(D) Valid, because sound trucks are not speech.
**Best: C** (*Ward*). B is the trap: TPM does not need the least restrictive means.

**FA-3 (limited forum, viewpoint).** A public university opens its meeting rooms to all registered student organizations but denies a room to a student group because it is Christian and wants to hold worship and discuss religious viewpoints.
(A) Valid, because the Establishment Clause forbids funding of religion.
(B) Valid, because rooms are not a public forum.
(C) Invalid, because even in a limited public forum the university may not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint.
(D) Valid, because the university may decide which groups to host.
**Best: C** (*Rosenberger*; *Good News Club*).

**FA-4 (nonpublic forum).** A state-owned airport authority bans in-person solicitation of funds inside terminals, but allows leafleting. A religious group challenges the solicitation ban.
(A) Invalid, because terminals are traditional public forums.
(B) Valid, because terminals are nonpublic forums, and the ban is viewpoint-neutral and reasonable.
(C) Invalid, because solicitation is protected speech.
(D) Valid only if narrowly tailored to a compelling interest.
**Best: B** (*Lee*, 1992).

**FA-5 (Brandenburg).** At a rally, a speaker says: "Some day soon, the people will have to rise up against this government, with guns if need be." No violence follows, and the speaker names no time, place, or target.
(A) May be punished as incitement because it advocates violence.
(B) May not be punished because the advocacy was not directed to producing imminent lawless action and likely to do so.
(C) May be punished because it is directed to a crowd.
(D) May be punished as a true threat.
**Best: B** (*Brandenburg*).

**FA-6 (Counterman).** A defendant sends dozens of messages to a stranger that she reasonably perceives as threatening. He argues he never meant to scare her. A state prosecutes under a statute that asks only whether a reasonable person would regard the statements as threats.
(A) Conviction valid, because true threats are unprotected, under an objective test.
(B) Conviction invalid, because the First Amendment requires proof that the defendant at least acted recklessly as to the threatening nature.
(C) Valid if the stranger actually became afraid.
(D) Invalid, because threats are protected speech.
**Best: B** (*Counterman*, 2023).

**FA-7 (defamation, public official).** A newspaper negligently publishes a false statement that a city's elected mayor accepted a bribe. The mayor sues for defamation.
(A) The mayor wins on proof of negligence.
(B) The mayor must prove the paper knew the statement was false or acted with reckless disregard of truth.
(C) The mayor wins because the statement is false.
(D) The paper wins because defamation is protected.
**Best: B** (*NYT v. Sullivan*).

**FA-8 (defamation, private figure).** A television station falsely and negligently reports that a private plumber was convicted of fraud, a matter of public concern. The plumber seeks presumed damages and punitive damages without proving actual injury or actual malice.
(A) Recoverable, because the report was false.
(B) Presumed and punitive damages are not recoverable without proof of actual malice, though actual damages may be recovered with fault.
(C) Nothing is recoverable, since the report was on a public matter.
(D) Everything is recoverable, since he is a private person.
**Best: B** (*Gertz*).

**FA-9 (commercial speech).** A state forbids liquor stores from advertising retail prices of alcoholic beverages in any medium, to promote temperance. A store challenges the ban.
(A) Valid, because states can regulate alcohol under the Twenty-first Amendment.
(B) Valid, because commercial speech is unprotected.
(C) Invalid, because the ban is more extensive than necessary to advance the state's interest.
(D) Invalid under strict scrutiny.
**Best: C** (*44 Liquormart*; *Central Hudson* step 4).

**FA-10 (Garcetti).** A deputy district attorney, as part of assigned duties, writes an internal memo recommending dismissal of a case because of police misconduct. His supervisor demotes him.
(A) The demotion violates the First Amendment because speech about police misconduct is on a matter of public concern.
(B) The demotion does not violate the First Amendment, because the memo was written pursuant to his official duties.
(C) The demotion is valid only if the office's interests outweigh his under *Pickering*.
(D) The demotion violates due process.
**Best: B** (*Garcetti*).

**FA-11 (Pickering).** A public high school teacher, on her own time, writes a letter to the local newspaper criticizing the school board's allocation of funds between athletics and academics. The board fires her. Her statements are not knowingly false, and the letter does not disrupt the school.
(A) Fired lawfully, because public employees may not criticize their employers.
(B) Fired lawfully, because speech by a teacher is always government speech.
(C) The firing violates the First Amendment, as she spoke as a citizen on a matter of public concern and the board's efficiency interest does not outweigh her interest.
(D) The firing is valid if the board offers a hearing.
**Best: C** (*Pickering*).

**FA-12 (Mahanoy).** A high school student, off campus on a Saturday, posts on social media a vulgar complaint about the cheerleading squad. The school suspends her from the team.
(A) Valid, because schools may regulate all student speech.
(B) Valid, because vulgar speech is unprotected.
(C) Invalid, because the school's interest in regulating off-campus speech is diminished and the school showed no substantial disruption.
(D) Invalid, because students have unlimited speech rights.
**Best: C** (*Mahanoy*, 2021).

**FA-13 (flag).** A state statute makes it a crime to burn a United States flag "in a manner likely to offend observers." A protester burns a flag at a demonstration to express political dissent.
(A) Valid, because flag burning is conduct.
(B) Valid, because the flag is a national symbol.
(C) Invalid, because the statute punishes expression because of its message and cannot satisfy strict scrutiny.
(D) Invalid only if no one is offended.
**Best: C** (*Texas v. Johnson*; *Eichman*).

**FA-14 (licensing).** A city ordinance requires a permit for any parade, giving the mayor "unfettered discretion" to deny permits "in the interest of public welfare." A group is denied a permit and marches without one.
(A) The group can be punished, because it must exhaust the permit process.
(B) The ordinance is facially invalid as a prior restraint, and the group may challenge it without having applied.
(C) The ordinance is valid, because parades are conduct.
(D) The ordinance is valid, because the mayor is elected.
**Best: B** (*Shuttlesworth*; *Forsyth County*).

**FA-15 (vagueness).** A state statute makes it a crime to "annoy or offend" any person in a public place by "conduct that is indecent or annoying."
(A) Valid, because it protects public order.
(B) Valid, because states may regulate public conduct.
(C) Invalid, because it is vague and overbroad, covering protected speech and inviting arbitrary enforcement.
(D) Valid if narrowly construed by police.
**Best: C** (*Coates v. Cincinnati*).

**FA-16 (303 Creative).** A state's public-accommodations law bars businesses from refusing service based on sexual orientation. A website designer who creates original, expressive wedding websites says she will not create sites celebrating same-sex weddings, though she serves all customers for other work. The state seeks to enforce the law against her.
(A) Valid, since public accommodation laws regulate conduct, not speech.
(B) Invalid as applied, because it would compel her to create expressive speech she disagrees with.
(C) Invalid because the law is vague.
(D) Valid because she is a commercial entity.
**Best: B** (*303 Creative*, 2023).

**FA-17 (Dale).** A state's public accommodations law bars membership discrimination on the basis of sexual orientation. A private youth organization whose stated mission includes moral values it believes are inconsistent with homosexual conduct revokes an openly gay adult leader's membership. He sues under the state law.
(A) The organization must comply, as the state has a compelling interest.
(B) Forced inclusion would significantly affect the organization's expressive association, so applying the law violates the First Amendment.
(C) The organization has no First Amendment rights.
(D) The law is valid because the organization is large.
**Best: B** (*Boy Scouts v. Dale*).

**FA-18 (Branzburg).** A reporter is subpoenaed to a grand jury investigating a bribery scheme and is asked to name a confidential source. She refuses based on the First Amendment.
(A) Privileged under the Press Clause.
(B) Not privileged, as the press has no First Amendment immunity from the grand jury's obligation to answer relevant questions.
(C) Privileged if the source was promised confidentiality.
(D) Privileged only in federal court.
**Best: B** (*Branzburg*).

**FA-19 (Richmond Newspapers).** In a criminal murder trial the judge, at the defendant's request and without findings, closes the entire trial to the public and press. A newspaper challenges the order.
(A) Valid, because the defendant has a Sixth Amendment right to a fair trial.
(B) Invalid, because the press and public have a First Amendment right to attend criminal trials and closure requires findings of an overriding interest and narrow tailoring.
(C) Valid, because judges control courtrooms.
(D) Invalid, because trials must always be open.
**Best: B** (*Richmond Newspapers*). D overstates.

**FA-20 (Miller/Pope).** At a prosecution for distributing an obscene film, the judge instructs the jury that to convict, it must find the film (1) appeals to prurient interest under contemporary community standards, (2) is patently offensive, and (3) lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value *under contemporary community standards*. The defendant objects to the third element's standard.
(A) Correct; all prongs use community standards.
(B) Incorrect; the value prong asks whether a reasonable person would find value.
(C) Incorrect; the value prong is for the judge only.
(D) Correct, because the Court has abandoned the third prong.
**Best: B** (*Pope v. Illinois*).

**FA-21 (campaign finance).** A federal statute limits individual contributions to a candidate's campaign to $3,000 per election. A donor challenges the limit.
(A) Invalid, as it burdens political speech.
(B) Valid, as closely drawn to serve the interest in preventing quid pro quo corruption or its appearance.
(C) Invalid, because corporations have First Amendment rights.
(D) Valid under rational basis.
**Best: B** (*Buckley*). 

**R-1 (Town of Greece).** A town board opens its meetings with a prayer by a volunteer local chaplain; most chaplains are Christian because the town sought chaplains from local congregations. Citizens attending are not required to participate, and the town does not edit prayers.
(A) Violates Establishment Clause, because prayers are sectarian.
(B) Valid, as consistent with historical practice and not coercive.
(C) Violates Establishment Clause unless prayers are nonsectarian.
(D) Valid only if prayer leaders include atheists.
**Best: B** (*Greece*).

**R-2 (Lee v. Weisman).** A public high school invites a local clergy member to give a nonsectarian prayer at graduation, and students are expected to stand respectfully.
(A) Valid, because attendance is voluntary.
(B) Invalid, because the school directs and in effect coerces participation in a religious exercise.
(C) Valid, because the prayer is nonsectarian.
(D) Valid, if a student can opt out of attending.
**Best: B.**

**R-3 (Stone).** A state requires that a copy of the Ten Commandments be posted on the wall of every public school classroom, paid for by private donations.
(A) Valid, because private donors fund them.
(B) Valid, because the Commandments are historic.
(C) Invalid under the Establishment Clause, as a religious (rather than secular) display in classrooms (*Stone v. Graham*); lower courts are testing this after *Kennedy*.
(D) Invalid only if students are required to recite them.
**Best: C.** `[?]` Keep a note; do not present as unchallenged.

**R-4 (Zelman).** A state provides tuition vouchers to low-income parents to use at any participating private or public school; most use them at religious schools, chosen by parents.
(A) Violates Establishment Clause because most vouchers go to religious schools.
(B) Valid, because the program is neutral and aid reaches religious schools only through the genuine private choices of parents.
(C) Invalid unless religious schools are excluded.
(D) Valid only if the state audits curricula.
**Best: B** (*Zelman*).

**R-5 (Lukumi).** A city bans the killing of animals in rituals and exempts kosher slaughter, hunting, and pest control; officials say they want to stop a local religious group's animal sacrifices.
(A) Valid, as neutral and generally applicable.
(B) Invalid, because it targets a religious practice and is not neutral and generally applicable; it fails strict scrutiny.
(C) Valid, because animal cruelty is a compelling interest.
(D) Invalid under the Establishment Clause.
**Best: B.**

**R-6 (Smith).** A state criminalizes possession of a controlled substance with no exceptions. A member of a church who uses the substance in a sacrament is prosecuted.
(A) The prosecution violates Free Exercise, since it burdens his religion.
(B) The prosecution is valid, because a neutral, generally applicable law need not exempt religious conduct.
(C) The prosecution is valid only if compelling.
(D) The prosecution violates Establishment.
**Best: B** (*Smith*). (RFRA is statutory and applies to the federal government; many states have mini-RFRAs.)

**R-7 (Carson).** A state offers tuition assistance for students in rural areas without public high schools to attend any approved private school, but excludes "sectarian" schools.
(A) Valid, because the state need not fund religion.
(B) Invalid under the Free Exercise Clause, because it excludes otherwise eligible schools solely due to their religious character or use of funds for religious instruction.
(C) Valid, because *Locke v. Davey* permits all such exclusions.
(D) Invalid under Establishment.
**Best: B** (*Carson v. Makin*, 2022).

**R-8 (Kennedy).** A public high school football coach kneels and quietly prays at midfield after games, joined by some students. The school suspends him to avoid an Establishment Clause violation.
(A) Valid, because his prayer is government speech.
(B) Valid, because Establishment overrides.
(C) The suspension violates the Free Exercise and Free Speech Clauses, because the coach's private prayer is not government speech and the Establishment Clause does not require suppressing it.
(D) Valid, because students might feel coerced.
**Best: C** (*Kennedy v. Bremerton*).

**R-9 (Larson/Catholic Charities).** A state requires disclosure for charities only if a religious organization receives more than half of its funds from nonmembers.
(A) Valid under rational basis.
(B) Subject to strict scrutiny, because it grants a denominational preference.
(C) Valid, because it is a tax law.
(D) Invalid under the Contracts Clause.
**Best: B** (*Larson*; *Catholic Charities* 2025).

**R-10 (ministerial exception).** A church-run elementary school fires a teacher who teaches religion classes, leads students in daily prayer, and holds the title "minister of religion", after the church concludes she has violated its doctrine. She sues the school for employment discrimination.
(A) The case may proceed.
(B) The case is barred by the ministerial exception under the Religion Clauses, because the teacher performs ministerial functions.
(C) Barred only if the school receives no federal funds.
(D) Barred because Title VII doesn't apply to churches.
**Best: B** (*Hosanna-Tabor*; *Our Lady of Guadalupe*).

## 10. How this maps into the app (suggestions)

**Unit plan (MBE path; ~11 units + a "Keeping current" side unit).** Weighted by the NCBE statement that about half of questions are Individual Rights (category IV).
1. **Gatekeeping: can the court hear it?** standing, ripeness, mootness, advisory opinions, political question, A&I state ground, Eleventh Amendment, congressional control of jurisdiction.
2. **Congress's powers:** commerce, taxing, spending (Dole), war/foreign affairs/treaty, Reconstruction Amendments (§5 congruence).
3. **The President and the branches:** Youngstown, appointments/removal (old rule vs Slaughter), immunities, presentment, nondelegation, Speech or Debate.
4. **Federalism:** intergovernmental immunities, anticommandeering, dormant commerce clause, market participant, preemption, Art. IV P&I.
5. **State action and due process:** state action exceptions, substantive due process (Glucksberg, Dobbs, Bruen), procedural due process (Mathews).
6. **Equal protection:** the three tiers sort; race/alienage/gender/rational basis; voting and districting.
7. **Takings and "other protections":** Lucas/Loretto/Penn Central/exactions; contracts clause, attainder, ex post facto, unconstitutional conditions.
8. **Speech I: content, forum, TPM, expressive conduct, compelled speech.**
9. **Speech II: unprotected categories, commercial speech, employees/students, prior restraint/overbreadth, press, association.**
10. **Religion:** Establishment (history/tradition after Lemon), Free Exercise (Smith to Fulton/Tandon).
11. **Mixed practice:** exam-style drills + the daily 5-question drill that rotates units; mock exam with 25 questions proportional to the 50/50 split (12-13 category IV).
12. **Keeping current (side unit, `recent: true`):** 2025-26 decisions in section 3 above, each card with a visible badge: "New in 2026: probably not on your July 2027 MBE, but good to know."

**Interaction types mapped to this subject**
- *Guess-first opener:* "Can this plaintiff sue?" before showing the three standing prongs.
- *Multiple choice / apply-the-rule transfer:* the fact patterns above (answers formatted "Yes, because... / No, because...").
- *Build-from-tiles:* ordered tests (e.g. Dole 5 factors; Pike; Central Hudson; Mathews; Brandenburg; Miller prongs; standing).
- *Match pairs:* case to one-line holding; "overruled" pairs.
- *Yes/no sort:* state action; Eleventh Amendment bars it; is it a taking per se?; which scrutiny.
- *Speed round "Cross-Examination":* one wrong answer and the witness walks; use rapid rule recall (Which scrutiny? Who has standing? Who is a state actor?).
- *Teach-it-in-one-breath lines* (examples): "Standing is injury, traceable, redressable, or it's no case." "Race gets strict, sex gets intermediate, everything else gets rational basis." "Physical occupation or total wipe-out is a taking per se; anything else, balance Penn Central." "Content-based: strict scrutiny. Content-neutral: significant interest, narrow tailoring, ample alternatives." "Dormant commerce clause: discrimination is nearly per se invalid; neutral burdens use Pike."
- *Mistakes notebook* tag: the trap type (e.g. "wrong tier", "wrong forum", "state action missing").

**Provenance field per item:** `status: book|exam|general|unsure`, `src: "Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife (1992)"` etc.; add `recent: true` and `confidence: P|S|M|?` for the table in section 3.
