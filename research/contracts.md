### 1.2 MBE Contracts outline [V: ncbex.org/sites/default/files/2023-01/MBE_Subject_Matter_Outline.pdf]
Note to examinees (verbatim substance): assume the **Official Text of UCC Articles 1 and 2** is in effect. **About half** of Contracts questions come from categories **I (Formation)** and **IV (Performance, breach, discharge)**; about half from II, III, V, VI. **About one-fourth** of Contracts questions are based on UCC Articles 1 and 2 (roughly 6 of 25). The outline "is not intended to list every aspect of a topic" and not every topic is tested every exam. NCBE publishes no per-topic frequencies; do not invent any.

- **I. Formation:** A mutual assent (offer and acceptance; unilateral, bilateral, implied-in-fact); B indefiniteness and absence of terms; C consideration (bargained-for exchange); D obligations enforceable without bargained-for exchange (reliance and restitution); E modification.
- **II. Defenses to enforceability:** A incapacity; B duress and undue influence; C mistake and misunderstanding; D fraud, misrepresentation, nondisclosure; E illegality, unconscionability, public policy; F statute of frauds.
- **III. Content and meaning:** A parol evidence; B interpretation; C omitted and implied terms.
- **IV. Performance, breach, discharge:** A conditions (express and constructive); B excuse of conditions; C breach (material and partial; anticipatory repudiation); D good faith and fair dealing; E express and implied warranties in sale-of-goods contracts; F other performance matters (cure, identification, notice, risk of loss); G impossibility, impracticability, frustration; H discharge (accord and satisfaction, substituted contract, novation, rescission, release).
- **V. Remedies:** A expectation (direct, incidental, consequential); B causation, certainty, foreseeability; C liquidated damages and penalties, limitation of remedies; D avoidable consequences and mitigation; E rescission and reformation; F specific performance and injunction; G reliance and restitution; H remedial rights of breaching parties.
- **VI. Third-party rights:** A third-party beneficiaries; B assignment of rights and delegation of duties.

### 1.3 NextGen Contracts overlay [V: NCBE NextGen Content Scope, Aug 2025, applies Jul 2026-Feb 2027; Blueprint; Contracts Sourcebook excerpt]
- NextGen standalone MCQ (120 per exam, 20 pretest) tests the **eight** Foundational Concepts and Principles "in approximately equal numbers", in two formats: select-one-of-four and **select-two-of-six** (partial credit). Also integrated question sets (21% of score) and performance tasks (30%); MCQ = 49%. Score 500-750.
- NextGen content scope adds or foregrounds (vs. the MBE outline): **identification of governing law incl. hybrid transactions** (goods + services etc.); **electronic transactions (UETA and E-SIGN effectiveness of e-signatures/records for the statute of frauds)**; **force majeure** and partial/temporary excuse; **breach of employment contracts (at-will vs. definite term)**; **UCC performance items: tender, risk of loss, title, delivery terms, rejection, cure, acceptance, revocation of acceptance**; **warranty disclaimers and failure to give notice as seller defenses**; **demand for adequate assurance and retraction of repudiation**; **defenses against claims asserted by intended beneficiaries**; **clauses prohibiting assignment/delegation and revocation of gratuitous assignments**; **restitution for the party in breach and for partial performance of void/voidable/unenforceable contracts**; **no-oral-modification clauses; modification vs. waiver vs. course of performance**; **conditions of satisfaction**; UCC parol evidence, usage/course of dealing under the UCC as separate topics.
- NCBE says its sourcebooks usually state **one approach (majority or clear trend)**, flag "Testable Jurisdictional Splits", and that for some splits **the question will explicitly tell you which approach to apply**. Where there is no UCC rule, examinees apply the common-law rule (UCC 1-103(b)). **App rule: when an item turns on a split, the stem must name the approach.**

That text is from the **2022 UCC amendments**, not in the LII Official Text I read (LII's 2-106 stops at subsection (4)). The MBE outline (dated 2023-01) says "Official Text of Articles 1 and 2". Safe teaching: use the **predominant-purpose/thrust test** (pre-2022 common law majority and the 2022 text agree); do not teach any 2022-only detail as MBE-tested.

## 3. Master comparison: common law vs UCC Article 2 (high-yield table)

| Issue | Common law (majority / Restatement 2d) | UCC Art. 2 (Official Text) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Services, land, intangibles, employment | Transactions in goods (movable at identification, incl. specially manufactured goods, growing crops, severable things) [V 2-105]; mixed deals: predominant purpose |
| Indefiniteness | Essential terms must be reasonably certain to give a remedy (parties, subject matter, price, quantity, time) [R §33] | Open terms OK if parties intended a contract and there is "a reasonably certain basis for giving an appropriate remedy" [V 2-204(3)]; open price = reasonable price [V 2-305]; only quantity essential for 2-201 |
| Acceptance | Mirror image; variance = counteroffer [R §59] | Definite expression of acceptance operates as acceptance despite added/different terms unless expressly conditional [V 2-207(1)]; any reasonable manner/medium [V 2-206(1)(a)]; prompt shipment of conforming OR nonconforming goods accepts (unless accommodation notice) [V 2-206(1)(b)] |
| Irrevocable offers | Option needs consideration (or §87 recital of consideration on fair terms); reliance (§87(2)); beginning performance of unilateral (§45) | Merchant, signed writing, assurance it will be held open: irrevocable without consideration, max **3 months**; on offeree's form needs separate signature [V 2-205] |
| Modification | Needs consideration unless §89 (unanticipated circumstances, fair and equitable), statute, or reliance [R] | **No consideration needed**, but good faith [V 2-209(1); comment on good faith R]; NOM clause enforceable but waiver possible [V 2-209(2),(4),(5)] |
| Statute of frauds | MY LEG; signed writing with essential terms | $500+ goods; signed writing indicating a contract and **quantity**; merchant's confirmation (10 days); specially manufactured goods; admission; payment/acceptance [V 2-201] |
| Parol evidence | Integration analysis; trade usage mostly only for ambiguity | Course of performance, course of dealing, usage of trade explain/supplement even a final writing; consistent additional terms unless the writing is complete and exclusive [V 2-202]; no ambiguity threshold [R comment] |
| Good faith | Implied covenant [R §205] | Every UCC contract/duty: honesty in fact; for **merchants** also observance of reasonable commercial standards [V 1-304; 1-201(b)(20); 2-103(1)(b)] |
| Performance standard | Substantial performance (constructive conditions) | **Perfect tender** [V 2-601], softened by cure [V 2-508], installment rule [V 2-612], shipment-contract rules [V 2-504] |
| Assurance | §251 (modern) | Written demand; suspend if commercially reasonable; no adequate assurance in a reasonable time, max **30 days** = repudiation [V 2-609] |
| Repudiation | Await reasonable time or sue for total breach; retract until aggrieved changes position [R §§250, 256] | Await for "commercially reasonable time" or any remedy; retraction until cancelled/changed position [V 2-610, 2-611] |
| Excuse | Impracticability §261; frustration §265 | Seller: failure of presupposed conditions, allocation, notice [V 2-615, 2-616]; casualty to identified goods [V 2-613] |
| Damages for breach by seller/buyer | Expectation formula | Cover/market/resale/profit formulas [V 2-706, 2-708, 2-712, 2-713, 2-714] |
| Liquidated damages | Reasonable in light of anticipated or actual loss + difficulty of proof [R §356] | Same, plus "inconvenience or nonfeasibility of otherwise obtaining an adequate remedy" [V 2-718(1)] |
| Specific performance | Land/unique chattels; not personal services | "Unique or other proper circumstances" [V 2-716(1)] |
| Assignment/delegation | §§317-328 | Rights assignable unless material change/increase of burden/impair return; ban on assigning "the contract" = bars only delegation [V 2-210] |
| Limitations period | State statute (often 4-6 yrs) | **4 years**, reducible to not less than 1 year by agreement, cannot extend; accrues at tender (future-performance warranty exception) [V 2-725] |

Merchant (UCC 2-104) [V]: person who deals in goods of the kind OR by occupation holds himself out as having knowledge/skill peculiar to the practices or goods involved, or employs an agent/broker who does. "Between merchants" = both chargeable with merchant knowledge. [R: Official Comment 2 says nearly everyone in business is a merchant for the business-practice rules (2-201(2), 2-205, 2-207, 2-209), but for **goods-specific** rules (2-314 merchantability, 2-312(3) infringement, 2-509(3) risk of loss) the seller must deal in goods of the kind.]

### 3.1 Identification of governing law: items (NextGen I; MBE assumes it throughout)

**C0-01 (hybrid; goods predominate).** A cabinet company agreed to build and install custom kitchen cabinets for $10,500 ($9,000 for the cabinets, $1,500 for installation). The cabinets were defective. The homeowner sues 3 years after delivery; the state's general contract statute of limitations is 6 years. Which law most likely governs?
A. Common law, because installation is a service.
B. UCC Article 2, because the predominant thrust of the transaction is the sale of goods; the 4-year UCC period applies and the suit is timely.
C. Common law, because the cabinets are custom.
D. UCC Article 2 for the cabinets and common law for the installation, so the suit is barred.
**Best: B.** Predominant purpose (and 2022 hybrid text where adopted). Custom goods are still goods (2-105 "specially manufactured goods"). D splits the contract, which the predominance test avoids.

**C0-02 (services).** A consultant agreed to analyze a company's supply chain for $40,000 and to deliver a bound 80-page report and three binders of source materials (printing cost $300). Which law governs?
A. UCC Article 2; goods are delivered.
B. Common law; the transaction is predominantly a service.
C. UCC Article 2 for the binders only.
D. Neither; hybrid contracts are unenforceable.
**Best: B.**

**C0-03 (capacity supplements the UCC).** A 16-year-old bought a $900 laptop from an electronics merchant, paid cash, and a week later tried to return it, disaffirming the contract. The merchant argues that Article 2 contains no minors' rule. Result?
A. The merchant wins; Article 2 controls and has no such rule.
B. The minor may disaffirm; Article 2 is supplemented by the common-law rules on capacity unless displaced (UCC 1-103(b)).
C. The minor may not disaffirm because merchants are protected.
D. The merchant wins because the sale was for cash.
**Best: B.**

**C0-04 (merchant status for a goods-specific rule).** A dentist sold her used dental X-ray machine to another dentist's practice. The machine failed its first day. The buyer sues for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability. Result?
A. For buyer; all sellers give the warranty.
B. For seller; she is not a merchant with respect to dental X-ray machines (not in the business of selling them), so 2-314 does not apply (express warranty or fitness theories remain possible).
C. For buyer; both parties are merchants in their business practices.
D. For seller; used goods cannot carry warranties.
**Best: B.** (Goods-specific rules like 2-314 need a seller who deals in goods of that kind.)

# PART A. FORMATION (MBE I)

## 4. Mutual assent: offer, acceptance, termination (I.A) [R unless tagged]

**Rules**
- Objective theory: what a reasonable person would understand from words/conduct, not secret intent (Lucy v. Zehmer is the classic label). A joke is a contract only if the other side reasonably took it seriously.
- **Offer** (Restatement §24): manifestation of willingness to enter a bargain so made as to justify the other in understanding that assent is invited and will conclude it. [V: NextGen sourcebook quotes §24]. Conveys power of acceptance; offeror is "master of the offer" (can set mode, time, medium).
- **Not offers (usually):** preliminary negotiations, price quotes, invitations to bid, **advertisements**. An ad becomes an offer when it is definite, specific, and leaves nothing for negotiation ("first come, first served"; Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus). **Rewards** are offers to the public, usually unilateral. Majority: offeree must know of the reward before performing.
- **Auctions** [V UCC 2-328]: with reserve unless explicitly "without reserve". With reserve: auctioneer may withdraw goods until the hammer. Without reserve: once bidding is called for, the lot cannot be withdrawn unless no bid within a reasonable time. Bidder may retract until the hammer. Each bid is an offer; the hammer is acceptance.
- **Mailbox rule** (§63): acceptance by an authorized medium is effective **on dispatch**, even if lost. Exceptions: (i) **option contract: acceptance effective on receipt**; (ii) offeror's terms say otherwise; (iii) rejection is effective on **receipt**; if offeree sends a rejection and then an acceptance, whichever is received first generally controls (§40) with an estoppel exception if offeror relied on the rejection.
- **Termination of the power of acceptance:** revocation (effective on receipt; may be learned from a reliable third party), lapse (stated or reasonable time), rejection, **counteroffer** (rejects the offer), death or incapacity of offeror or offeree (**no notice needed**), destruction of the subject matter, supervening illegality. A mere **inquiry** ("would you take less?") is not a counteroffer. "Grumbling acceptance" is still acceptance.
- **Public offers** can be revoked by equally effective publicity.
- **Silence** is not acceptance, except: offeree takes the benefit of services knowing compensation expected; prior dealings make silence acceptance; offeror says silence = acceptance and offeree intends to accept (§69).
- **Mode:** unless the offer unambiguously requires otherwise, it invites acceptance by any reasonable means (§30) and is read as inviting **either promise or performance** (§32; UCC 2-206(1)(a)). **Unilateral** offers (promise for a performance): beginning performance creates an **option** (§45); offeror cannot revoke for a reasonable time while the offeree completes; offeree is not bound to finish. Notice of acceptance by performance is required only if offeror requires or has no adequate way to learn of it (§54); UCC 2-206(2): offeree should give notice within a reasonable time or offeror may treat the offer as lapsed.
- **Express vs. implied-in-fact vs. implied-in-law:** implied-in-fact = real contract inferred from conduct (no legal difference from express) [V sourcebook]. Implied-in-law (quasi-contract) is a restitution remedy, not a contract.
- **Agreements to agree / preliminary agreements:** enforceable if parties intended to be bound **and** terms are definite enough for a remedy (both must be met) [V sourcebook, citing Ferriell]. If parties intend to reduce a concluded deal to a formal writing, they are bound unless they manifest intent not to be bound until signing (§27) [V].
- **Option contract / irrevocable offers:** supported by consideration (even nominal if actually given, or recited consideration on fair terms in a signed writing, §87(1)(a)); UCC firm offer (2-205); **reliance** (§87(2): offeror should reasonably expect substantial reliance before acceptance and it occurs, e.g., subcontractor bids relied on by general contractor, Drennan v. Star Paving; binds the sub, not the GC).

**Traps**
1. "Ad = offer" is almost always wrong, but "first come, first served" or "limited to the first three customers" flips it.
2. Mailbox rule applies to **acceptance**, not revocation or rejection (receipt).
3. Mailbox rule does NOT apply to options (receipt).
4. Death of the offeror kills the offer even if the offeree does not know. Option contracts and firm offers survive.
5. Counteroffer terminates the original offer; "I'll pay $90 instead" cannot be revived by then saying "OK, $100" (that is a new offer).
6. Late acceptance is a new offer.
7. Starting performance in a unilateral contract: the traditional rule (no contract until full performance, revocable until then) is **not** the modern/MBE rule; use §45 option reasoning.

**Items**

**C1-01 (ads).** A furniture store ran a newspaper ad: "SATURDAY ONLY: three oak desks, $40 each, first come, first served." Ines arrived first Saturday morning, tendered $40, and demanded a desk. The store said the price was a printing mistake and refused. Ines sues. Likely result?
A. Ines loses, because advertisements are invitations to negotiate, not offers.
B. Ines loses, because the store had not accepted her $40.
C. Ines wins, because the ad was a definite offer that she accepted by tendering the price.
D. Ines wins only if she can show she relied on the ad to her detriment.
**Best: C.** A specific ad that states quantity, price, and "first come, first served" leaves nothing to negotiate; the person to appear and perform accepts. A is the usual rule but this ad is the exception. B is wrong because acceptance was Ines's act. D is wrong because no reliance is required for a contract (that is promissory estoppel).

**C1-02 (option + mailbox).** On Jan 1, Pia wrote Quinn: "I will sell you my lake lot for $90,000. In return for your payment of $500 now, I will hold this offer open until Feb 1." Quinn paid the $500. On Jan 20 Pia mailed a revocation. On Jan 22 Quinn mailed an acceptance. Pia's letter arrived Jan 23; Quinn's arrived Jan 24. Is there a contract?
A. No, because Pia's revocation was mailed before Quinn's acceptance.
B. No, because Pia's revocation arrived before Quinn's acceptance.
C. Yes, because Quinn's acceptance was effective when mailed on Jan 22, before the revocation arrived.
D. Yes, because the option made the offer irrevocable and Quinn's acceptance was received before Feb 1.
**Best: D.** An option supported by consideration cannot be revoked during the stated period, so the revocation was ineffective; for options, acceptance is effective on **receipt** (Jan 24, within the period). C reaches the right result for the wrong reason (mailbox rule does not govern options). A and B treat the revocation as effective.

**C1-03 (death).** On Monday, Rob e-mailed Sal an offer to sell his vintage guitar for $3,000, "good for ten days." Rob died Wednesday. Thursday, Sal, unaware, e-mailed an acceptance. Is there a contract?
A. Yes, because Rob promised to keep the offer open for ten days.
B. Yes, because Sal accepted before the ten days ran and did not know of Rob's death.
C. No, because Rob's death terminated the offer, and Sal's lack of knowledge does not matter.
D. No, because the offer was not in a signed writing.
**Best: C.** Death of the offeror terminates the power of acceptance without notice. A fails because a bare promise to keep an offer open is not an option without consideration, and Rob (an individual, not a merchant) is outside the UCC firm-offer rule (2-205), so the offer was revocable and died with him. B is wrong for the reason stated. D is irrelevant (an e-mailed offer for a $3,000 guitar is not defeated by a writing rule here, and the SoF is a separate issue from termination).

**C1-04 (counteroffer).** Tess offered to sell her bike for $400. Uma replied, "I'll give you $300." Tess said no. Uma then said, "Fine, $400." Tess says she has changed her mind. Is Tess bound?
A. Yes, because Uma's last statement accepted Tess's original offer.
B. Yes, because an offeror cannot revoke an offer without notice.
C. No, because Uma's $300 response was a counteroffer that terminated the original offer.
D. No, because bicycle sales must be in writing.
**Best: C.** A counteroffer rejects and terminates the offer. "Fine, $400" is a new offer Tess may decline. Contrast "Would you take $300?" which is a mere inquiry and would not terminate.

**C1-05 (unilateral, §45).** Vera told her neighbor Wes, "I'll pay you $1,000 if you paint my fence by Friday." On Wednesday Wes bought $120 of paint and painted half the fence. Wednesday evening Vera told Wes, "Stop, I revoke my offer." Wes finished Thursday. Under the modern majority rule, Wes may recover?
A. Nothing, because a unilateral offer is revocable until performance is complete.
B. $120 in reliance only, because no contract formed.
C. $1,000, because beginning performance made the offer irrevocable for a reasonable time to complete.
D. $500, the value of the work done before revocation.
**Best: C.** Restatement §45: beginning performance creates an option; Wes completed within the time. A is the old rule. B and D confuse reliance/restitution with the contract remedy once the contract is complete.

**C1-06 (reward / knowledge)** *(split; stem must name rule)*. A city posted "$5,000 for information leading to the arrest of the arsonist." Dana, who had never seen the notice, gave the police information that led to an arrest. Under the majority rule, Dana may recover?
A. Yes; the offer was accepted by performance.
B. No; an offeree must know of an offer to accept it.
C. Yes, in restitution, because the city benefited.
D. No, because a reward cannot be accepted by providing information.
**Best: B.** Majority: acceptance requires knowledge of the offer (a minority permits recovery). C is wrong because a volunteer's benefit is not unjust enrichment here. [U flag: marks majority; keep a "minority" note out of the quiz.]

## 5. Indefiniteness, absence of terms, gap-filling (I.B)

**Rules**
- **Common law:** enough certainty to determine breach and give a remedy (§33); courts fill missing terms with reasonable ones (price, time, place) if the parties intended to be bound. Missing **quantity** or **price** is fatal more often at common law; "agreement to agree" on a material term is unenforceable unless objective standards fill it.
- **UCC:** contract may be made "in any manner sufficient to show agreement", even if the moment of making is undetermined, and "does not fail for indefiniteness if the parties have intended to make a contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for giving an appropriate remedy" [V 2-204(1)-(3)]. Gap-fillers [V]:
  - **Price:** reasonable price at time for delivery if nothing said / left to agree and parties fail / third-party standard not set; price fixed by one party must be fixed in good faith; but if parties intend **not to be bound unless price is fixed or agreed**, there is no contract [2-305].
  - **Output and requirements:** quantity = actual output or requirements "as may occur in good faith", not unreasonably disproportionate to any stated estimate or to normal prior output/requirements; **exclusive dealing** imposes best efforts by seller to supply and by buyer to promote [2-306].
  - **Delivery:** single lot, payment due on tender [2-307]; seller's place of business is place for delivery (or location of identified goods known to both) [2-308]; **reasonable time** for shipment or any action [2-309(1)]; indefinite-duration successive-performance contracts: valid for a reasonable time, terminable at any time by either party on **reasonable notice** (agreement dispensing with notice invalid if unconscionable) [2-309(2)-(3)]; payment due at time and place buyer is to receive goods [2-310]; particulars of performance left to one party must be specified in good faith [2-311].
- **Illusory promise:** a promise that commits the promisor to nothing (e.g., "I will buy as much as I decide I want") is not consideration. A requirements contract is not illusory because of the good-faith limit.
- **Employment of indefinite duration** is at will.

**Traps**
- Missing price in a goods contract does not kill the deal; "subject to price to be agreed later and we are not bound until then" does.
- "Open terms" must be paired with "intent to contract"; two merchants shaking hands with no quantity still may fail 2-201 (SoF) even if 2-204 is satisfied.
- Output contracts: the quantity limit is **good faith + not unreasonably disproportionate**, not the exact historical figure.

**Items**

**C1-07 (open price).** On the phone, a restaurant owner agreed to buy 400 lb of live crab weekly from a seafood wholesaler "at the going price." Nothing else was said about price. Three months later the wholesaler claims there was no contract because price was never fixed. Result?
A. No contract; price is an essential term at common law.
B. No contract; the going price is not tied to a date.
C. A contract exists; if the parties fail to agree, the price is a reasonable price at the time for delivery.
D. A contract exists only if the order was in a signed writing.
**Best: C.** UCC 2-204(3), 2-305(1). A applies a common-law idea the UCC displaced. D confuses the SoF (which is satisfied here by performance if accepted, and at any rate is a different issue).

**C1-08 (requirements).** Beta Bakery agreed in writing to buy "all the flour required for its Dallas plant" from Mill Co. for 2 years at $0.40/lb. The plant's stated estimate was 10,000 lb/month. In month 5 Beta ordered 80,000 lb because it had opened a huge new line. Mill refuses. Result?
A. Mill must supply; "all requirements" is binding.
B. The contract is illusory because Beta set its own quantity.
C. Mill may refuse the excess; quantity may not be unreasonably disproportionate to the stated estimate or normal requirements.
D. Mill may refuse only if Beta acted in bad faith.
**Best: C.** UCC 2-306(1). B is wrong (good faith makes it valid). D is wrong because the "unreasonably disproportionate" limit is independent of motive. A ignores the limit.

**C1-09 (illusory).** Ann promised to sell Ben "such quantity of widgets as I choose to deliver" at $5 each. Ben promised to pay for what Ann delivered. Ann delivers nothing and Ben sues. Result?
A. Ben wins; Ann's promise is a binding output promise.
B. Ben loses; Ann's promise is illusory, so there is no bargained-for exchange.
C. Ben wins; the UCC supplies a reasonable quantity.
D. Ben loses; widgets under $500 are outside the SoF.
**Best: B.** Ann committed to nothing. (A and C wrongly rescue; D misapplies.)

**C1-10 (agreement to agree).** Two companies signed a "Letter of Intent" saying they "intend to enter a supply agreement for 5 years, price and quantity to be negotiated in good faith." Talks broke down. One sues for breach of the supply contract. Result?
A. Contract exists; courts will supply a reasonable price and quantity.
B. No supply contract: the parties left price and quantity open and, taken as a whole, did not intend to be bound before agreeing on them.
C. Contract exists because a letter of intent is always binding.
D. No contract, because LOIs are never enforceable in any respect.
**Best: B.** Intent to be bound and definiteness both required. D overstates: a duty to negotiate in good faith can be binding if the parties expressly agreed to it (jurisdiction-dependent) [U].

## 6. Consideration (I.C) [R unless tagged]

**Rules**
- **Bargained-for exchange** (§71): promise or performance sought by the promisor in exchange for his promise and given by the promisee in exchange for it. Benefit/detriment is the usual evidence. **Adequacy is not examined** (§79); a sham or nominal "peppercorn" may fail the bargain test; gifts and conditional gifts ("if you go to the store") are not bargains.
- **Illusory promises** fail; a **good-faith satisfaction** clause or a real termination-on-notice right does not make a promise illusory.
- **Pre-existing duty rule** (§73): promising or doing what you are already legally obligated to do is not consideration. A **new or different** performance (even a slight change, or something extra like earlier payment, different place or form, a new item) is consideration. A good-faith dispute over the duty (e.g., unliquidated debt) supports consideration.
- **Liquidated, undisputed debt**: creditor's promise to accept less is not supported by consideration if debtor pays the sum due on the due date; add a peppercorn or earlier payment and it is. **Unliquidated or disputed debt**: the accord is enforceable.
- **Settlement of claims**: forbearance to sue is consideration if the claim is **honestly believed to be valid and asserted in good faith** (§74) (not if known to be frivolous).
- **Past consideration / moral obligation** is not consideration. **Exceptions** (promise binding without new consideration): promise to pay a debt barred by the statute of limitations (§82); debt discharged in bankruptcy (§83); ratification of a voidable duty after capacity (§85); **material benefit rule** (§86: promise made in recognition of a benefit previously received by the promisor from the promisee, binding to the extent necessary to prevent injustice; not for gifts or officious benefits; not if disproportionate).
- **Third-party** consideration: the exchange may flow to or from a third person (Hamer v. Sidway: nephew gave up drinking, smoking).
- **Mutual rescission** of an executory contract is supported by the mutual release of duties.
- **UCC deviations:** modification needs no consideration [V 2-209(1)]; firm offer needs none [V 2-205]; written waiver of a claim after breach needs none [1-306, R]; seals inoperative for sale-of-goods contracts [V 2-203].

**Traps**
- "Past consideration" fact patterns (thank-you promises) are the favorite: care for a relative last year, a gift of a car "for all your help".
- A promise to pay more to a contractor for the same work: look for **something new**.
- Pre-existing duty also covers an employee's promise to keep working or a public duty (police reward for doing the job); some reward cases carve out exceptions for performance beyond duty.
- Mutual promises must both be real (watch "if I feel like it").

**Items**

**C2-01 (past consideration).** Last spring, Dee drove her uncle Gus to chemotherapy each week without being asked or promised anything. In June, Gus signed a note: "In gratitude for your help, I promise to pay you $8,000." Gus died and his estate refuses to pay. Dee sues. Likely result?
A. For Dee; the signed writing is enforceable.
B. For Dee; Gus received a benefit and a moral obligation supports the promise.
C. For the estate; the promise was not given in exchange for Dee's help, which was already completed.
D. For the estate, only because Gus is deceased.
**Best: C.** Past consideration does not satisfy the bargain requirement. B is the moral obligation argument that most courts reject; the §86 "material benefit" doctrine is a possible argument but requires a benefit conferred on the promisor in circumstances where compensation was reasonably expected, so the safest MBE answer remains C. [U: some jurisdictions apply §86 more broadly; the item stem should say "assume the jurisdiction applies the traditional rule".]

**C2-02 (pre-existing duty).** Hal Builders agreed to build Meg's house for $400,000. Midway, Hal said lumber prices had risen and he would stop unless Meg agreed to pay $430,000. Meg agreed in writing. Hal finished. Meg pays $400,000. Assuming a common-law (non-UCC) jurisdiction that has not adopted Restatement §89 and that the lumber increase was foreseeable, Hal may recover?
A. $30,000, because the modification was in writing.
B. $30,000, because Meg's agreement was voluntary.
C. Nothing more, because Meg's promise was not supported by consideration.
D. $15,000, the reasonable share of the added cost.
**Best: C.** Hal was already bound to build for $400,000; he gave nothing new. (A: writing is no substitute for consideration. D: no such rule.) *Twin item (UCC):* if the contract were to sell lumber, the modification would bind without consideration if made in good faith (2-209(1)); extortion of a modification with no legitimate commercial reason fails.

**C2-03 (debt, part payment).** Carl owes Nia $10,000, undisputed, due March 1. On March 1, Nia says, "Give me $8,000 today and we're square." Carl pays $8,000 cash. Nia sues for $2,000. Result?
A. For Nia; accepting less than a liquidated, undisputed debt is not supported by consideration.
B. For Carl; a creditor's oral promise to forgive part of a debt is binding.
C. For Carl; payment on the due date is sufficient consideration.
D. For Nia only if she did not receive the $8,000 first.
**Best: A.** (Variants that flip the answer: the debt was disputed in good faith; or Carl paid a few days early; or paid with a car; or Nia relied to her detriment, estoppel.)

**C2-04 (settlement).** Omar honestly believed Pru had damaged his fence and threatened to sue for $2,000. Pru, not conceding liability, promised to pay $1,200 if Omar forbore. Omar did. Pru's lawyer later concludes Omar had no valid claim. Is Pru bound?
A. No; a promise to settle an invalid claim is not supported by consideration.
B. Yes; forbearing from a claim honestly asserted in good faith is consideration.
C. Yes, only if the claim was in fact valid.
D. No, because Pru did not admit liability.
**Best: B.** (§74.) The result flips if Omar knew the claim was baseless.

**C2-05 (illusory vs real).** Rex promised to supply Lia with firewood "for as long as Lia buys at least 5 cords a month"; Lia may cancel anytime on 30 days' written notice. Rex stops delivering after Lia's second month, with no notice. Lia sues. Rex argues the contract is illusory because Lia can cancel. Result?
A. Rex wins; Lia's right to cancel renders her promise illusory.
B. Lia wins; Lia's right to cancel only on notice means her promise to buy 5 cords was real for the notice period.
C. Rex wins; the contract lacks a fixed term.
D. Lia wins only if she prepaid.
**Best: B.** A termination right exercisable only on notice is still a commitment during the notice period. A would be right if she could cancel instantly and without notice.

**C2-06 (promissory vs bargain)** *(good guess-first opener)*. "Which of these is a bargained-for exchange? (a) 'I'll give you my old laptop if you come over for dinner.' (b) 'I'll give you my old laptop in exchange for your doing the dishes tonight.' (c) 'Come pick up the old laptop; it's yours.'" **Answer: (b).** (a) is a conditional gift (the dinner is a condition, not the price); (c) is a gift.

## 7. Promissory estoppel and restitution (I.D) [R unless tagged]

**Promissory estoppel (§90).** A promise which the promisor **should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance of a definite and substantial character** on the part of the promisee or a third person and which **does induce such action or forbearance** is binding **if injustice can be avoided only by enforcement**. **The remedy may be limited as justice requires** (often reliance damages, sometimes expectation). Elements for a tile build: (1) clear promise; (2) reasonable expectation of reliance; (3) actual, reasonable, substantial reliance; (4) injustice absent enforcement. Variants: charitable subscriptions and marriage settlements are binding without proof of reliance (§90(2)); sub-bid reliance (§87(2)); pre-contract negotiation promises (Hoffman v. Red Owl); family gratuitous promises (Ricketts v. Scothorn: grandfather's note, granddaughter quit job). [V: NextGen scope lists "promissory estoppel and restitution" and "reliance damages as a remedy for promissory estoppel actions".]

**Restitution / unjust enrichment (quasi-contract).** Requires a benefit conferred on defendant, appreciated/retained, under circumstances where it would be unjust to retain without payment. **Not** for volunteers/officious intermeddlers; **emergency** services (e.g., treating an unconscious person) allow recovery of reasonable value. Measure: **benefit conferred** (market or reasonable value), not the contract price (but a fully performing plaintiff who is owed only money is capped at the contract price, §373). Available: when a contract is **unenforceable** (statute of frauds), **void or voidable**, discharged by impracticability/frustration, rescinded for fraud/mistake, or on breach (non-breaching party can elect restitution). **Breaching party** may recover the benefit conferred in excess of the other's damages (§374), unless a valid liquidated-damages clause or forfeiture clause controls [V: NextGen scope includes "restitution for the party in breach; partially performed void, voidable, unenforceable contracts"].

**Traps**
- PE is a **substitute for consideration**, not an element of a bargain contract.
- PE damages: usually reliance, possibly limited; do not award expectation by reflex.
- Restitution measure is **value to the defendant**, not plaintiff's costs.
- "Breaching party cannot recover anything" is wrong in most states.

**Items**

**C2-07 (PE).** Uncle Fred told his nephew Gil, "I'll give you $30,000 for a new truck." Gil sold his old truck to a friend for $4,000 (worth $9,000) and signed a purchase contract for the new truck. Fred then said he had changed his mind. Gil sues. Gil's best theory and likely recovery?
A. Contract; $30,000, because Fred promised in writing.
B. No claim; Fred's promise was a gift and no consideration was given.
C. Promissory estoppel; recovery may be limited to Gil's reliance loss, such as the $5,000 shortfall on the sale.
D. Quasi-contract; the value of benefits Gil conferred on Fred.
**Best: C.** Gratuitous promise, foreseeable and substantial reliance. D fails because Gil conferred no benefit on Fred. (B is the pre-§90 answer.)

**C2-08 (sub-bid).** A general contractor used Sub's $82,000 electrical bid in her own bid for a school project, and the GC won. Before the GC accepted Sub's bid, Sub withdrew, saying he miscalculated. The GC hired a replacement at $91,000. Likely result?
A. For Sub; an offer may be revoked any time before acceptance.
B. For Sub; the GC gave no consideration for an option.
C. For the GC; Sub should reasonably have expected reliance, and the offer is irrevocable to avoid injustice (here, damages of $9,000).
D. For the GC; she accepted the offer when she used the bid.
**Best: C.** Restatement §87(2)/Drennan. D is wrong because using a bid is not acceptance. The GC is not bound to use Sub (one-sided).

**C2-09 (quasi-contract).** A hiker collapsed outside a remote cabin. Dr. Roy, a passer-by, treated him until help came. The hiker, unconscious throughout, later refuses to pay. Roy sues. Result?
A. Roy loses; there was no agreement and he was a volunteer.
B. Roy wins; the reasonable value of the emergency services, in restitution.
C. Roy wins; the express contract was formed by the hiker's silence.
D. Roy wins; $0 because the hiker received no benefit.
**Best: B.** Emergency exception to the volunteer rule. C is wrong because silence by an unconscious person does not manifest assent; implied-in-law, not implied-in-fact.

**C2-10 (breaching party's restitution).** Builder agreed to renovate a restaurant for $200,000. After completing work worth $120,000 at market value, Builder walked off the job without justification. Owner paid a replacement $150,000 to finish; the original contract price for the unfinished work was $80,000. Owner has paid Builder nothing so far. Under the majority/Restatement view, what can Builder recover?
A. Nothing; a breaching party cannot recover.
B. $50,000: the $120,000 benefit conferred, less Owner's $70,000 loss from the breach.
C. $120,000 in full.
D. $80,000, the unperformed portion of the price.
**Best: B.** Restitution for the breaching party is limited to the benefit conferred minus the injured party's damages (§374). Owner's damages = cost to finish $150,000 - $80,000 contract balance = $70,000. Cross-check: Owner pays $50,000 + $150,000 = $200,000 total, exactly the contract price. A is the old rule; C ignores Owner's loss; D uses the wrong measure.

**C2-11 (SoF + restitution).** Orally, Seller agreed to sell a parcel to Buyer for $200,000. Buyer paid $20,000 and Seller then refused to proceed, relying on the statute of frauds. Buyer's best claim?
A. Specific performance of the contract.
B. Restitution of $20,000.
C. Reliance damages of $200,000.
D. Nothing; the oral land contract is void.
**Best: B.** Contract is unenforceable (not void); restitution of the benefit conferred remains. A fails absent part performance beyond payment (see section 10).

## 8. Modification (I.E) [R; UCC V]

**Rules**
- **Common law:** modification of an executory contract requires consideration (pre-existing duty rule) unless **(a)** the modification is **fair and equitable in view of circumstances not anticipated** when the contract was made (§89(a); the Restatement/trend rule; many states adopt), **(b)** a statute makes it binding, **(c)** justifiable reliance on the promise (§89(c)), **(d)** the modification is a mutual rescission and a new contract (both sides give something). If the underlying contract is within the SoF, the modification must meet the SoF.
- **Waiver:** voluntary relinquishment of a known right (e.g., a condition); no consideration needed; **may be retracted as to future performance by reasonable notice** unless the other party has materially changed position in reliance (§84; UCC 2-209(5)). **Modification vs. waiver vs. course of performance:** modification changes terms by agreement; waiver excuses a condition/right unilaterally; repeated accepted performance can show waiver or interpretation (UCC 1-303, 2-208).
- **No-oral-modification (NOM) clauses:** [U] common-law jurisdictions split: many enforce them only against modifications lacking other grounds, and let them be waived by words or conduct (including oral waiver) especially with reliance; a minority (e.g., by statute) require a signed writing. UCC says a signed agreement excluding modification except by signed writing "cannot be otherwise modified or rescinded", but an attempt at modification that fails can operate as a **waiver** (retractable), and a NOM clause on a merchant's form must be **separately signed** by a non-merchant [V 2-209(2),(4),(5)]. If the modified contract is within 2-201, the modification needs a writing [V 2-209(3)].
- **UCC modification (2-209(1)):** "needs no consideration to be binding" [V]. Limited by **good faith** (1-304): extortionate modifications without legitimate commercial reason are ineffective; a modification to reflect real cost increases is in good faith [R: Official Comment 2].

**Traps**
- Don't apply the pre-existing duty rule to a UCC goods contract.
- Don't treat a written modification as automatically valid at common law; look for consideration or §89.
- Waiver ≠ modification: waiver of a **condition** can be retracted for future performance with notice; a modification cannot be unilaterally undone.

**Items**

**C2-12 (UCC mod).** Under a signed contract, Zed Co. agreed to deliver 2,000 steel brackets to Yara Inc. at $10 each. After steel prices rose 12%, Zed phoned Yara and demanded $11 per bracket. Yara agreed and faxed a signed note saying "OK at $11." Yara later refuses to pay more than $10. Result?
A. Yara wins; Zed gave no new consideration.
B. Yara wins; modifications must be in the original form of writing.
C. Zed wins; modifications of sale-of-goods contracts need no consideration but must meet the good-faith requirement, which is satisfied by a real cost increase.
D. Zed wins; a price modification is a new contract.
**Best: C.** UCC 2-209(1). A states the common-law rule.

**C2-13 (NOM + waiver).** A written equipment-supply contract between two businesses provides, "No modification unless in a writing signed by both parties." The buyer's manager orally agreed to accept deliveries on Fridays instead of Mondays. In reliance, the seller spent $40,000 restructuring its delivery routes. After 3 months the buyer insisted on Monday deliveries and invoked the NOM clause. Under the UCC, result?
A. Buyer wins; the NOM clause makes the oral change ineffective, and nothing can supplement it.
B. Seller wins; the oral change fails as a modification, but it operates as a waiver, and the buyer cannot retract the waiver because retraction would be unjust in light of the seller's material change of position.
C. Seller wins; the oral change is a binding modification despite the clause.
D. Buyer wins; any waiver may be retracted at any time.
**Best: B.** 2-209(2) enforces the clause against modification, but 2-209(4) lets the failed modification operate as a waiver, retractable on reasonable notice unless retraction would be unjust due to a material change of position in reliance (2-209(5)). Without the reliance facts, the buyer could retract for future deliveries after reasonable notice.

**C2-14 (§89).** *Stem names the rule: "assume the jurisdiction follows Restatement (Second) §89."* Dig Co. agreed to excavate a lot for $50,000. Hitting unexpected bedrock, Dig Co. asked for $65,000; the owner orally agreed. After the work, the owner pays $50,000. Dig Co. sues for $15,000. Result?
A. Dig Co. loses; the owner received nothing new.
B. Dig Co. wins; the modification is fair and equitable in view of unanticipated circumstances.
C. Dig Co. loses; the owner's promise was oral.
D. Dig Co. wins only in quantum meruit.
**Best: B.** (If the stem said the jurisdiction follows the traditional pre-existing duty rule, A.) The oral form is irrelevant (no SoF issue for construction on one's own land). [U: which approach the exam assumes is a testable split; always state it.]

# PART B. UCC FORMATION AND STATUTE OF FRAUDS

## 9. Sales formation: 2-206, 2-205, 2-207 (the battle of the forms) [V statute text; R comments]

**2-206 [V].** Unless unambiguously indicated, an offer to make a contract invites acceptance "in any manner and by any medium reasonable in the circumstances." An order for **prompt or current shipment** invites acceptance by a prompt promise to ship **or by prompt or current shipment of conforming or nonconforming goods**. But a shipment of nonconforming goods is **not** an acceptance if the seller **seasonably notifies** the buyer that it is offered only as an **accommodation**. (Otherwise shipping nonconforming goods = acceptance AND breach.) Where beginning performance is a reasonable mode of acceptance, an offeror not notified within a reasonable time may treat the offer as lapsed.

**2-205 firm offer [V].** An offer by a **merchant** to buy or sell goods, in a **signed writing** which by its terms gives **assurance that it will be held open**, is not revocable for lack of consideration during the time stated, or a reasonable time if none stated, **but never more than 3 months**. If the assurance is on a form supplied by the offeree, the offeror must **separately sign** it. (Past 3 months the offer survives as an ordinary revocable offer, unless supported by consideration.)

**2-207 [V text above].**
(1) A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance (or a written confirmation sent in a reasonable time) operates as an acceptance although it states terms additional to or different from those offered/agreed, **unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on assent to the additional or different terms**.
(2) Additional terms are proposals. **Between merchants** they become part of the contract **unless** (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms; (b) they **materially alter** it; or (c) notification of objection has been or is given within a reasonable time.
(3) If the writings do not form a contract but **conduct by both parties recognizes a contract**, the contract consists of the terms on which the writings agree **plus UCC gap-fillers**.

**How to analyze (tile order):** Step 1 Is there an offer? Step 2 Is the response a **definite, seasonable expression of acceptance**? Does it have the "expressly conditional" proviso (strictly construed: it must clearly show the offeree is unwilling to proceed absent assent; "subject to the terms on the back" is not enough; Dorton v. Collins & Aikman)? If expressly conditional, it is a counteroffer (then go to (3) if the parties perform). Step 3 Contract formed under (1): are the extra terms **additional** (offer silent) or **different** (offer addresses the subject, response contradicts)? Step 4 Additional terms: non-merchant involved? then proposals only, not part of the contract unless assented to. Both merchants? in unless (a), (b), (c). Step 5 Different terms: **majority = knockout rule**: conflicting terms cancel; UCC gap-fillers supply the term [U-majority; Comment 6, R; Daitom v. Pennwalt (10th Cir.), Northrop v. Litronic (7th Cir.) per secondary sources]. Minority: treat different terms like additional terms under (2), or offeror's terms control. Step 6 If no contract by writings: conduct under (3).

**Material alteration** [R, Comment 4/5]: a term that would "result in surprise or hardship if incorporated without express awareness by the other party." Typical materially altering: clause negating standard warranties (merchantability/fitness), arbitration clause (most courts), reserving the right to cancel, requiring a guaranty, shifting risk. Typical not material: clause setting interest on overdue invoices, limiting remedy in a reasonable manner, customary trade terms, a reasonable time for complaints. The party resisting the term has the burden to show materiality.

**Confirmations.** 2-207(1) also covers a written confirmation of an earlier oral agreement; extra terms analyzed under (2).

**Traps**
1. Common-law mirror image vs 2-207: if both are goods, 2-207 applies; the acceptance with a different price is still an acceptance (price dickered term is usually "different", knockout leaves reasonable price... note: courts usually say a different **price** is a core term and no acceptance because acceptance must be "definite"; keep to quantity/ship date/warranty/arbitration examples).
2. Merchant-ness matters only for additional terms in (2).
3. "Expressly conditional" is hard to satisfy.
4. The knockout applies to **different** terms; **additional** terms go through (2)(a)-(c).
5. Contract by conduct (3) excludes both sides' extra terms; gap-fillers apply.
6. A **firm offer** survives 3 months only; a **signed** writing by a **merchant**.

**Items**

**C3-01 (2-206).** Retailer faxed Wholesaler: "Ship 500 size-M polo shirts immediately, $6 each." Wholesaler shipped 500 size-L shirts the next day with no accompanying note. Retailer sues for breach. Wholesaler argues no contract was formed because the shirts shipped did not match the order. Result?
A. No contract; an acceptance must conform to the offer.
B. A contract was formed when Wholesaler shipped, and shipment of nonconforming goods was also a breach.
C. A contract was formed; there is no breach because Retailer can reject.
D. No contract; the SoF is not met.
**Best: B.** 2-206(1)(b): prompt shipment of nonconforming goods is an acceptance and a breach, unless the seller seasonably notifies that it is only an accommodation. A states the common-law mirror image rule. D: the fax is a signed writing probably, and the issue is not tested here.

**C3-02 (2-205).** A computer wholesaler's president (a merchant) signed and e-mailed an offer to sell 200 laptops at $700: "This offer will remain open for six months." Four months later, before acceptance, the wholesaler revoked. The buyer accepted in month five. Is there a contract?
A. Yes, the offer was irrevocable for six months.
B. Yes, the offer was irrevocable for a reasonable time.
C. No, firm offers are irrevocable at most three months; after that the wholesaler could revoke and did.
D. No, firm offers require consideration.
**Best: C.** The revocation came in month four, after the 3-month cap had expired, so it was effective. (If revoked in month two, ineffective.) D is the common-law rule.

**C3-03 (2-207 additional term; arbitration).** Buyer (a retailer) faxed a purchase order for 300 chairs. The order did not mention arbitration. Seller (a manufacturer) sent an acknowledgment accepting the order and adding on the reverse side: "All disputes shall be arbitrated in Seller's home state." Buyer never responded. Seller shipped and Buyer accepted. A dispute arose. Is Buyer bound to arbitrate?
A. Yes; Buyer's silence assented to the term.
B. Yes; between merchants, additional terms become part of the contract.
C. No; the arbitration clause is a proposal that materially alters the contract, so it does not become part of the contract.
D. No; there is no contract because the acknowledgment varied the offer.
**Best: C.** Contract formed under 2-207(1) (acceptance not expressly conditional); arbitration clause is an additional term; materially alters (surprise or hardship; most courts treat arbitration clauses as material alterations), so excluded under (2)(b). D is the mirror-image rule. B ignores the exceptions.

**C3-04 (expressly conditional).** Same facts, but Seller's acknowledgment says, "Seller's acceptance is expressly conditional on Buyer's assent to the terms on this form, including arbitration." Buyer never responded. Seller shipped and Buyer took the chairs and paid. Which terms govern?
A. Seller's form in full; Buyer's conduct manifests assent.
B. Buyer's purchase order in full; Seller's form is a rejected counteroffer.
C. A contract exists by conduct (2-207(3)); its terms are those on which the writings agree plus UCC gap-fillers; the arbitration clause is out.
D. No contract exists; Buyer must return the chairs.
**Best: C.** The proviso makes Seller's form a counteroffer; Buyer didn't assent; conduct forms a contract under (3). A would require assent beyond mere acceptance of goods. D is wrong because conduct establishes a contract.

**C3-05 (different terms; knockout).** Buyer's PO: "Delivery by June 1." Seller's acknowledgment (not expressly conditional): "Delivery by June 30." Both are merchants; neither said more. Seller delivers June 20, and Buyer sues for breach, citing the June 1 term. Assume a reasonable delivery time for this order is about a month. Under the majority rule?
A. Buyer wins; the offer's terms control.
B. Seller wins; the acceptance's delivery date is part of the contract.
C. Seller wins; the conflicting delivery terms cancel each other and the UCC gap-filler (a reasonable time) supplies the term; June 20 was within a reasonable time on these facts.
D. No contract; the delivery dates conflict.
**Best: C.** Knockout (majority). Item writer must say "under the majority rule" because a minority treats the offeror's term as controlling.

**C3-06 (offer limits acceptance).** Buyer's PO provided: "This offer is limited to the terms stated; any additional or different terms are rejected." Seller's acknowledgment (not conditional) added a clause disclaiming implied warranties. Does the disclaimer bind Buyer?
A. Yes; between merchants additional terms become part of the contract.
B. No; the offer expressly limited acceptance to its own terms.
C. Yes; Buyer didn't object.
D. No; acceptances cannot add terms.
**Best: B.** 2-207(2)(a). (Independent of materiality, though the disclaimer would also materially alter.)

**C3-07 (non-merchant).** A homeowner (non-merchant) e-mailed a seller of patio sets a signed order for a $2,400 set. The seller's confirmation, not conditional, added a "no returns" clause. Does the clause bind the homeowner?
A. Yes; all additional terms in an acceptance become part of the contract.
B. No; between a merchant and a non-merchant, additional terms are only proposals, and the homeowner did not assent.
C. Yes, because the homeowner didn't object.
D. No; the confirmation was not a contract.
**Best: B.**


## 10. Statute of frauds (II.F) [R common law; V UCC 2-201, 2-209(3), E-SIGN 7001/7003]

**Common-law categories (mnemonic MY LEG):**
- **M**arriage: a promise **made in consideration of marriage** (prenup, a promise to pay money if one marries), NOT mutual promises to marry.
- **Y**ear: contracts that **by their terms cannot be performed within one year from the making**. Test: is there any possible way to perform within a year? If yes, outside the statute (even if unlikely). Lifetime contracts (can end by death within a year) are outside; "2 years" fixed term inside; contracts terminable at will or by an event that could occur in under a year are outside. Full performance by one side generally removes the bar for the other side's promise (majority).
- **L**and: sale or transfer of an interest in real property (sale, mortgage, easement, lease for more than a year [typical]). Not a contract to build on land.
- **E**xecutor/administrator: promise by a personal representative to pay the estate's debts from personal funds.
- **G**oods: sale of goods for $500+ [UCC 2-201]. Do not confuse with "G" for guaranty.
- Surety: promise to answer for the debt or default of another (collateral promise). Not within the statute: **primary** promise, **indemnity**, and the **main purpose / leading object rule** (promisor's main purpose is to secure an economic benefit for himself).

**Writing requirements:** any signed writing, letters, e-mails, receipts, or several related writings; signed by the **party to be charged** (not both parties) or agent; reasonably identifies the parties, subject matter, and essential terms (land: the property; price and terms for a land-sale usually); consideration need be recited only for surety (modern: not required). "Signed" = any symbol adopted with present intent to authenticate [V UCC 1-201(b)(37)]. Missing a term is fatal only if the term is essential.

**Exceptions / substitutes (common law):**
- **Part performance (land):** buyer's possession plus either payment of part or substantial improvements (the majority requirement; payment alone is insufficient). Specific performance available.
- **Full performance by one side** (one-year provision, majority).
- **Promissory/equitable estoppel** (Restatement §139, limited to cases where injustice requires enforcement).
- **Restitution** for benefits conferred (value, not contract price) remains available despite the statute [see C2-11].
- Effect of non-compliance: **unenforceable, not void**; defense must be raised; oral rescission is allowed; a modification that brings the contract within the statute needs a writing.
- Agents: "equal dignities" rule for real estate contracts.

**UCC 2-201 [V]:**
- Contract for sale of goods for **$500 or more** is not enforceable "by way of action or defense" without a writing **sufficient to indicate that a contract for sale has been made**, **signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought** (or authorized agent/broker). Writing is not insufficient merely because it omits or misstates a term, but the contract is **enforceable only to the quantity shown**. (So **quantity** is the one essential term.)
- **Merchant's confirmation (2-201(2)):** between merchants, a written confirmation sufficient against the sender, received in a reasonable time, binds the recipient unless he gives **written objection within 10 days of receipt**. Effect: it only removes the SoF defense; the confirming party must still prove the oral contract was made.
- **Exceptions (2-201(3)):** (a) goods **specially manufactured** for the buyer and not suitable for sale to others in the ordinary course of the seller's business, with the seller having substantially begun manufacture or commitments before notice of repudiation; (b) the party **admits in pleading, testimony or otherwise in court** that a contract was made (enforceable to the quantity admitted); (c) goods **for which payment has been made and accepted or which have been received and accepted** (enforceable only to that extent).
- **Modification** of a contract within 2-201 must satisfy 2-201 [V 2-209(3)].

**Electronic transactions (NextGen explicit; MBE: good to know).** E-SIGN (15 U.S.C. 7001) [V]: for transactions in or affecting interstate/foreign commerce, a signature, contract or other record may not be denied legal effect solely because it is electronic. E-SIGN's exception removes **UCC** matters **other than UCC 1-107, 1-206, and Articles 2 and 2A** [V 15 U.S.C. 7003(a)(3)], i.e., **E-SIGN does apply to sale-of-goods contracts**; it also excludes wills, family law, certain court documents and notices (utility cutoffs, foreclosure/eviction on primary residence, health insurance cancellation, product recalls). **UETA** (adopted by nearly every state) [R] similarly gives electronic records/signatures legal effect where the parties have agreed to conduct the transaction electronically (agreement determined from context and conduct), attributes a signature to a person if it was the act of the person, covers automated "electronic agents," and sets rules on when a record is sent/received. A typed name at the end of an e-mail, a click-through "I accept," or a voice signature can be a signature.

**Traps**
- Services contracts never fall under 2-201 even if >$500.
- Merchant's confirmation: check **both merchants**, **received**, **no written objection within 10 days**; it does not prove the oral deal.
- The SoF is about **enforceability**, not whether a contract was formed; admission, estoppel, and restitution survive.
- Suretyship vs. primary promise: "I will pay if he doesn't" is surety; "Deliver to him and bill me" is primary.
- One-year: can the contract be performed in under a year, not **will** it?
- Land: part performance requires more than a payment.

**Items**

**C3-08 (suretyship; main purpose).** A subcontractor told a general contractor he would stop work on a hospital job because the supplier was not being paid. The GC orally promised the supplier, "If you keep shipping to the subcontractor, I'll pay you what he owes." The supplier shipped $60,000 of materials. The GC, now refusing, asserts the SoF. Likely result?
A. For the GC; a promise to pay another's debt must be in writing.
B. For the GC; the supplier gave no consideration.
C. For the supplier; the GC's main purpose was to secure completion of her own project, an exception to the surety provision.
D. For the supplier; oral promises to pay debts are never within the SoF.
**Best: C.** Main-purpose rule. A states the general rule. D is false.

**C3-09 (one year).** Ellen orally agreed to work as the exclusive photographer for Hotel Corp. "for 18 months, starting next month, at $4,000 per month." After two months Hotel fires her without cause. Ellen sues. Result?
A. Ellen wins; employment contracts are outside the SoF.
B. Ellen loses; the contract cannot be performed within one year from the making and is not evidenced by a signed writing.
C. Ellen wins; she began performance.
D. Ellen loses; the contract is unconscionable.
**Best: B.** Fixed term of 18 months from a start next month cannot be performed within a year. C: part performance by Ellen does not remove the bar for a one-year contract (full performance by one side does, majority). Variation: "for life" or "as long as the hotel stays open" could be performed within a year. Ellen's alternative: restitution for the 2 months.

**C3-10 (land; part performance).** Orally, Sellers agreed to sell Buyer a farm for $300,000. Buyer paid a $30,000 deposit, but did not take possession. Sellers then refused to close. Buyer seeks specific performance. Result?
A. For Buyer; payment of part of the price is part performance.
B. For Buyer; oral agreements to sell land are enforceable in equity.
C. For Sellers; payment alone is insufficient part performance; Buyer may seek restitution of the $30,000.
D. For Sellers; Buyer is entitled to nothing.
**Best: C.** Majority requires possession (or substantial improvements) in addition to payment. D ignores restitution.

**C3-11 (UCC merchant confirmation).** Two lumber dealers orally agreed that Dealer A would sell Dealer B 40,000 board feet of oak for $64,000. A day later B faxed A a signed letter: "Confirming our deal: 40,000 bf oak at $1.60." A received it, did not respond, and one month later refused to deliver, asserting the SoF. B sues. Result?
A. For A; A signed nothing.
B. For A; the oral contract is unenforceable absent A's signature.
C. For B; A failed to object in writing within 10 days, so the SoF defense is unavailable, but B still must prove the oral contract was made.
D. For B; the letter itself proves the contract.
**Best: C.** 2-201(2). D overstates.

**C3-12 (specially manufactured goods).** Buyer orally ordered 5,000 tote bags printed with Buyer's own logo from Printer for $20,000. Printer printed 3,000 bags before Buyer repudiated. The bags cannot be sold to anyone else in Printer's ordinary course of business. Buyer asserts the statute of frauds. Is Printer's action barred?
A. Yes; there is no signed writing.
B. Yes; the exception for specially manufactured goods applies only to goods already delivered.
C. No; the specially manufactured goods exception applies because Printer substantially began manufacture before receiving notice of repudiation.
D. No; the SoF does not apply to custom goods under any circumstances.
**Best: C.** 2-201(3)(a). (Keep damages out of this item: Printer's measure is a separate question, 2-704 / 2-708.) B misstates the exception; D overstates it (the conditions matter: not suitable for others, substantial beginning).

**C3-13 (writing missing price).** Seller signed a paper stating, "I'll sell Buyer my 200 used pallets." No price or delivery date was written. Buyer sues when Seller refuses. The goods are worth $1,500. Seller asserts the SoF. Result?
A. Seller wins; the writing omits the price.
B. Seller wins; a writing must contain all essential terms.
C. Buyer wins; the writing indicates a contract for sale, is signed, and states quantity; omitted terms do not defeat it.
D. Buyer wins; the SoF does not apply to used goods.
**Best: C.** 2-201(1): quantity is the only required term; price is supplied by 2-305.

**C3-14 (e-signature).** Dana e-mailed Eli: "Confirming that I'll sell you the 12-acre pasture for $240,000. Closing May 1. -Dana Park." Eli replied "Agreed." Dana later argues that the SoF requires a paper contract signed in ink. Result?
A. Dana wins; land contracts require manuscript signatures.
B. Dana wins; E-SIGN excludes land contracts.
C. Eli can satisfy the writing requirement; a typed name on an e-mail can be a signature, and electronic records are not denied effect merely because they are electronic.
D. Eli loses because Eli's "Agreed" did not repeat the terms.
**Best: C.** (E-SIGN does not exclude real estate sales as a category; the §7003 exceptions do not list them; D is irrelevant because the SoF is enforced against the party to be charged, Dana.) [Flag U: UETA vs. E-SIGN preemption and "consent to electronic transactions" nuance (parties' agreement to transact electronically) is tested on NextGen in resource-provided form.]

# PART C. DEFENSES (MBE II.A-E)

General: defenses make a contract **void** (no legal effect, e.g., physical compulsion, fraud in the factum, illegal subject matter, adjudicated incompetence in some states) or **voidable** (power of avoidance in the protected party: minority, mistake, misrepresentation, duress by threat, undue influence). Voidable contracts can be **ratified**. The result of avoiding is normally rescission plus restitution. UCC 1-103(b) preserves these common-law defenses for sales [V: NextGen sourcebook quotes 1-103(b)].

## 11. Incapacity (II.A) [R; V NextGen scope: minors, mentally impaired, guardianship, drugs/alcohol, disaffirmance, ratification]

- **Minors (under 18 in nearly all states):** contract is **voidable at the minor's option**. Disaffirm during minority or within a reasonable time after reaching majority. The adult counterparty has no power to avoid. On disaffirmance of an executed contract the minor must return the consideration he still has (majority rule: no further liability for depreciation or damage; a minority applies a "benefit" rule). **Necessaries** (food, clothing, shelter, medical care): the minor is liable in quasi-contract for **reasonable value**, not the contract price. **Ratification** after majority by words or conduct (e.g., keeping and using the item for an unreasonable time after turning 18, making payments). Misrepresenting age: varies.
- **Mental illness or defect:** voidable if, because of mental illness or defect, the person is **unable to understand the nature and consequences** of the transaction (cognitive test, traditional) or (modern, Restatement §15) is unable to act reasonably in relation to it **and** the other party has reason to know. After a **court adjudication / guardianship**, the contract is **void** in many states (voidable in others). Necessaries: reasonable value. Capacity is judged **at the time of contracting**; ratification is possible on regaining capacity.
- **Intoxication / drugs:** voidable at the option of the intoxicated party **if the other party had reason to know** the condition prevented understanding or reasonable action (§16). Ratification upon sobriety.
- UCC: capacity left to common law (1-103(b)).

**Traps:** a minor can bind the adult, not vice versa; minor restores only what remains (majority) and need not pay for damage; necessaries = reasonable value; an unwise bargain is not incapacity.

**Items**

**C4-01.** Jules, 17, paid $5,000 cash to a dealer for a used motorcycle and wrecked it a month later. The next week he told the dealer he disaffirms. The dealer says Jules must pay for the damage and cannot recover the price. In a majority-rule jurisdiction, Jules may recover?
A. Nothing; he damaged the goods.
B. The $5,000 price, returning the wrecked motorcycle.
C. The $5,000 price less depreciation and damage.
D. Nothing; motorcycles are necessaries.
**Best: B.** A minor may disaffirm and must return what remains; he is not liable for damage under the majority rule. C is the minority "benefit" rule. D: a motorcycle is not a necessary.

**C4-02.** After turning 18, Kate kept the laptop she had contracted for at 17, paid every monthly installment for 14 months, and then told the seller she disaffirmed. Result?
A. Kate may disaffirm; she was a minor when she contracted.
B. Kate may not; she ratified by conduct and unreasonable delay after majority.
C. Kate may disaffirm only if the laptop was defective.
D. Kate may not because minors cannot disaffirm installment contracts.
**Best: B.**

**C4-03.** A 16-year-old agreed to rent an apartment for $900 a month, signed a one-year lease, and moved in. After 4 months she moved out and disaffirmed. The landlord sues for the remaining 8 months' rent. Result?
A. For the landlord for all 8 months; she is bound.
B. For the landlord only for the reasonable value of the housing she actually used (a necessary).
C. For the minor; a minor can never be liable in any amount.
D. For the landlord for 12 months because shelter is a necessary.
**Best: B.**

**C4-04.** A court placed Hal, an adult with dementia, under a plenary guardianship. Later, without the guardian's knowledge, Hal agreed to sell his car to Tim for $2,000 less than its value. Tim did not know of the guardianship. The guardian seeks to avoid the sale. Assume the jurisdiction treats contracts made after adjudication as void. Result?
A. Enforceable; Tim acted in good faith.
B. Not enforceable against Hal, regardless of Tim's knowledge.
C. Enforceable unless Hal proves he could not understand.
D. Enforceable; the price was only slightly below value.
**Best: B.** [U: states differ void/voidable; the stem must say which.]

**C4-05 (intoxication).** Maya, visibly drunk at a party, signed a contract to sell her motorcycle for $1,000 to Ned, who knew she was impaired. Next morning she refuses. Result?
A. Binding; voluntary intoxication is never a defense.
B. Voidable by Maya because Ned had reason to know her condition prevented understanding.
C. Void from inception.
D. Binding unless Maya returns the $1,000.
**Best: B.** If Ned had no reason to know, she would be bound.

## 12. Duress and undue influence (II.B) [R]

- **Duress by improper threat** (§175): assent induced by an **improper threat** that **leaves the victim no reasonable alternative** → voidable. Improper threats: a crime or tort; criminal prosecution; bad-faith civil process; **a threat of breach in bad faith to extract a contract modification** (economic duress: A threatens to withhold a performance already due, in bad faith, when B cannot get an alternative in time). A threat to do what one has a legal right to do (e.g., sue on a valid claim) is **not** improper unless made in bad faith or the exchange is grossly unfair.
- **Physical compulsion** (hand guided to sign) → **void**.
- **Undue influence** (§177): unfair persuasion of a party who is under the domination of the persuader or who, because of a relationship, is justified in assuming the persuader won't act inconsistently with the party's welfare → voidable. Look for a **confidential or fiduciary relationship**, vulnerability (age, illness), isolation, haste, no independent advice, grossly unfair terms. A presumption arises in fiduciary relations where the dominant party benefits.
- Remedy: avoidance/rescission and restitution; ratification by acting freely after the pressure ends.

**Traps:** "no reasonable alternative" is required; a hard bargain is not duress; threatening a valid lawsuit is not duress; economic duress needs wrongful (bad-faith) conduct.

**C4-06.** A company discovered that employee Lou had embezzled $10,000. His boss told him, "Sign this promissory note for $30,000 or I'll call the police." Lou signed. The company sues on the note. Result?
A. Enforceable; Lou in fact owed $10,000.
B. Voidable by Lou; a threat of criminal prosecution to obtain a contract is an improper threat.
C. Void; a note cannot be signed under pressure.
D. Enforceable; Lou had a reasonable alternative (calling a lawyer).
**Best: B.** (Contrast: "Pay the $10,000 you owe or I will sue" is a threat to exercise a legal right.)

**C4-07 (economic duress).** A week before a school-year deadline, a contractor told a school district, "Unless you pay an extra $200,000, I'll stop work." The contractor had no excuse; no other contractor could finish in time. The district signed the modification. Later it refuses to pay the extra. Result?
A. Contractor wins; the district agreed in writing.
B. District wins; the modification is voidable for duress and, at common law, also lacks consideration.
C. Contractor wins; the district could have sued.
D. District loses; economic pressure is not duress.
**Best: B.** Bad-faith threat of breach + no reasonable alternative.

**C4-08 (undue influence).** Opal, 84, ill and dependent on her nurse Quinn, signed a deed transferring her house to Quinn for $1 after Quinn kept visitors away and urged haste, with no legal advice. Opal's niece sues to set it aside. Result?
A. Valid; Opal signed voluntarily.
B. Voidable for undue influence given the dependent relationship, isolation, haste, and unfair terms.
C. Valid unless Opal was legally incompetent.
D. Void for lack of consideration.
**Best: B.**

## 13. Mistake and misunderstanding (II.C) [R; V NextGen scope list]

- **Mutual mistake (§152):** both parties are mistaken about a **basic assumption** on which the contract was made; the mistake has a **material effect on the agreed exchange**; and the party seeking avoidance **does not bear the risk** of the mistake → voidable by the adversely affected party. **Risk allocation (§154):** a party bears the risk if the agreement allocates it to him, if he was **aware of limited knowledge** and treated it as sufficient ("conscious ignorance"), or if the court reasonably allocates it. Mistakes about market **value** or future events are usually risks, not defenses; mistakes about the existence, identity or essential quality of the subject matter can qualify (the classic: both think a cow is barren and she is in fact fertile; by contrast, a seller who sells an unidentified stone for $1, knowing she does not know what it is, bears the risk through conscious ignorance).
- **Unilateral mistake (§153):** voidable only if (i) basic assumption + material effect, (ii) party doesn't bear risk, **and** (iii) **enforcement would be unconscionable** or the **other party had reason to know of the mistake or caused it**. Classic: bid with an obvious clerical error.
- **Misunderstanding (§20)** (Raffles v. Wichelhaus: two ships "Peerless"): parties attach **different meanings** to a material term and **neither knows or has reason to know** the other's meaning → **no contract**. If one party knew or had reason to know the other's meaning, the contract is formed on that **other** party's meaning.
- **Scrivener's error:** the writing misstates the actual agreement because of a mutual mistake (or one party's mistake the other knows of) → **reformation**, proved by clear and convincing evidence; the parol evidence rule does not bar the proof.
- **Failure to read:** a party who signs without reading is generally bound, absent fraud.

**Traps:** mutual vs unilateral; conscious ignorance shifts risk; value mistakes are risks; **misunderstanding = no contract** (different remedy from mistake); avoidance needs prompt action and restoration.

**C4-09.** Both parties believed a painting sold for $90,000 was by a famous artist. The seller said: "I found it in the attic; I make no representation about who painted it." It proved to be a copy worth $2,000. The buyer seeks to avoid. Result?
A. Buyer wins; mutual mistake about a basic assumption.
B. Buyer loses; the buyer bore the risk because the seller disclaimed authenticity and the buyer proceeded despite conscious uncertainty.
C. Buyer wins; the seller got a windfall.
D. Buyer loses; mistake is never a ground for avoidance.
**Best: B.** (Without the disclaimer facts, A.)

**C4-10.** Sub submitted a $400,000 bid for a plumbing subcontract, omitting a $100,000 line by arithmetic error. The next lowest bid was $520,000. The GC accepted Sub's bid at once. Sub asks to rescind. Result?
A. Enforceable; unilateral mistakes never excuse.
B. Sub may avoid; the GC had reason to know of the mistake given the bid's obvious deviation.
C. Sub may avoid only if the mistake was mutual.
D. Enforceable; Sub's negligence bars relief.
**Best: B.**

**C4-11 (misunderstanding).** Seller offered to sell "the Marsh Street lot." Seller owned two lots on Marsh Street and meant the vacant one; Buyer meant the other. Neither knew of the other's meaning. Result?
A. Contract on Seller's meaning.
B. Contract on Buyer's meaning.
C. No contract; neither party knew of the other's different meaning.
D. Contract; the court chooses the more reasonable meaning.
**Best: C.** (If Seller had known of Buyer's meaning, the contract would be on Buyer's meaning.)

**C4-12 (scrivener's error).** Ann and Bo orally agreed that Ann would pay Bo $42,000 for equipment. When the written contract was prepared, a typist wrote "$24,000." Both signed without noticing. Ann refuses to pay more than $24,000. Bo's best claim?
A. Reformation to $42,000; the parol evidence rule is no bar to proof of the mistake.
B. Nothing; the writing is integrated.
C. Only $24,000; the writing controls.
D. Rescission only; reformation is unavailable.
**Best: A.**

## 14. Fraud, misrepresentation, nondisclosure (II.D) [R; V UCC 2-721; V NextGen scope]

- **Misrepresentation** (§159): an assertion **not in accord with the facts**. A contract is **voidable** where (i) there is a misrepresentation that is **fraudulent or material**, (ii) it **induced** assent, and (iii) reliance was **justifiable** (§164). **Fraudulent** = knowledge or reckless disregard of falsity and intent to induce reliance. **Material** = likely to induce a reasonable person to assent (or the maker knows it will induce this recipient). A fraudulent misrepresentation need not be material; an **innocent** one must be.
- **Fraud in the inducement** (usual case) → voidable. **Fraud in the factum** (recipient doesn't know the character or essential terms of the writing and has no reasonable opportunity to learn) → **void**.
- **Reliance** need not be careful; it is not barred merely because the recipient could have investigated, unless the falsity is obvious (§172).
- **Opinion and puffery** are generally not actionable, except by one with superior knowledge/expertise being relied upon, a fiduciary, or an opinion implying facts.
- **Nondisclosure / concealment:** silence isn't a misrepresentation **except** (§161) (a) to prevent a prior assertion from misleading (half-truths), (b) where silence breaches good faith about a fact the other is mistaken on as a basic assumption (modern trend: sellers of homes must disclose known latent material defects), (c) correcting a mistake about the contents or effect of a writing, (d) fiduciary or confidential relationship. **Active concealment** counts as an assertion.
- **Remedies:** avoidance/rescission with restitution; **tort damages for fraud** when scienter exists; rescission doesn't bar damages. UCC: remedies for fraud include all remedies for non-fraudulent breach, and neither rescission nor rejection bars damages [V 2-721].
- Merger and non-reliance clauses do not automatically bar fraud claims; the parol evidence rule does not exclude proof of fraud.

**Traps:** puffery vs fact; justifiable reliance; silence vs half-truth vs concealment; void vs voidable; innocent misrepresentation (rescission only) vs fraudulent (rescission + damages).

**C4-13.** A seller told a buyer, "The furnace was installed new last year," knowing it was installed in 1998. The buyer had a chance to inspect but did not. After closing, the furnace failed. Buyer seeks rescission and damages. Result?
A. Buyer loses; he could have inspected.
B. Buyer wins; fraudulent misrepresentation of fact, justifiably relied upon.
C. Buyer loses; the seller made an opinion.
D. Buyer gets rescission only.
**Best: B.** (D is correct for an innocent misrepresentation.)

**C4-14 (puffery).** A used-boat seller said, "She's the sweetest-running boat on the lake, and a steal at this price." The motor has a chronic overheating problem the seller never noticed. Buyer sues for misrepresentation. Result?
A. Buyer wins; the statements were false.
B. Seller wins; sales talk and opinion are not actionable misrepresentations of fact.
C. Buyer wins; sellers must disclose everything.
D. Seller wins only if Buyer inspected.
**Best: B.**

**C4-15 (concealment).** Seller knew the basement flooded each spring. Without mentioning it, Seller painted over water stains before showing the house. Buyer did not ask. After closing, the basement flooded. Result?
A. Seller wins; caveat emptor and Buyer didn't ask.
B. Buyer wins; active concealment is treated as a misrepresentation.
C. Seller wins; Buyer had constructive notice.
D. Buyer wins only if there was a written warranty.
**Best: B.** (In a jurisdiction recognizing a duty to disclose known latent defects, mere silence would also qualify.)

**C4-16 (fraud in the factum).** A salesperson told an elderly customer who could not read English that the paper she was signing was a receipt for a free sample; it was a purchase contract. Applying the rule that fraud in the factum renders a contract void, result?
A. Void; she had no reasonable opportunity to learn the character of the writing.
B. Voidable for fraud in the inducement only.
C. Binding; one has a duty to read.
D. Binding; she received the sample.
**Best: A.** [U: stem must name the void/voidable classification.]

## 15. Illegality, unconscionability, public policy (II.E) [R; V UCC 2-302 text]

- **Illegality:** a bargain is **unenforceable** if performance or consideration is illegal or violates public policy. Courts generally leave the parties where they are. Exceptions: (i) **a party who is not at fault** (ignorant of the facts making it illegal, or in the class the statute protects) may recover; (ii) **severability**: enforce the legal part if the illegal part isn't essential; (iii) **withdrawal** before performance of the illegal act → restitution; (iv) **licensing** statutes: if the license is to protect the public, an unlicensed provider can't recover (often not even in quasi-contract); if for revenue only, the contract is enforceable.
- **Covenants not to compete:** enforceable only if **ancillary** to a legitimate transaction (employment, sale of a business), **reasonable** in time, geography and scope, and protecting a legitimate business interest. Overbroad covenants: refused, or (many states) enforced to the extent reasonable (blue pencil/reformation) [U: varies by state].
- **Exculpatory clauses:** typically unenforceable for intentional or grossly negligent misconduct, where a public duty or special relationship exists, or if unconscionable.
- **Unconscionability** (UCC 2-302; Restatement §208; common law) [V statute text]: if the court "as a matter of law" finds the contract or any clause unconscionable, it may **refuse to enforce the contract, enforce the remainder without the clause, or limit the clause**; parties get a reasonable opportunity to present evidence of commercial setting, purpose and effect. **Procedural** (unfair surprise, adhesion, hidden terms, high-pressure, no meaningful choice, vastly unequal bargaining power) and **substantive** (grossly one-sided terms, price grossly disproportionate); most courts want both on a sliding scale; judged **at formation**; decided by the judge. [R: Comment 1: aim is prevention of oppression and unfair surprise, not disturbing the allocation of risks because of superior bargaining power.] [V NextGen scope: "procedural unconscionability and substantive unconscionability", "under the common law and under the UCC".]

**Traps:** a hard bargain isn't unconscionable; clause vs whole contract remedy; licensing public-protection vs revenue; judged at formation.

**C4-17 (licensing).** Zed, an unlicensed electrician, installed wiring for Mia under a $12,000 contract. The state licensing law exists to protect the public and says unlicensed persons "may not maintain any action for compensation." Mia refuses to pay. Result?
A. Zed wins; Mia received the benefit.
B. Mia wins; the licensing law protects the public, so the contract is unenforceable by Zed.
C. Zed wins; licensing laws are for revenue only.
D. Zed wins in quantum meruit despite the statute.
**Best: B.**

**C4-18 (noncompete).** An employee of a regional bakery signed a covenant barring her from working "for any food business anywhere in the United States for 10 years" after leaving. She joins a competitor in the same town. Likely result?
A. Enforceable; she signed.
B. Unenforceable as written; the covenant exceeds what is reasonable to protect the bakery's legitimate interest (some courts would enforce a narrower version).
C. Enforceable; noncompetes are always valid in employment.
D. Unenforceable; employment contracts cannot contain restraints.
**Best: B.**

**C4-19 (unconscionability).** A door-to-door seller sold a $1,500 water filter on a $5,200 installment plan to a low-income buyer with limited English, telling him only "sign here," with a tiny-print clause making every item he ever bought from the seller collateral for each debt. The buyer defaults and asserts unconscionability. Likely result?
A. Enforceable in full; the buyer signed.
B. A court may refuse to enforce the contract or the clause; the procedural and substantive factors are both strong.
C. Void for lack of mutual assent.
D. Unenforceable only if the seller intended to deceive.
**Best: B.** (Intent is not an element.)

**C4-20 (severability).** A franchise contract contains a lawful supply promise and an unlawful price-fixing clause. A party refuses to perform the lawful promise because of the illegal clause. Result?
A. Whole contract void.
B. The court may enforce the lawful promise and sever the illegal clause if it is not essential to the bargain.
C. Whole contract fully enforceable.
D. Restitution only.
**Best: B.**

# PART D. CONTENT AND MEANING (MBE III) AND WARRANTIES (IV.D-E)

## 16. Parol evidence rule (III.A) [R; V UCC 2-202 text; V NextGen scope]

**What it is.** A rule of **substantive law**. When parties adopt a writing as the **final expression** of some or all of their agreement (an **integration**), evidence of **prior or contemporaneous** agreements (oral or written) is not admissible to **contradict** the writing; if the writing is a **complete and exclusive** statement (**completely integrated**), such evidence cannot even **supplement** it with consistent additional terms. If **partially integrated**, consistent additional terms may be shown but contradictory ones may not.

**Steps (tile order):** (1) Is there a writing intended as the final expression of at least some terms? (2) Is the evidence of a **prior or contemporaneous** agreement (not a later modification)? (3) Is the writing **completely** or **partially** integrated? (4) Does the evidence **contradict** (barred in either case), **supplement with a consistent additional term** (barred only if complete integration), or **explain/interpret** (allowed if ambiguity, or by UCC usage)? (5) Does an **exception** apply?

**Complete vs partial:** a **merger clause** ("this is the entire agreement") is strong evidence of complete integration, but not conclusive, and does not bar proof of fraud, mistake, etc. **Modern/Restatement/Corbin approach (majority):** the judge may consider all relevant circumstances, including the alleged side agreement, to decide integration; **traditional four-corners (Williston):** decide from the face of the writing. Test for consistent additional terms: such terms as might **naturally be omitted** from the writing (Restatement §216(2)(b)); the traditional/UCC comment version: would **certainly** have been included. [U: which standard NCBE uses; both lead to the same result for typical items; flag in stem if needed.]

**Exceptions (evidence admissible despite integration):** (1) to show **defenses to formation or enforcement**: fraud, duress, undue influence, mistake, misrepresentation, illegality, incapacity, unconscionability, lack of consideration; (2) **existence of a condition precedent to the effectiveness** of the contract (oral condition on whether the contract takes effect); (3) **ambiguity** (to interpret; the majority permits extrinsic evidence to show ambiguity, a minority requires facial ambiguity ("plain meaning" rule)); (4) **subsequent** modifications or agreements (after the writing); (5) **reformation** (mutual mistake in the writing/ scrivener's error); (6) **collateral agreements** not within the writing's scope (separate consideration, naturally made separately), (7) **trade usage, course of dealing, course of performance** (UCC).

**UCC 2-202 [V].** Terms in a writing intended as final expression "may not be contradicted by evidence of any prior agreement or of a contemporaneous oral agreement but may be explained or supplemented (a) by course of dealing or usage of trade (1-303) or by course of performance (2-208); and (b) by evidence of consistent additional terms unless the court finds the writing to have been intended also as a complete and exclusive statement of the terms of the agreement." [R: Comment 1(c): no need to find ambiguity first; usage can supplement a completely integrated writing unless carefully negated.] The NextGen scope lists "Parol evidence under the UCC" as its own topic [V].

**Traps**
1. The rule bars **prior/contemporaneous** only; later oral modifications come in (but check consideration/SoF/NOM).
2. A **merger clause** doesn't stop fraud evidence.
3. Contradict vs supplement: a partially integrated writing + consistent term = OK; contradiction never.
4. Parol evidence rule isn't about **interpretation** evidence on ambiguity.
5. UCC: usage of trade and course of dealing can add terms even to a completely integrated writing.
6. Rule is only between the parties to the contract; third parties are generally not bound by it.

**Items**

**C5-01 (merger; complete integration).** Seller and Buyer signed a written contract for the sale of a boat for $20,000. It included: "This writing is the complete and exclusive statement of the agreement; no oral agreements exist." Buyer later sought to testify that before signing, Seller orally promised to include a trailer. Is the testimony admissible?
A. Yes; the promise was not contradicted by the writing.
B. Yes; a merger clause never bars evidence of earlier promises.
C. No; the writing is a complete integration, so evidence of prior agreements within its scope is barred, even if consistent.
D. No; oral promises are not enforceable for goods.
**Best: C.** (If the writing had been only partially integrated, a consistent trailer term could come in. D is wrong: the SoF is a separate issue.)

**C5-02 (fraud exception).** Same writing and merger clause. Buyer now claims Seller falsely said the boat had never been in a wreck. Is that evidence admissible?
A. No; the merger clause bars it.
B. No; it contradicts the integrated writing.
C. Yes; the parol evidence rule does not bar proof of fraud.
D. Yes, but only if the writing is silent about wrecks.
**Best: C.**

**C5-03 (condition to effectiveness).** Dee and Ed signed a written agreement for Dee to buy Ed's dental practice, but orally agreed beforehand that the agreement would not take effect unless Dee obtained bank financing by June 1. Dee did not. Ed sues. Is evidence of the oral financing condition admissible?
A. No; it contradicts the integrated writing.
B. No; it is a prior oral agreement.
C. Yes; it shows that a condition on the effectiveness of the contract was not met, rather than varying its terms.
D. Yes, but only if the writing is ambiguous.
**Best: C.**

**C5-04 (later modification).** A signed written contract for lawn care says $200 per month. Two months after signing, the parties orally agreed to $180 per month in exchange for the owner's paying six months in advance, which the owner did. The company now sues for $200 per month. Is the owner's evidence of the later oral agreement barred by the parol evidence rule?
A. Yes; the writing is integrated.
B. Yes; it contradicts the writing.
C. No; the rule excludes only prior and contemporaneous agreements, not later modifications.
D. No; services contracts are exempt from the rule.
**Best: C.** (Whether the modification is otherwise enforceable is a separate question: here the advance payment supplies new consideration; compare section 8.)

**C5-05 (UCC usage supplements).** A signed, complete contract for "500 cords of seasoned hardwood" did not define "seasoned." The buyer wants to introduce evidence that in the region "seasoned" means dried at least six months. The seller objects that the writing is complete and unambiguous. Result under the UCC?
A. Inadmissible; the writing is completely integrated.
B. Inadmissible; the term is unambiguous.
C. Admissible; usage of trade may explain or supplement a final writing even if it's completely integrated and without a threshold finding of ambiguity.
D. Admissible only if the buyer proves the seller knew of the usage.
**Best: C.** (Subject to the usage being regularly observed such that parties are held to have contracted with reference to it: 1-303(c); the question of existence and scope of usage is a fact.)

## 17. Interpretation (III.B) [R; V UCC 1-303, 2-208, 1-201(b)(3)]

- **Objective approach.** Give words their ordinary meaning in context; the court decides interpretation as a question of law unless it depends on credibility of extrinsic evidence.
- **Same-meaning / different-meaning rules (§201):** if both parties attach the same meaning, that meaning governs; if they attach different meanings and one party knew or had reason to know the meaning the **other** attached, the contract is read in the **other** party's sense (the party who knew bears the risk of the divergence); if neither knew, there is no contract (§20).
- **Canons:** read the contract as a whole and give effect to all terms; **specific terms control general**; **handwritten > typed > printed form terms**; **express terms > course of performance > course of dealing > usage of trade** [V UCC 1-303(e), 2-208(2)]; **ambiguities against the drafter** (contra proferentem; a last resort, typically not applied between sophisticated negotiating parties); prefer lawful and reasonable constructions; interpret to avoid forfeiture; public interest in preference to private gain; technical terms get technical meaning in the trade.
- **Course of performance** (CoP): repeated occasions for performance within the same contract, with knowledge and opportunity to object [V 1-303(a)]; **course of dealing** (CoD): prior transactions between the parties [V 1-303(b)]; **usage of trade** (UoT): regular practice in a trade [V 1-303(c)]. Express terms are read consistently if reasonable. CoP is also relevant to waiver/modification subject to 2-209 [V 2-208(3)].
- **Ambiguity** = reasonably susceptible to more than one meaning.

**Traps:** hierarchy; contra proferentem last; CoP isn't parol evidence (it comes after formation); "agreement" (bargain in fact) vs "contract" (legal obligation) [V 1-201(b)(3)].

**C5-06 (hierarchy).** A supply contract states: "Payment due within 30 days of invoice." For two years, the seller accepted payment on day 45 without protest. When the buyer pays on day 45 again, the seller declares a default. Under the UCC, the buyer's strongest argument is that?
A. Express terms never yield to course of performance.
B. The seller's acceptance of late payment without objection is relevant course of performance, which may show the parties meant to allow or waived strict compliance with the 30-day term (subject to the seller's right to retract the waiver on reasonable notice as to future installments).
C. Course of dealing overrides express terms.
D. Parol evidence is inadmissible.
**Best: B.** Express terms prevail over CoP only where they can't be reasonably reconciled; CoP also shows waiver (2-208(3), 2-209(4)-(5)).

**C5-07 (contra proferentem).** A negotiated contract between two large companies contained an ambiguous indemnity clause drafted by Company A's lawyer, with both sides negotiating the language. Company B argues the clause must be construed against Company A. Result?
A. B wins automatically.
B. The clause is first interpreted using the parties' intent and other evidence; contra proferentem is a last resort and often is not applied to a jointly negotiated clause between sophisticated parties.
C. A wins; ambiguity favors the drafter.
D. B wins; contra proferentem is the first step.
**Best: B.**

**C5-08 (typed vs form).** A printed form lease says "tenant pays all utilities." A typed rider inserted by the parties says "landlord pays water." Which controls on water?
A. The printed term because it came first.
B. The typed rider, because specific, negotiated terms control over general printed terms.
C. Neither; the contract fails for ambiguity.
D. The printed term because it's more detailed.
**Best: B.**

## 18. Omitted and implied terms; good faith (III.C, IV.D) [R; V UCC 1-304, 2-103(1)(b), 2-306(2), 2-305-2-311, 2-309]

- **Gap-filling**: courts supply terms that are reasonable in the circumstances if the parties intended to be bound; the UCC supplies specific defaults (price, place, time of delivery/payment, assortment, cooperation, termination notice) [V text in section 5].
- **Implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing** (Restatement §205; UCC 1-304): "Every contract or duty within the Uniform Commercial Code imposes an obligation of good faith in its performance and enforcement" [V]. Good faith in the UCC = **honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing** (1-201(b)(20)); for **merchants** in Article 2: "honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing in the trade" [V 2-103(1)(b)]. The obligations of good faith, diligence, reasonableness and care **cannot be disclaimed** by agreement, though the parties may set standards if not manifestly unreasonable [V 1-302(b)]. At common law good faith is implied in every contract but **cannot override express terms** (e.g., a clear right to terminate at will); it limits discretion and prevents one party from destroying the other's right to the fruits of the contract.
- **Specific implied obligations:** duty to cooperate/not hinder; **best efforts** in exclusive dealing (seller to supply; buyer to promote) [V 2-306(2)]; **good faith in output/requirements** [V 2-306(1)]; **good faith in price set by one party** [V 2-305(2)] and in specifications [V 2-311(1)]; reasonable time; reasonable notice before terminating an indefinite-duration contract [V 2-309(3)].
- **Employment (NextGen explicit):** an indefinite-duration employment contract is **at will**; definite-term contracts require cause or breach for early termination [V].

**Items**

**C5-09 (exclusive dealing).** Distributor received exclusive rights in a state to resell Maker's tools. The contract is silent about effort. Distributor orders nothing for six months and does no marketing. Maker sues. Result?
A. For Distributor; the contract imposed no marketing obligation.
B. For Maker; an exclusive dealing arrangement imposes an obligation of best efforts by the buyer to promote the goods, unless otherwise agreed.
C. For Distributor; courts do not imply terms.
D. For Maker only if the contract was in writing.
**Best: B.** UCC 2-306(2).

**C5-10 (at-will).** An employer terminated an at-will manager without cause two weeks after the manager's best sales quarter. The manager argues the implied covenant of good faith prohibits the firing. Result under the majority common-law rule?
A. For the manager; every discharge needs good cause.
B. For the employer; the implied covenant cannot override the express at-will term (subject to statutory and public-policy exceptions not at issue).
C. For the manager; the timing shows bad faith.
D. For the employer; at-will employment is not a contract.
**Best: B.** [U: a few states imply limits on bad-faith terminations; the exam usually assumes the majority.]

**C5-11 (requirements; good faith).** Plant Co. had a requirements contract for all the natural gas its plant needs from Gas Co. at $4 per unit. When the market price rose to $8, Plant Co. doubled its plant's operating hours to build excess product it could resell. Gas Co. refuses to fill the extra gas. Result?
A. Gas Co. must supply all gas ordered.
B. Gas Co. may limit supply to the quantity not unreasonably disproportionate to Plant Co.'s normal requirements; the increase was not requirements "that may occur in good faith."
C. The contract is void as illusory.
D. Gas Co. wins only if it can show it lost money.
**Best: B.**

## 19. Warranties in sales of goods (IV.E) [V UCC text; R comments]

**Express (2-313) [V].** Created by (a) any **affirmation of fact or promise** relating to the goods that becomes part of the **basis of the bargain**; (b) any **description** of the goods that is part of the basis; (c) any **sample or model** that is part of the basis. No formal words ("warrant") or intent needed; **an affirmation merely of value or a seller's opinion or commendation (puffery) does not create a warranty**. [R: reliance is not required; a buyer need not show he relied, but the seller may rebut that the statement was part of the basis.]

**Title and infringement (2-312) [V].** Seller warrants good title and rightful transfer; goods free from security interests/liens unknown to the buyer; excluded only by specific language or circumstances. A **merchant regularly dealing in goods of the kind** warrants goods free of rightful third-party infringement claims; buyer furnishing specifications must indemnify.

**Merchantability (2-314) [V].** Implied in a contract for sale **if the seller is a merchant with respect to goods of that kind**; serving food or drink for value is a sale. Goods must at least: pass without objection in the trade under the contract description; (fungible goods) be of fair average quality; be **fit for the ordinary purposes** for which such goods are used; be of even kind, quality and quantity within each unit; be adequately contained, packaged and labeled; conform to promises/affirmations on the container or label.

**Fitness for a particular purpose (2-315) [V].** Where the seller **at the time of contracting has reason to know** any particular purpose for which the goods are required **and** that the buyer is **relying on the seller's skill or judgment** to select or furnish suitable goods → implied warranty of fitness. **Seller need not be a merchant.** Particular purpose is narrower than ordinary purpose.

**Disclaimers (2-316) [V].**
- Express warranty vs. negating words: construed consistent where reasonable; if unreasonable, the negation is inoperative (subject to PER).
- To exclude **merchantability**: language must **mention "merchantability"** and, if in writing, be **conspicuous**. To exclude **fitness**: must be in a **writing** and **conspicuous** (e.g., "There are no warranties which extend beyond the description on the face hereof").
- **"As is," "with all faults,"** or similar language calling the buyer's attention to the exclusion excludes all implied warranties.
- **Examination:** if the buyer before contracting examined the goods (or a sample) as fully as he desired or **refused to examine**, no implied warranty for defects that the examination ought in the circumstances to have revealed.
- Implied warranties can also be excluded by **course of dealing, course of performance or usage of trade**.
- **Conspicuous** [V 1-201(b)(10)]: so written, displayed, or presented that a reasonable person against whom it operates ought to have noticed it; decided by the court; examples: capitals/ larger/contrasting type or color.

**Cumulation (2-317) [V]:** warranties are cumulative; if inconsistent, intention controls: exact specifications displace samples/general description; sample from bulk displaces general description; **express warranties displace inconsistent implied warranties other than fitness**.

**Third parties (2-318) [V]:** three alternatives (states choose one). A: family, household, and guests in the home, natural persons injured in person. B: any natural person who may reasonably be expected to use, consume, or be affected by the goods and is injured in person. C: any person (including economic loss) reasonably expected to use, consume or be affected, injured. The seller may not exclude the third-party extension. Vertical privity (remote manufacturers) is left to case law. [U: any MBE item will state which alternative or ask about the "official text"; teach by name only if the stem does.]

**Notice and limitations.** Buyer must **notify the seller within a reasonable time after discovering the breach** or is barred from any remedy [V 2-607(3)(a)]; burden on buyer to prove breach of accepted goods [V 2-607(4)]. Limitations: 4 years from tender of delivery (future-performance warranty: from when breach is or should have been discovered) [V 2-725]. **Remedy limits:** the agreement may limit remedies to repair/replace or return-and-refund; if the limited remedy **fails of its essential purpose**, general remedies are available [V 2-719(2)]; **consequential damages** may be limited unless unconscionable; limiting consequential damages for **personal injury in consumer goods is prima facie unconscionable**, commercial loss is not [V 2-719(3)]. Damages: difference at time and place of acceptance between value as accepted and value as warranted [V 2-714(2)], plus incidental and consequential (including **injury to person or property proximately resulting from breach of warranty**) [V 2-715(2)(b)].

**Traps**
1. Merchant requirement for merchantability, not for fitness (and not for express warranties).
2. Puffery vs affirmation of fact; "basis of the bargain."
3. Disclaimer formalities: say "merchantability" + conspicuous; fitness: writing + conspicuous; "as is" is enough for both without the formalities.
4. Disclaimer can't defeat an express warranty (inconsistent = inoperative).
5. Sample/model: whole goods conform.
6. Notice defense; four-year statute.
7. 2-318 alternative.

**Items**

**C5-12 (puffery vs fact).** A power-tool salesperson told a contractor, "This saw is top of the line, and it will cut 3-inch hardwood all day without overheating." The saw overheated on 2-inch pine within an hour. The contractor sues. The seller argues puffery. Result?
A. Seller wins; both statements are puffery.
B. Contractor wins; "will cut 3-inch hardwood all day without overheating" is an affirmation of fact that became part of the basis of the bargain, creating an express warranty; "top of the line" alone would be puffery.
C. Contractor wins; but only for the implied warranty.
D. Seller wins; no written warranty.
**Best: B.**

**C5-13 (merchantability; non-merchant).** An orthodontist sold her personal car to a neighbor. The car's engine failed in a week due to a hidden defect. The neighbor sues for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability. Result?
A. Neighbor wins; every sale of goods carries it.
B. Seller wins; she was not a merchant with respect to cars, so no implied warranty of merchantability arose.
C. Neighbor wins because the sale was within a week.
D. Seller wins because used goods carry no warranties.
**Best: B.** (Implied warranty of fitness or express warranties could still apply on other facts.)

**C5-14 (fitness).** A buyer told a hardware clerk, "I need paint that will stay on a cedar deck exposed to ocean salt spray." The clerk picked "Brand X Exterior," which peeled in two months. The buyer sues for breach of implied warranty of fitness. The seller notes the paint is merchantable. Result?
A. For seller; merchantable goods can't breach fitness.
B. For buyer; the seller had reason to know the particular purpose and the buyer relied on the seller's judgment.
C. For seller; fitness requires a written request.
D. For buyer only if the clerk used the word "warranty."
**Best: B.**

**C5-15 (disclaimer).** The sale invoice for a used lawnmower read, in 8-point type among other terms, "SELLER MAKES NO WARRANTIES OF ANY KIND, EXPRESS OR IMPLIED." The mower, sold by a merchant dealer, had a defective blade assembly. Buyer sues for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability. Result?
A. For seller; the clause disclaimed all warranties.
B. For buyer; a disclaimer of merchantability must mention "merchantability" (and be conspicuous), so this clause is ineffective as to merchantability.
C. For buyer; used goods carry warranties.
D. For seller; a conspicuous disclaimer isn't required.
**Best: B.** 2-316(2). ("AS IS" would have worked.)

**C5-16 (notice).** A business bought 40 commercial ovens in January, used them, and discovered in March that their thermostats were defective. It said nothing to the seller and sued in December for breach of warranty. Result?
A. For buyer; the four-year statute governs.
B. For seller; the buyer failed to notify the seller of the breach within a reasonable time after discovery, barring any remedy.
C. For buyer; notice is unnecessary for express warranties.
D. For seller; the buyer accepted the goods.
**Best: B.** 2-607(3)(a). (D is wrong: acceptance doesn't bar damages.)

**C5-17 (express vs disclaimer).** A dealer's brochure promised "all-weather tires rated for 60,000 miles." The invoice said "All warranties, express or implied, disclaimed; sold AS IS." The tires failed at 12,000 miles. Result?
A. For the dealer; AS IS disclaimed everything.
B. For the buyer on the express warranty; the disclaimer is inoperative to the extent it is inconsistent with the express warranty, though "AS IS" did exclude the implied warranties.
C. For the buyer on the implied warranty of merchantability.
D. For the dealer; the brochure was not a contract term.
**Best: B.**

# PART E. PERFORMANCE, BREACH, DISCHARGE (MBE IV.A-C, F-H)

## 20. Conditions and excuse of conditions (IV.A-B) [R; V NextGen scope: promises vs conditions, nonoccurrence and excuse, conditions of satisfaction]

**Concepts**
- **Promise** = a commitment (breach → damages). **Condition** = an event, not certain to occur, that must occur before a duty arises (non-occurrence → no duty, no breach by the other side). Conditions may be **express** (agreed) or **constructive** (implied by law, e.g., the order-of-performance rules), **precedent**, **subsequent**, or **concurrent** (exchange of goods for payment).
- **Interpretation preference:** where doubtful, courts read a term as a **promise** rather than a condition to avoid forfeiture (§227). Signal words for conditions: "if," "provided that," "on condition that," "subject to," "unless."
- **Strict compliance** is required for **express** conditions; **substantial performance** suffices for **constructive** conditions.
- **Order of performance** (§234): where one party's performance takes time and the other's can be rendered at once, the one needing time goes first; otherwise concurrent. Exchanges of goods for money: concurrent conditions (each must tender).
- **Satisfaction clauses:** **objective** (reasonable person) standard for commercial or operative fitness (e.g., machinery, construction) and when in doubt; **subjective** good-faith standard where taste, fancy or judgment is involved (art, personal services).
- **Excuse of a condition:** (1) **waiver** (voluntary relinquishment, no consideration needed; retractable as to future performance by reasonable notice unless the other has relied) or **estoppel**; (2) **prevention/hindrance** or failure to cooperate (breach of the implied duty of good faith); (3) **avoiding disproportionate forfeiture** when the condition's occurrence is **not a material part of the agreed exchange** (§229; e.g., late notice with no prejudice); (4) **repudiation/material breach** by the other party; (5) **impossibility/impracticability** of the condition; (6) conditions that are for the benefit of one party can be waived by that party.
- **Constructive conditions of exchange:** each party's duty to perform is conditioned on the other's substantial performance; a **material breach** by one excuses the other's remaining duties.
- **Conditions of satisfaction under the UCC:** buyer's right to inspection and acceptance are governed by UCC rules (section 25).

**Traps:** condition vs promise; express conditions strict (the "harsh" result is avoided by §229 or waiver); subjective vs objective satisfaction; waiver can be retracted as to executory future performance but not past; prevention excuses the condition.

**Items**

**C6-01 (express condition; prevention).** Owner agreed to pay Contractor $90,000 "only if the architect certifies in writing that the work satisfies the plans." Contractor built to plan, but the architect, a close friend of Owner, refused to inspect, saying "I won't sign anything for that guy." Contractor sues. Result?
A. Owner wins; the certificate is an express condition that did not occur.
B. Owner wins; Contractor failed to strictly comply.
C. Contractor wins; the architect's unjustified refusal to inspect (acting for the Owner's benefit) excuses the certification condition on grounds of prevention/bad faith.
D. Contractor wins only in quantum meruit.
**Best: C.** (If the architect had honestly inspected and refused because of real defects, A.)

**C6-02 (satisfaction).** A homeowner agreed to pay for a new central-air system "if it is satisfactory to me." The system met the manufacturer's specs and cooled every room. The homeowner said, "I just don't like how it looks," and refused to pay. Result?
A. Homeowner wins; satisfaction is entirely subjective.
B. Installer wins; for mechanical-fitness contracts the test is objective: whether a reasonable person would be satisfied.
C. Homeowner wins; satisfaction clauses are always honored.
D. Installer wins only if the homeowner acted in bad faith.
**Best: B.** (For portrait painting or tailoring, the subjective good-faith test applies.)

**C6-03 (waiver).** A lease conditioned the tenant's renewal option on timely written notice 60 days before expiration. Landlord accepted late oral notice and talked with the tenant about the renewal terms for a month. Landlord then said the option was lost. Result?
A. For Landlord; strict compliance with an express condition is required.
B. For Tenant; Landlord's conduct waived (or is estopped to assert) the late-notice condition.
C. For Landlord; waivers must be in writing.
D. For Tenant; conditions are never enforced.
**Best: B.**

**C6-04 (disproportionate forfeiture).** A policy conditioned coverage for a $2 million loss on giving notice within 30 days. The insured gave notice on day 33; the insurer suffered no prejudice. In a jurisdiction applying Restatement §229, result?
A. Insurer wins; express condition, strict compliance.
B. Insured wins; the court may excuse the condition because the forfeiture of $2 million would be disproportionate and the notice term was not a material part of the exchange.
C. Insured wins only if the insurer waived in writing.
D. Insurer wins; courts never excuse express conditions.
**Best: B.** [U: some jurisdictions apply notice-prejudice only for specific policy types.]

**C6-05 (order of performance).** A painter and homeowner agreed that the painter would paint the house for $5,000. They said nothing about when payment is due. The painter finished the job on Monday. Is the homeowner's payment due on Monday?
A. No, only 30 days after.
B. Yes; the painter's performance requires time while payment can be made at once, so the painter performs first and the owner's duty to pay arises on the painter's substantial completion.
C. No, payment is due before painting begins.
D. No, payment is due only after a written invoice.
**Best: B.**

## 21. Material breach, partial breach, substantial performance (IV.C) [R]

- **Material breach** (§241 factors): (a) extent to which the injured party is **deprived of the benefit** reasonably expected; (b) adequacy of **compensation** in damages; (c) **forfeiture** the breaching party would suffer; (d) likelihood the breaching party will **cure**; (e) **good faith and fair dealing** of the breaching party. A **material breach** gives the injured party the right to suspend, and (if uncured after a reasonable time) **terminate** its performance and sue for **total** breach. A **minor (partial) breach** gives damages but the other party must still perform (the other's duty is only suspended while the breach continues, if at all).
- **Election:** the injured party who continues to accept performance after a material breach waives the right to terminate but keeps damages.
- **Substantial performance** (constructive conditions; typically construction): a party who has substantially performed in good faith (**no willful deviation**) can recover the contract price minus damages for the deviation. Measure of owner's damages: **cost to remedy** the defect, unless that would involve **unreasonable economic waste**, in which case **diminution in value** (Jacob & Youngs v. Kent: pipe of different brand; contrast Peevyhouse on cost vs value in a mining restoration case).
- **Divisible (severable) contracts** (§240): where performance on each side is divided into corresponding pairs of agreed equivalents, breach of a later part doesn't forfeit payment for earlier parts.
- **Perfect tender** is the UCC's stricter standard (section 25).
- Good faith: every contract (see section 18).
- **Employment (NextGen):** definite-term employee discharged without cause recovers salary for the remainder minus mitigation; at-will has no breach claim (see section 18).

**Traps:** substantial performance isn't available for willful or serious deviations; the owner can't withhold the whole price but may deduct damages; cost-to-repair vs diminution (economic waste); material breach excuses; partial breach doesn't.

**C6-06 (substantial performance).** Owner contracted with Builder for a $400,000 house specifying "Brand A copper pipe." Builder, unaware of the specification, installed Brand B copper pipe, identical in quality and appearance. Replacing it would cost $25,000 and wreck finished walls. Owner refuses to pay the last $50,000. Result?
A. Owner wins; express specifications must be strictly satisfied.
B. Builder recovers the $50,000 balance less the diminution in value (here essentially nothing); replacement would be unreasonable economic waste.
C. Builder recovers nothing; he is in material breach.
D. Owner may withhold $25,000 as cost of repair.
**Best: B.**

**C6-07 (willful deviation).** A contractor knowingly built a house with cheaper wiring than specified to save money, then sued for the unpaid balance. Likely result?
A. Contractor recovers the full balance minus the repair cost.
B. Contractor generally cannot invoke the substantial performance doctrine for an intentional departure, and may be limited to restitution.
C. Contractor recovers the full price.
D. Contractor recovers nothing under any theory.
**Best: B.** [U: states vary; stem should say the jurisdiction does not permit substantial performance by a willful breacher.]

**C6-08 (divisible).** Tiller agreed to harvest Owner's 10 fields for $1,000 per field, payable on completion of each. Tiller completed 4, then walked off without cause. Owner sues for damages. Is Tiller entitled to $4,000 for the completed fields?
A. No; he breached the whole contract.
B. Yes, subject to Owner's offset for damages; the contract is divisible into corresponding pairs of performances.
C. Yes, with no offset.
D. No; the contract was entire.
**Best: B.**

**C6-09 (material vs partial).** A supplier delivered 1,000 uniforms that were ready 3 days late; the buyer needed them only for a 3-week event beginning 10 days later and received them in time. The buyer refuses to pay anything. Result under common-law principles?
A. Seller wins damages for price; the delay was a minor breach.
B. Buyer may cancel; any breach is material.
C. Buyer may recover damages but must pay for the uniforms; the delay was not a material breach.
D. Buyer may cancel because time is always of the essence.
**Best: C.** (For goods under the UCC, perfect tender could allow rejection: see section 25.)

## 22. Anticipatory repudiation and adequate assurance (IV.C) [V UCC 2-609-2-611; R §§250-257]

- **Anticipatory repudiation (AR):** an **unequivocal** statement or a **voluntary, affirmative act** that makes performance impossible or clearly indicates the party will not perform when the time comes. Applies to a **bilateral executory** contract before performance is due. Non-repudiating party may: (1) **suspend** own performance, and **either** (2) **await** performance for a reasonable time (commercially reasonable time for sales), urging retraction (but can't recover damages that could have been avoided after repudiation), **or** (3) **treat as a total breach** and sue immediately [V 2-610].
- **Retraction** is possible until the aggrieved party has cancelled, materially changed position, or indicated that it considers the repudiation final [V 2-611].
- **Not repudiation:** statements of doubt, requests to modify, good-faith dispute about the contract's meaning, or insecurity.
- **Demand for adequate assurance** (UCC 2-609): when **reasonable grounds for insecurity** arise, the other party may **demand in writing** adequate assurance and, if commercially reasonable, **suspend its own performance** (that it has not already received the agreed return for); **failure to provide adequate assurance within a reasonable time, not exceeding 30 days, is a repudiation** [V 2-609(1),(4)]. Between merchants, reasonableness is judged by commercial standards [V 2-609(2)]. **Common law** (§251): adopted by many courts; the Restatement permits a demand when reasonable grounds arise to believe that the obligor will commit a breach that would give a claim for total breach. [U: some common-law jurisdictions limit to sales or insolvent parties; NextGen lists "demand for adequate assurance" under common law and UCC.]
- **Exceptions:** AR doesn't apply to **unilateral** contracts, or when the repudiated party has **fully performed** and the only remaining obligation is the payment of money in installments (majority rule: must wait for each installment unless there's an acceleration clause) [R §243(3) context].
- **Damages:** measured as of the time performance is due or (UCC) per market rules [V 2-723(1): if trial comes before the time for performance, market-based damages use the price prevailing when the aggrieved party learned of the repudiation]; must mitigate.

**Traps:** equivocal statements aren't repudiation; AR gives a choice (wait vs sue), but waiting doesn't extend damages; retraction cut off by reliance; adequate assurance is the safe move when doubtful; no assurance + 30 days = repudiation (UCC).

**C6-10.** Under a contract for delivery of 5,000 bushels of soybeans on Oct 1, Seller wrote Buyer on Aug 1: "Prices have gone up; I will not deliver unless you pay $2 more per bushel." Buyer replied the same day: "I'll wait until Oct 1 to see if you come to your senses," then bought replacement soybeans in September. Seller delivered nothing on Oct 1. Buyer's best position?
A. Buyer can't recover; he failed to await performance.
B. Seller's August letter was a repudiation; Buyer could await or resort to remedies and covering in September was a valid response (2-610(b), 2-712).
C. Buyer can recover only after Oct 1.
D. No repudiation occurred because performance was not due.
**Best: B.**

**C6-11 (equivocal).** A subcontractor told the general contractor, "Material delays may make it hard for me to start July 1, and I'm worried." The GC immediately hired a replacement and sued for breach. Result?
A. GC wins; the statement was a repudiation.
B. Sub wins; the statement was equivocal and not a repudiation; the GC's remedy was to demand adequate assurance.
C. GC wins; any worry is a breach.
D. Sub wins only if he performs on July 1.
**Best: B.**

**C6-12 (UCC assurance).** A buyer under a multi-delivery contract learned the seller's factory was shut by a regulator. The buyer wrote the seller demanding assurance of the next delivery. The seller did not respond within 31 days. Result?
A. The seller's silence is a repudiation after a reasonable time not exceeding 30 days; the buyer may pursue remedies.
B. The buyer has no remedy until the delivery date.
C. The seller can still demand payment.
D. The buyer must wait 90 days.
**Best: A.** (Item writer should say the buyer's insecurity was objectively reasonable.)

**C6-13 (retraction).** On Monday Seller told Buyer, "I'm cancelling our deal." On Tuesday, before Buyer did anything, Seller said, "I've changed my mind; I'll perform." Buyer says it was already a total breach. Result?
A. Buyer wins; repudiation cannot be retracted.
B. Seller wins; a repudiation can be retracted before the other party cancels, materially changes position, or indicates it considers the repudiation final.
C. Buyer wins; because retraction needs consideration.
D. Seller wins only if retraction was written.
**Best: B.**

**C6-14 (installment money).** Lender lent Borrower $120,000, fully advanced, repayable in 60 monthly installments. After 12 months, Borrower said, "I will never pay you another cent." No acceleration clause. Lender sues immediately for all remaining $96,000. Result under majority rule?
A. Lender wins; AR gives right to total damages.
B. Lender wins for the missed installments only; because Lender fully performed and only installment money payments remain, AR doesn't allow suit for the whole until installments come due.
C. Lender wins nothing.
D. Lender wins everything with interest.
**Best: B.**

## 23. Impossibility, impracticability, frustration of purpose; force majeure (IV.G) [V UCC 2-613-2-616; R §§261, 262-266, 265]

- **Impossibility / impracticability** (§261; UCC 2-615 for sellers): after the contract is made, a **supervening event** occurs whose **non-occurrence was a basic assumption** of the contract, making performance **impracticable** (extreme and unreasonable difficulty, expense, injury or loss; objective), **without the fault** of the party, and the party **did not assume the risk** → duty is discharged (or suspended). Classic situations: **destruction of the specific subject matter** (Taylor v. Caldwell, music hall), **death or incapacity** of a person essential to personal performance, **supervening illegality**. **Increased cost alone** does not excuse (even a sharp market rise) unless an unforeseen contingency alters the essential nature of performance (UCC Comment 4 R). **Foreseeability** is relevant: foreseeable events are often risks assumed (and force majeure clauses allocate).
- **Frustration of purpose** (§265; Krell v. Henry, coronation rooms): a supervening event **substantially frustrates a party's principal purpose**, the purpose was **known to both** and is the basis of the deal, non-occurrence was a basic assumption, no fault → discharge. Not frustration: a mere decline in profitability or reduced value; foreseeable risks.
- **UCC specifics [V text]:** 2-615: delay or non-delivery by a **seller** is not breach if performance as agreed has been made impracticable by a contingency whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption **or by compliance in good faith with a government regulation or order whether or not it later proves invalid**; when capacity is only partly affected the seller **must allocate** production and deliveries among customers in a **fair and reasonable** manner (may include regular customers not under contract and own needs for further manufacture); seller **must notify seasonably** of delay or non-delivery and estimated quota. 2-616: the buyer may by written notice **terminate** the unexecuted portion or **modify** the contract to take the available quota; if he doesn't modify in a reasonable time not exceeding 30 days, the contract lapses as to affected deliveries. 2-614: substituted performance where agreed means/ facilities fail but a commercially reasonable substitute exists. 2-613: **casualty to identified goods** (identified when the contract is made) without fault before risk of loss passes: total loss → contract avoided; partial → buyer may treat as avoided or accept with price allowance.
- **Temporary** impracticability: suspension of duty during the event; discharge only if later performance would be materially more burdensome. **Partial** excuse: perform what is possible, proportionally.
- **Effect:** discharge of the unperformed duties; **restitution** for benefits conferred; **reliance** damages may be allocated (Restatement §272). **Force majeure clauses** are enforced according to their terms; interpreted as to listed events; a general "catch-all" is read in light of the listed events (ejusdem generis). [V NextGen scope: "effects of partial and temporary excuse and force majeure provisions".]

**Traps:** cost increase ≠ impracticability; self-induced or foreseeable → no excuse; frustration requires known principal purpose; UCC 2-615 is for sellers (buyers use common law via 1-103(b)); seller's duty to allocate and notify; casualty before risk passes → avoid vs after → buyer bears loss.

**C6-15 (cost increase).** A contractor agreed in March to supply and install 500 tons of steel framing at a fixed price. By June, because of a worldwide supply shortage, the steel price rose 40%. Contractor claims impracticability. Result?
A. Excused; 40% is extreme.
B. Not excused; a cost increase alone, even a large one, doesn't make performance impracticable absent extraordinary unforeseen circumstances.
C. Excused if the contractor gives written notice.
D. Not excused only if the price was fixed in writing.
**Best: B.**

**C6-16 (destruction of specific subject).** Doe contracted to sell his vintage 1957 Chevrolet, kept in his garage, to Roe for $60,000, delivery in two weeks. Without Doe's fault, a lightning-caused fire destroyed the car before delivery. Doe is a collector, not a merchant. Result?
A. Doe liable for expectation damages.
B. Contract avoided; the specific goods identified at contracting were totally destroyed before risk of loss passed (2-613; impracticability).
C. Roe may demand a substitute car.
D. Doe must refund the deposit but also pay damages.
**Best: B.** (Deposit refund is restitution.)

**C6-17 (frustration).** Gwen leased a Main Street storefront for 5 years to run a coffee shop. In year 2, a city ordinance, unforeseeable at signing, closed Main Street to all traffic for 3 years, cutting her customers by 80%. Gwen wants out of the lease. Result?
A. Frustration discharges her duty; the principal purpose was destroyed.
B. Likely no discharge; Gwen's business is still possible, reduced profitability isn't frustration, and the risk of such changes lies with the lessee.
C. Lease is void ab initio.
D. Discharge because she had notified the landlord.
**Best: B.** (A flips if the lease was solely for a viewing spot for a parade that was cancelled, both parties knowing.)

**C6-18 (seller allocation).** A beet-sugar refiner under contract with 10 customers had a fire that cut output by half, with no fault. It delivered half to its biggest customer and nothing to Customer 7. It gave no notice. Customer 7 sues. Result?
A. The seller is excused entirely.
B. The seller is excused only if it allocated fairly and reasonably and seasonably notified the buyers; here it did neither.
C. The seller is excused because a fire is an act of God.
D. The seller is liable only for lost profits.
**Best: B.** 2-615(b),(c).

## 24. Discharge of duties: accord and satisfaction, substituted contract, novation, rescission, release (IV.H) [R; V NextGen scope; UCC 1-306 R]

- **Accord:** an agreement in which one party agrees to accept, **in satisfaction of an existing duty**, a **different performance** from the other party. **Satisfaction:** performance of the accord, which discharges the original duty. **Executory accord**: the original duty is **suspended** until performance of the accord; if the debtor breaches the accord, the creditor may sue on the **original duty** or on the **accord**; if the debtor performs, the original duty is discharged. Requires consideration (a good-faith dispute or a new/different performance).
- **Substituted contract:** the parties agree to a **new contract** that **immediately** discharges the earlier duties and replaces them; if the new contract is breached, the claim is only on the new contract.
- **Novation:** a **substituted contract** that **adds or substitutes a new party** (new obligor or obligee) with the consent of the obligee (and the others); the original obligor is **discharged**. Contrast **delegation**/assumption, which does not discharge the delegator without novation.
- **Rescission** (mutual): agreement to discharge unperformed duties on both sides; supported by the mutual release of duties (if one side has fully performed, a consideration/ release is needed). **Rescission as a remedy** (section 30) is different.
- **Release:** a signed writing discharging a claim; under the common law effective with consideration or per the writing/seal rule; under the UCC a claim for alleged breach may be discharged **without consideration** by a written waiver or renunciation signed and delivered by the aggrieved party [R 1-306].
- **Waiver, cancellation, merger, alteration, running of the statute of limitations** are other discharge ideas.

**Traps:** accord ≠ satisfaction; executory accord suspends; novation discharges old party; a mere assignment/delegation doesn't discharge; substituted contract vs accord (immediate discharge vs suspend).

**C6-19 (executory accord).** Debtor owed Creditor $9,000 on a contract claim. They agreed that Creditor would accept Debtor's used boat on Friday in full satisfaction. On Friday, Debtor refused to deliver the boat. Creditor's best position?
A. Creditor may sue only on the accord.
B. Creditor may sue on the original $9,000 debt or on the accord; the original duty was only suspended.
C. Creditor has no claim; the accord discharged the debt.
D. Creditor may sue for $9,000 only after giving written notice.
**Best: B.**

**C6-20 (substituted contract).** Same facts, but Creditor and Debtor agreed that "the new contract replaces the old debt," under which Debtor would deliver the boat on Friday. Debtor failed to deliver. Creditor's remedy?
A. Sue on the original debt.
B. Sue only on the new contract; the new contract immediately discharged the old duty.
C. Sue on either.
D. No remedy.
**Best: B.**

**C6-21 (novation).** Tenant Alice leased a store for 3 years. In year one she transferred the lease to Ben, who promised Alice to pay the rent. Landlord Lara signed a document stating she accepted Ben as tenant and "releases Alice from all obligations." Ben defaults. Is Alice liable?
A. Yes; delegation never discharges.
B. No; the three-party agreement was a novation that discharged Alice.
C. Yes; Alice still owes unless Ben pays.
D. No, but only if Alice gave notice.
**Best: B.** (Without Lara's release, Alice would remain secondarily liable.)

**C6-22 (rescission).** A buyer and seller of a custom machine agreed, before either performed, to cancel the contract and release each other. A week later, the seller demanded performance. Result?
A. For seller; rescission requires a writing.
B. For buyer; mutual rescission of an executory contract, supported by the mutual release, discharged the duties.
C. For seller; rescission needs a court order.
D. For buyer only if consideration was paid.
**Best: B.** (If the machine was within the SoF, the oral rescission remains effective; the SoF doesn't require writings for rescission.)

## 25. UCC performance: perfect tender, rejection, acceptance, revocation, cure (IV.F) [V text of UCC 2-307-2-312, 2-503-2-510, 2-513, 2-601-2-612]

**Seller's obligations and buyer's response (a timeline)**
1. **Tender** [V 2-503(1)]: seller puts and holds **conforming** goods at the buyer's disposition and gives any notice reasonably necessary; at a reasonable hour; goods kept available for a reasonable period; buyer furnishes suitable facilities. Tender entitles the seller to acceptance and payment and is a condition to the buyer's duty to accept and (unless otherwise agreed) pay [V 2-507(1)]. All goods in a **single delivery** unless otherwise agreed [V 2-307]; place of delivery = seller's place of business (or the known location of identified goods) [V 2-308]; time = reasonable [V 2-309]; payment due at time and place the buyer is to receive the goods [V 2-310(a)]; buyer's right to **inspect before payment** (except C.O.D. or payment against documents) [V 2-513].
2. **Shipment contract** (seller must put goods with a carrier, make a reasonable contract of carriage, deliver necessary documents, promptly notify; failure to notify or contract is ground for rejection **only if material delay or loss ensues**) [V 2-504]; **destination contract** (seller must tender at destination) [V 2-503(3)]. FOB place of shipment = shipment; FOB destination = destination [V 2-319].
3. **Perfect tender rule** [V 2-601]: if goods or tender "fail in any respect to conform to the contract," the buyer may (a) **reject the whole**, (b) **accept the whole**, or (c) **accept any commercial unit(s) and reject the rest**. Exceptions: installment contracts (2-612), parties' agreed limited remedy (2-719), cure (2-508), shipment-contract technicalities (2-504).
4. **Installment contract** [V 2-612]: contract authorizing delivery in lots to be separately accepted. Buyer may reject an installment only if the non-conformity **substantially impairs the value of that installment and cannot be cured** (or a document defect); if seller gives adequate assurance of cure, buyer must accept. A non-conformity that **substantially impairs the value of the whole** is a breach of the whole; the aggrieved party **reinstates** the contract by accepting a non-conforming installment without seasonably notifying cancellation, or by suing only on past installments or demanding future performance.
5. **Rejection** [V 2-602, 2-605, 2-603]: must be **within a reasonable time** after delivery or tender and **seasonable notice** to the seller; after rejection, buyer must not exercise ownership; must hold goods with reasonable care at the seller's disposition; **merchant buyer** with no agent of the seller nearby follows reasonable instructions or sells perishable goods for the seller's account. **Failure to particularize** a defect ascertainable by reasonable inspection forecloses reliance on it where the seller could have cured, or between merchants after a written request for a full and final statement.
6. **Cure** [V 2-508]: if the time for performance has not expired, seller may **seasonably notify** the buyer of intent to cure and make a conforming delivery within the contract time; if the buyer rejected a non-conforming tender that the seller had **reasonable grounds to believe would be acceptable** (with or without money allowance), the seller may, on seasonable notice, have a **further reasonable time** to substitute a conforming tender (even after the time expired).
7. **Acceptance** [V 2-606]: after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, (a) buyer **signifies** that goods conform or he will take them anyway; (b) **fails to make an effective rejection**; or (c) **does any act inconsistent with the seller's ownership** (wrongful act is acceptance only if ratified). Acceptance of part of a commercial unit = acceptance of the whole unit.
8. **Effects of acceptance** [V 2-607]: buyer must pay at the contract rate; cannot reject; **may not revoke** acceptance unless it fits 2-608; must **notify the seller of breach within a reasonable time** after discovery or is barred from any remedy; buyer bears the burden of proving breach of accepted goods; may give seller notice of third-party litigation.
9. **Revocation of acceptance** [V 2-608]: of a lot or commercial unit whose non-conformity **substantially impairs its value to the buyer**, if accepted (a) on the **reasonable assumption that it would be cured** and it has not been seasonably cured, or (b) **without discovery** of the non-conformity because of the **difficulty of discovery** or the **seller's assurances**. Must occur within a reasonable time after the buyer discovered or should have discovered, **before substantial change in condition** not caused by the defects, and requires **notice**. A buyer who revokes has the same rights and duties as if he had rejected.
10. **Buyer's remedies** (section 32).

**Traps**
1. **Perfect tender** applies to single-delivery contracts; installment contracts use the substantial-impairment standard.
2. **Cure** is available to the seller before the deadline, even for a non-conforming tender; after the deadline only if the seller reasonably thought it would be acceptable.
3. **Reject vs revoke**: reject before acceptance (any nonconformity); revoke after acceptance (substantial impairment + conditions).
4. **Acts inconsistent with ownership** (using, reselling) = acceptance; but use consistent with inspection isn't.
5. **Notice** appears everywhere: rejection, revocation, breach after acceptance, cure.
6. **Shipment contract**: failure to notify of shipment matters only if it caused material delay or loss.
7. **Contract limiting buyer's remedy** to repair/replace: perfect tender rejection may be displaced.

**C7-01 (perfect tender; cure).** Under a contract requiring delivery of 100 red lamps by June 30, Seller delivered 100 lamps on June 10, but 20 were blue. Buyer rejected all lamps on June 11 with prompt notice. Seller immediately notified Buyer it would deliver 100 conforming red lamps by June 25 and did. Buyer refuses them, relying on the perfect tender rule. Result?
A. Buyer wins; the tender was nonconforming.
B. Seller wins; seller seasonably notified Buyer of intent to cure and made a conforming delivery within the contract time, so the Buyer must accept.
C. Buyer wins; cure is only for installment contracts.
D. Seller wins only if Buyer consents.
**Best: B.** 2-508(1).

**C7-02 (cure after deadline).** Buyer ordered 'Brand R' mixers by Oct 1. For years, buyer had accepted 'Brand S' mixers whenever 'Brand R' was out of stock. Seller delivered Brand S on Oct 1; Buyer rejected. Seller seasonably notified Buyer it would send Brand R by Oct 15. Result?
A. Buyer may refuse; time expired.
B. Seller may cure within a further reasonable time; it had reasonable grounds to believe the substitution would be acceptable given prior dealings, and gave seasonable notice.
C. Buyer wins; there is no cure after the deadline.
D. Seller wins only if the Buyer agrees.
**Best: B.** 2-508(2).

**C7-03 (installment).** A restaurant group contracted to buy 12 monthly shipments of olive oil. The third shipment contained 3% oil that did not meet the specified acidity, but the oil was usable at a modest price allowance, and seller offered to replace the nonconforming 3%. Buyer rejects the whole third shipment and cancels the contract. Result?
A. Buyer wins; perfect tender applies.
B. Seller wins; Buyer may reject an installment only if the nonconformity substantially impairs its value and cannot be cured; seller gave adequate assurance of cure, so the buyer must accept.
C. Buyer wins; any nonconformity in an installment contract justifies rejection of the whole contract.
D. Seller wins only if the contract expressly said it was an installment contract.
**Best: B.** 2-612(2).

**C7-04 (acceptance).** Buyer received a shipment of 50 laptops on a Monday, opened them, loaded software and used them for three weeks in business, then wrote Seller to "reject" them for a screen defect. Result?
A. Rejection effective if the defect exists.
B. Rejection was ineffective; by failing to reject within a reasonable time and using the laptops (an act inconsistent with seller's ownership), Buyer accepted. Buyer may still recover damages for breach after notice, or try revocation if its conditions are met.
C. Rejection effective; buyers can reject any time before payment.
D. No remedy at all.
**Best: B.**

**C7-05 (revocation).** Buyer purchased a delivery van; seller assured Buyer that a persistent engine knock would be repaired. After four failed repair attempts over two months, and with the van still unusable for deliveries, Buyer notified Seller that it revokes acceptance. Result?
A. Not allowed; Buyer accepted the van.
B. Allowed; Buyer accepted on the reasonable assumption the defect would be cured, it was not seasonably cured, and the defect substantially impairs the van's value to Buyer.
C. Allowed only within 3 days.
D. Not allowed; revocation applies only to latent defects.
**Best: B.**

**C7-06 (shipment notice).** Seller shipped goods under a shipment contract but forgot to notify Buyer. The goods arrived on time and undamaged. Buyer rejects because of the missing notice. Result?
A. Buyer wins; failure to notify is ground for rejection.
B. Seller wins; failure to notify is ground for rejection only if material delay or loss ensues.
C. Buyer wins; the perfect tender rule.
D. Seller wins only if goods were insured.
**Best: B.** 2-504.

## 26. Risk of loss, title, identification (IV.F) [V UCC 2-401, 2-501, 2-509, 2-510, 2-319, 2-327]

- **Risk of loss does not depend on title** under Article 2 (risk provisions control; title rules apply only where a provision refers to title) [V 2-401 intro].
- **No breach [V 2-509]:**
  - Contract **requires or authorizes shipment by carrier**: if it **doesn't** require delivery at a particular destination (shipment contract), risk passes to the buyer when goods are **duly delivered to the carrier**; if it **does** require delivery at a destination, risk passes when goods are **duly tendered at destination** in the possession of the carrier.
  - Goods held by a **bailee** to be delivered without being moved: risk passes on buyer's receipt of a **negotiable document of title**, or on the bailee's **acknowledgment** of buyer's right to possession, or after buyer's receipt of a **non-negotiable document** or written direction and a reasonable time to present it [V 2-503(4)].
  - **Otherwise**: if the seller is a **merchant**, risk passes to the buyer on **receipt** of the goods (physical possession); if not a merchant, on **tender**.
  - All subject to contrary agreement.
- **Breach [V 2-510]:** if tender so fails to conform that the buyer has a right to reject, risk of loss remains on the **seller until cure or acceptance**; buyer who rightfully revokes may treat risk as resting on seller from the beginning to the extent of any deficiency in his insurance; if the **buyer** repudiates or breaches as to conforming identified goods before risk has passed, the seller may treat risk as on buyer for a commercially reasonable time to the extent of deficiency in seller's insurance.
- **Identification [V 2-501]:** buyer gets a **special property** and **insurable interest** by identification of existing goods; identification occurs by agreement, else when contract is made for existing identified goods; for future goods, when **shipped, marked or otherwise designated** by the seller; for crops/young, per statute. Seller keeps an insurable interest while title or a security interest remains.
- **Title [V 2-401(2)]:** unless otherwise explicitly agreed, title passes to buyer at the time and place the seller **completes physical delivery** (shipment contract: at shipment; destination: on tender). Retention of title in goods shipped = reservation of a security interest only. Rejection revests title in the seller.
- **Sale on approval / sale or return [V 2-327]:** on approval: risk and title stay with seller until acceptance; sale or return: return at buyer's risk and expense; option to return must be exercised seasonably.
- **Delivery terms [V 2-319]:** FOB place of shipment: seller bears expense and risk of putting goods into the carrier's possession (shipment contract); FOB place of destination: seller bears expense and risk to destination and tenders there.

**Traps:** title is not the risk trigger; breach shifts risk; merchant vs non-merchant seller when goods are picked up; FOB term as shorthand for shipment vs destination; goods must be conforming and duly tendered/delivered.

**C7-07 (shipment).** Under a contract "FOB Seller's Dallas warehouse," Seller delivered conforming goods to a carrier. They were destroyed in a derailment. Buyer refuses to pay. Result?
A. For Buyer; risk remains on Seller until delivery to Buyer.
B. For Seller; under a shipment contract risk of loss passed to Buyer on proper delivery to the carrier.
C. For Buyer; title had not passed.
D. For Seller only if Buyer had insured.
**Best: B.**

**C7-08 (destination).** Under a contract "FOB Buyer's plant," Seller delivered conforming goods to a carrier; they were destroyed in transit. Result?
A. Buyer bears the loss.
B. Seller bears the loss; risk passes only when the goods are duly tendered at the destination.
C. Loss is shared.
D. Buyer bears it if the goods were insured.
**Best: B.**

**C7-09 (merchant seller; pick-up).** Buyer agreed to buy a lawn tractor from a dealer (merchant), to be picked up at the dealer's store. The dealer called Buyer on Tuesday to say it was ready. The store burned that night, destroying the tractor, before Buyer picked it up. Who bears the loss?
A. Buyer; the dealer tendered on Tuesday.
B. Dealer; because the seller is a merchant, risk passes to the buyer on receipt of the goods.
C. Buyer; title had passed at the time of contracting.
D. Loss is shared.
**Best: B.** (If the seller were not a merchant, risk would pass on tender.)

**C7-10 (breach and risk).** Seller shipped goods that did not conform, giving Buyer a right to reject. The goods were destroyed in transit by a carrier's fault-free accident. Buyer has not yet received or accepted the goods. Who bears the loss?
A. Buyer; shipment contract.
B. Seller; where the tender fails to conform so as to give a right of rejection, risk stays with the seller until cure or acceptance.
C. Carrier only.
D. Shared.
**Best: B.**

**C7-11 (bailee).** Seller stored 500 sacks of flour at a public warehouse, then sold them to Buyer, delivering a negotiable warehouse receipt to Buyer on Monday. The warehouse burned on Tuesday. Who bears the loss?
A. Seller.
B. Buyer; risk passes on receipt of a negotiable document of title for goods in a bailee's possession.
C. Warehouse only.
D. Shared.
**Best: B.**

# PART F. REMEDIES (MBE V)

## 27. Expectation, reliance, restitution; foreseeability, certainty (V.A, B, G, H) [R; V UCC 2-710, 2-715]

**The three interests (Restatement §344).** **Expectation** (put the plaintiff where full performance would have), **reliance** (reimburse out-of-pocket loss in reliance, putting the plaintiff where it would have been had the contract not been made), **restitution** (give back the benefit the plaintiff conferred on the defendant). The plaintiff chooses among available measures; the usual default is expectation.

**Expectation formula (§347):** (value of the performance promised minus value of the performance received, i.e., **loss in value**) + (**other loss**: incidental and consequential) − (**cost or other loss avoided** by not having to perform) − (**loss avoided** through mitigation). Categories: **direct/general** damages (loss in value of the promised performance), **incidental** (costs of dealing with the breach: inspection, transport, storage, resale/cover costs), **consequential** (special: lost profits on collateral transactions, harm to property, etc.).
- **Construction:** owner v. breaching builder → **cost to complete/repair** (or **diminution in value** if the cost would involve unreasonable economic waste); builder v. breaching owner → **contract price minus cost saved** (= profit plus costs already incurred).
- **Sale of land:** buyer v. breaching seller → market value minus contract price (plus restitution of deposit); seller v. breaching buyer → contract price minus market value (or resale).
- **Employment:** discharged employee → wages for the unexpired term minus what was or could have been earned in a **comparable** job (see mitigation).
- **Interest:** prejudgment interest on liquidated sums. **Attorney's fees**: American rule unless contract/statute. **Punitive damages** and **emotional distress** generally unavailable for breach of contract (exceptions: breach that is also a tort; contracts where serious emotional disturbance is a particularly likely result, e.g., funeral, hospital) (§353, §355).
- **Foreseeability (Hadley v. Baxendale; §351):** damages are recoverable only for loss that was **foreseeable** as a probable result of breach **at the time of contracting**, either in the ordinary course (general damages) or from **special circumstances** the breaching party had **reason to know** (consequential). Communicating the special circumstances at contracting is what matters. UCC 2-715(2)(a): "any loss resulting from general or particular requirements and needs of which the seller at the time of contracting had reason to know and which could not reasonably be prevented by cover or otherwise" [V]; plus injury to person or property proximately resulting from breach of warranty [V 2-715(2)(b)].
- **Certainty (§352):** the fact of loss must be established with **reasonable certainty**; amount can be approximated. Lost profits of a **new business** are recoverable if proven with reasonable certainty (modern rule) rather than barred as a matter of law. **Causation** is required.
- **Reliance damages (§349):** including preparation expenses before the contract; reduced by loss the breacher proves the plaintiff would have suffered had the contract been performed. Used when expectation cannot be proved with reasonable certainty, and as the usual PE remedy.
- **Restitution** (§§370-377): benefit conferred, measured by market value; non-breaching party can elect restitution after material breach (capped by the contract price if fully performed except for payment of money, §373); **breaching party** recovers the excess of benefit conferred over the injured party's damages (§374); party in a contract that is unenforceable (SoF) or avoided (mistake, fraud) recovers restitution.

**Traps:** consequential damages need foreseeability at contracting AND causation AND certainty; reliance vs expectation (not both); builder v. owner: don't subtract the owner's savings incorrectly; emotional distress/punitive: no; cost to repair vs diminution (economic waste); Hadley: only communicated/known special circumstances.

**Items**

**C8-01 (expectation arithmetic).** Contractor agreed to build a deck for Owner for $100,000. Contractor's total cost to build would have been $75,000. After Contractor had spent $20,000 on custom materials that can't be resold, Owner repudiated. Contractor's expectation damages are?
A. $20,000, reliance only.
B. $25,000, the lost profit.
C. $45,000: profit of $25,000 plus costs incurred of $20,000.
D. $100,000, the contract price.
**Best: C.** (Equivalent: price $100,000 minus cost avoided $55,000.) A is reliance; B ignores costs already incurred; D ignores costs saved.

**C8-02 (Hadley).** A bakery hired a delivery firm to ship a broken oven part for repair. The delivery firm delayed, so the oven was idle for a week. The bakery had no backup oven and lost $12,000 in sales. The firm didn't know the oven was the bakery's only one. Bakery sues for the lost profits. Result?
A. Recovers $12,000; the loss was caused by the breach.
B. Recovers nothing for lost profits; the firm had no reason to know of the special circumstances at contracting, so the loss was not foreseeable.
C. Recovers $12,000 because lost profits are always direct damages.
D. Recovers nothing; delay is not a breach.
**Best: B.** (If the bakery had told the firm the oven was its only one, A.)

**C8-03 (cost to repair vs diminution).** Owner contracted for a 25-foot-deep pool; Builder built it 24 feet deep. The pool's value and usefulness are the same. Rebuilding would cost $60,000; value difference is $0. The pool is otherwise complete, the price $80,000, of which Owner paid $60,000. Builder demands the $20,000 balance. Result?
A. Builder gets nothing; breach was complete.
B. Builder recovers $20,000 less the diminution in value (zero); cost to remedy would be unreasonable economic waste.
C. Owner recovers the $60,000 cost to rebuild and owes Builder nothing more.
D. Owner may withhold the $20,000 balance as damages for the breach.
**Best: B.** [U: assumes no willful deviation; Peevyhouse-type cases are in tension with Jacob & Youngs.]

**C8-04 (new-business lost profits).** A startup coffee shop's equipment supplier delivered the espresso machine three months late. The owner sues for lost profits, supported by an expert using sales data from five comparable shops in the same area. Result?
A. Barred; lost profits of a new business are always too speculative.
B. May be recovered if proven with reasonable certainty; the comparable-business evidence can suffice.
C. Barred; consequential damages never available.
D. Awarded in full without proof.
**Best: B.**

**C8-05 (reliance when expectation uncertain).** A film producer relied on a venue's promise to host a premiere and spent $18,000 on promotion. The venue breached. The producer can't prove profits with certainty. The venue proves that, had the premiere happened, the producer would have lost $4,000 on it. The producer's reliance recovery is?
A. $18,000.
B. $14,000: reliance reduced by the loss the producer would have suffered anyway.
C. $4,000.
D. Nothing; reliance damages are unavailable.
**Best: B.**

**C8-06 (no emotional distress).** A couple hired a wedding photographer who failed to show up; the couple seek $50,000 for emotional distress and punitive damages. Result under the majority rule?
A. Recover both; weddings are emotional.
B. Recover contract damages (e.g., the cost of replacement and fee refund, plus possibly emotional damages in the few jurisdictions recognizing the wedding/funeral exception) but no punitive damages for breach of contract.
C. Recover punitive only.
D. Recover nothing.
**Best: B.** [U: the wedding-photographer emotional-distress exception is jurisdiction specific; teach the default: not recoverable.]

## 28. Mitigation / avoidable consequences (V.D) [R; V UCC 2-712(3), 2-715(2)(a), 2-708(2)]

- Damages are not recoverable for loss the injured party could have avoided **without undue risk, burden, or humiliation** (§350). The injured party may recover the **reasonable costs** of mitigation even if unsuccessful. The **breaching party bears the burden** of showing the loss could have been avoided.
- **Employment:** the discharged employee must seek **comparable** work (similar position, location, pay); need not accept a **different or inferior** one (Parker v. Twentieth Century-Fox: switching from a musical to a western). Earnings from other work reduce damages.
- **Anticipatory repudiation:** after a repudiation the injured party can't run up damages by continuing performance.
- **Lost-volume seller / lost-volume provider:** if the seller could have made both sales (unlimited supply), the second sale does not mitigate; seller recovers lost profit [V 2-708(2)]. Same at common law for service providers with excess capacity.
- **UCC:** buyer's failure to cover does not bar other remedies [V 2-712(3)], but **consequential damages** that **could have been prevented by cover** are not recoverable [V 2-715(2)(a)].

**Items**

**C8-07 (employment).** Taylor was wrongfully discharged from a five-year contract as a regional manager, with 2 years left at $120,000/year. Her employer showed that comparable regional-manager jobs paying $115,000 in the same city were available, and she refused them to take a $60,000 job in a different field. Her damages are most likely?
A. $240,000, the full unexpired wages.
B. About $10,000: unexpired wages ($240,000) less what she could have earned in the comparable jobs ($115,000 a year, or $230,000), plus reasonable job-search costs.
C. $120,000: unexpired wages less her $60,000-a-year earnings.
D. Nothing; she failed to mitigate.
**Best: B.** She must seek comparable work; the employer proved comparable jobs were available, so damages are reduced by what she could have earned. D overstates (the comparable jobs paid slightly less, so some loss remains). C credits only her actual earnings in an unrelated, inferior job.

**C8-08 (lost-volume seller).** A boat dealer with a large inventory contracted to sell a boat to Buyer for $40,000 (dealer's profit $7,000). Buyer repudiated; the dealer sold the same boat a week later to another customer for $40,000. Dealer sues. Result?
A. Nothing; no loss after resale.
B. $7,000; as a lost-volume seller, the dealer could have made both sales, so the resale does not offset the loss.
C. $40,000, the price.
D. The difference between market and contract price.
**Best: B.** 2-708(2).

**C8-09 (cover and consequentials).** A buyer relied on a seller's promise of 100 specialized valves by March 1, which the seller failed to deliver. Valves of equal quality were available from another supplier at a slightly higher price within days. The buyer did not buy them and its plant stood idle for six weeks, losing $200,000. Result?
A. Buyer recovers the $200,000.
B. Buyer cannot recover consequential damages that could reasonably have been prevented by cover; it recovers only what cover would have cost (the price difference) and incidentals.
C. Buyer recovers nothing.
D. Buyer recovers the $200,000 plus punitive damages.
**Best: B.**

## 29. Liquidated damages, penalties, limitation of remedies (V.C) [V UCC 2-718, 2-719; R §356]

- **Liquidated damages (LD):** enforceable if the amount is **reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss** and the **difficulty of proving loss** (Restatement §356). The UCC adds "**inconvenience or nonfeasibility of otherwise obtaining an adequate remedy**"; "A term fixing unreasonably large liquidated damages is void as a penalty" [V 2-718(1)]. Traditional two-part test (damages hard to estimate at formation + reasonable forecast); the modern test also looks at actual loss. Burden on the party challenging the clause. A valid LD clause is generally the **exclusive** measure; clauses allowing the **greater of** LD or actual damages are penalties. A penalty is unenforceable; plaintiff recovers actual damages.
- **Deposits / buyer's restitution after breach [V 2-718(2)-(3)]:** if the seller justifiably withholds delivery because of the buyer's breach, the buyer is entitled to restitution of the amount by which his payments exceed the amount of any valid LD, or, absent such terms, **20% of the value of the total performance for which the buyer is obligated, or $500, whichever is smaller**; subject to offset for the seller's proven damages and benefits the buyer received.
- **Limitation of remedies [V 2-719]:** parties may add or substitute remedies or limit them (repair/replace, return and refund), and a remedy is optional unless expressly exclusive. If an **exclusive or limited remedy fails of its essential purpose**, general UCC remedies are available. **Consequential damages** may be limited or excluded unless unconscionable; limiting consequential damages for injury to the person in **consumer goods** is prima facie unconscionable; commercial loss limits are not. [U: courts are split whether a consequential-damages exclusion survives when the exclusive remedy fails of its essential purpose; teach the text, not the split.]
- **UCC limitations period:** 4 years; by original agreement reducible to not less than 1 year [V 2-725].

**C8-10 (penalty).** A contract to supply $5,000 of office chairs provided that if Seller delivered late, Seller would pay $1,000,000. Seller delivered two weeks late; Buyer's actual loss was $400. Result?
A. Buyer recovers $1,000,000; parties are free to contract.
B. The clause is an unenforceable penalty; Buyer recovers actual damages.
C. Buyer recovers nothing.
D. Buyer recovers $1,000,000 minus $400.
**Best: B.**

**C8-11 (valid LD).** A highway paving contract set $5,000 for each day of delay beyond the completion date; the state's loss from road closure (lost toll revenue, detours, enforcement) was difficult to estimate and the daily sum approximated the likely loss. The contractor was 10 days late. The state proves actual loss of $80,000. Result?
A. State recovers $80,000.
B. State recovers $50,000; the LD clause is valid and exclusive.
C. State recovers $130,000.
D. State recovers $0; LD can't be used by governments.
**Best: B.**

**C8-12 (UCC deposit).** Buyer agreed to buy a machine for $5,000 and paid a $2,000 deposit. The contract had no liquidated-damages clause. Buyer repudiated, and Seller, who suffered no provable damages, kept the machine in inventory. Buyer demands its deposit back. Result?
A. Buyer recovers $2,000.
B. Buyer recovers $1,500: Seller may retain the smaller of 20% of the total price ($1,000) or $500, i.e., $500, absent proof of greater damages.
C. Buyer recovers nothing; the deposit is forfeited.
D. Buyer recovers $1,000.
**Best: B.** 2-718(2)(b), (3). (The seller may offset proven damages beyond that retention.)

**C8-13 (failure of essential purpose).** A sales contract limited the buyer's remedy to "repair or replacement of defective parts." The seller made five repair attempts, but the machine still wouldn't run. The buyer seeks damages. Result?
A. Buyer limited to repair forever.
B. Because the limited remedy has failed of its essential purpose, buyer may pursue other UCC remedies, including damages for breach of warranty.
C. Buyer recovers only the price.
D. Contract void.
**Best: B.** 2-719(2).

## 30. Equitable remedies: specific performance, injunction, rescission, reformation (V.E-F) [R; V UCC 2-716]

- **Specific performance (SP):** available when **damages are inadequate**: **land** (each parcel unique; available to either buyer or seller), **unique goods** (heirlooms, art, antiques), and under UCC "**where the goods are unique or in other proper circumstances**" (e.g., inability to cover, output or requirements contracts in a shortage; Laclede Gas), plus **replevin** if buyer can't cover after reasonable effort [V 2-716(1),(3)]. Conditions: terms definite, feasible to supervise, no unfair hardship, mutuality not required today; plaintiff must be ready, willing and able; equitable defenses (unclean hands, laches, unconscionability, mistake) apply. **Not available for personal services** (13th Amendment; supervision problems).
- **Injunction:** a court may enjoin a **unique-services** provider from working for a competitor (negative covenant; Lumley v. Wagner) if the services are unique and the injunction doesn't practically compel performance; requires an express or implied negative covenant; not available for ordinary employees; covenants not to compete are separately tested for reasonableness.
- **Rescission (as a remedy):** unwinding the contract for fraud, duress, mistake, failure of consideration, material breach. Requires **tender back/restoration** and prompt action; leaves party in pre-contract position with restitution. Under the UCC, rescission or rejection doesn't bar damages for fraud [V 2-721].
- **Reformation:** a court **rewrites** a writing to reflect the parties' actual agreement where a writing fails to express it due to **mutual mistake** or one party's mistake known to the other (scrivener's error); proof by clear and convincing evidence; defends against parol evidence rule; also reformation of overbroad terms is distinct. [V NextGen scope: "requirements for obtaining reformation of a contract when the parties inadvertently omit an agreed-upon term from the writing".]
- **Election of remedies:** a party can't recover both inconsistent remedies (rescission and expectation damages for the same loss), but fraud allows rescission plus damages in the UCC [V 2-721].

**C8-14 (land SP).** Vendor refused to close on a signed contract to sell her house for $400,000, a price within the market range. Buyer wants the house. Likely remedy?
A. Damages only; houses are fungible.
B. Specific performance; each parcel of land is unique.
C. Injunction against any sale.
D. Reformation.
**Best: B.**

**C8-15 (personal services).** A singer under a one-year exclusive contract with Label A breached by agreeing to perform for Label B. Label A seeks (i) an order compelling her to sing for A and (ii) an injunction barring her from singing for B. Likely result?
A. Both granted.
B. (i) denied (no specific performance of personal services); (ii) may be granted if her services are unique and the contract contains a negative covenant and the injunction doesn't amount to forcing her to work for A.
C. Neither can be granted.
D. (i) granted; (ii) denied.
**Best: B.**

**C8-16 (UCC SP).** In a shortage, a hospital's contract with a sole supplier required delivery of a unique reagent needed for diagnosis. The supplier repudiated and the hospital couldn't cover. Likely remedy?
A. Damages only.
B. Specific performance is available because the goods are unique or the circumstances are otherwise proper (inability to cover).
C. Rescission.
D. No remedy.
**Best: B.**

**C8-17 (rescission requires restoration).** Buyer bought a business based on seller's fraudulent financial statements and seeks rescission 18 months later after running the business into the ground and selling off its equipment. Result?
A. Rescission is automatic.
B. Rescission may be denied because Buyer cannot restore the status quo; damages for fraud remain available.
C. No remedy.
D. Reformation.
**Best: B.**

## 31. UCC seller's remedies (V, UCC) [V UCC 2-702-2-710, 2-723]

When the buyer wrongfully rejects or revokes, fails to pay when due, or repudiates [V 2-703], the seller may: **withhold delivery**; **stop delivery** in transit [V 2-705] (for **any** goods if the buyer is insolvent; for carload/truckload shipments if the buyer repudiates or fails to pay); **identify goods to the contract** or salvage unfinished goods [V 2-704]; **resell and recover damages** [V 2-706]; **recover damages for non-acceptance** [V 2-708] or **the price** [V 2-709]; **cancel**. **Insolvent buyer** [V 2-702]: refuse delivery except for cash; **reclaim goods** received on credit while insolvent by demand within **10 days** of receipt (10-day limit waived if the buyer misrepresented solvency **in writing within 3 months** before delivery), subject to good-faith purchasers; successful reclamation excludes other remedies as to those goods.

**Resale damages [V 2-706]:** contract price − resale price + incidental damages − expenses saved. Resale must be in **good faith and commercially reasonable**; private sale requires reasonable notice to the buyer; public sale: notice of time and place, seller may buy at public sale; good-faith purchaser takes free of the original buyer's rights.
**Market damages [V 2-708(1)]:** (unpaid contract price − market price at time and place for tender) + incidentals − expenses saved. Market means price at the time and place for tender; if proof of that price isn't available, comparable [V 2-723].
**Lost profits [V 2-708(2)]:** if (1) is inadequate to put the seller in as good a position as performance, then **profit (including reasonable overhead)** + incidentals, with credit for payments or proceeds of resale (lost-volume sellers; component sellers who would not otherwise have produced).
**Action for the price [V 2-709]:** only for (a) goods **accepted**, or conforming goods **lost or damaged within a commercially reasonable time after risk of loss passed** to the buyer; (b) goods **identified to the contract** if the seller is **unable after reasonable effort to resell** at a reasonable price or circumstances show effort would be unavailing (e.g., custom goods). Seller must hold identified goods for buyer, credit net proceeds on resale.
**Incidental damages [V 2-710]:** commercially reasonable charges for stopping delivery, transportation, care and custody of goods after breach, return or resale.

**C8-18 (resale).** Buyer wrongfully refused to accept goods under a $10,000 contract. Seller resold them at a commercially reasonable private sale, after notifying Buyer, for $7,000 and incurred $300 in storage and sale costs. Seller saved nothing. Seller's damages?
A. $3,000.
B. $3,300.
C. $10,000.
D. $7,300.
**Best: B.** (price $10,000 − resale $7,000 + incidental $300).

**C8-19 (market damages).** Same contract, but Seller did not resell. The market price at the time and place for tender was $8,000. Seller had $100 of expenses saved by not shipping and $200 in incidental costs. Damages?
A. $2,000.
B. $2,100.
C. $2,300.
D. $10,000.
**Best: B.** ($10,000 − $8,000 = $2,000; + $200 incidentals − $100 saved).

**C8-20 (insolvent buyer).** A wholesaler delivered $50,000 of goods on credit to a retailer on June 1. On June 5, the wholesaler learned that the retailer had been insolvent on June 1. On June 12 the wholesaler demanded the goods back. The retailer had not made any written misrepresentation. Can the wholesaler reclaim?
A. Yes; any time.
B. No; the demand came more than 10 days after receipt, and no written misrepresentation of solvency within the 3 months before delivery extends the period.
C. Yes; within 30 days.
D. No; reclamation is never allowed.
**Best: B.** 2-702(2). (If demand had been June 8, yes.)

**C8-21 (action for price).** Buyer ordered 200 custom-printed brochures with its logo and refused to take them. Printer can't resell them. Printer sues for the $9,000 price. Result?
A. Printer recovers only market damages.
B. Printer recovers the price; identified goods that cannot be resold at a reasonable price after reasonable effort (or when effort would be unavailing).
C. Printer recovers nothing.
D. Printer recovers the price only if buyer accepted the goods.
**Best: B.** 2-709(1)(b).

## 32. UCC buyer's remedies (V, UCC) [V UCC 2-711-2-717, 2-502, 2-723]

Where the seller fails to deliver or repudiates, or the buyer rightfully rejects or justifiably revokes acceptance [V 2-711]: **cancel**, recover the price paid, and **either cover** [V 2-712] **or recover market damages for non-delivery** [V 2-713]; also recover **identified goods** if the seller becomes insolvent within 10 days of receiving the first installment of the price [V 2-502] and **specific performance/replevin** [V 2-716]. After rightful rejection/revocation the buyer has a **security interest** in goods in his possession for payments made and expenses [V 2-711(3)].
- **Cover** [V 2-712]: buyer makes **in good faith and without unreasonable delay** any reasonable purchase of substitute goods; damages = (cost of cover − contract price) + incidental + consequential − expenses saved. Failure to cover doesn't bar other remedies [V 2-712(3)].
- **Market damages** [V 2-713]: (market price at the time the buyer learned of the breach − contract price) + incidental + consequential − expenses saved. Market is measured at the **place for tender** (or place of arrival for rejection/ revocation). [U: for repudiation the "time buyer learned of the breach" is read by many courts as after a commercially reasonable time to await performance; 2-723 offers the pre-trial rule. Teach the formula; avoid items turning on this nuance.]
- **Accepted goods** [V 2-714]: buyer must give notice [V 2-607(3)(a)]; damages = loss resulting in the ordinary course from the breach "determined in any manner which is reasonable"; for **breach of warranty**, the difference at the time and place of acceptance between the value of the goods **as accepted** and the value **as warranted**, unless special circumstances show proximate damages of a different amount; incidental and consequential in a proper case.
- **Incidental** [V 2-715(1)]: inspection, receipt, transport, care of rejected goods; commercially reasonable charges in effecting cover; other reasonable expenses.
- **Consequential** [V 2-715(2)]: requirements and needs the seller had reason to know of at contracting that couldn't be prevented by cover; personal injury or property damage proximately resulting from warranty breach.
- **Deduct damages from the price** still due under the same contract on notice [V 2-717].

**C8-22 (cover).** Seller failed to deliver 1,000 units of a component at $5 each ($5,000 total). Buyer, acting in good faith and without delay, bought 1,000 substitutes at $7 each and paid $100 for expedited shipping. Buyer's damages?
A. $2,000.
B. $2,100.
C. $7,100.
D. $100.
**Best: B.** (Cover $7,000 − contract $5,000 + incidental $100.)

**C8-23 (accepted goods).** A buyer accepted and kept a commercial freezer, gave timely notice of the defect, and proved the freezer, as warranted, would have been worth $10,000, while as delivered it was worth $6,000. The buyer also proved $500 in reasonable incidental costs. Damages?
A. $4,000.
B. $4,500.
C. $10,500.
D. $6,500.
**Best: B.** 2-714(2) ($10,000 − $6,000) + 2-715(1) incidentals ($500).

**C8-24 (consequential).** Seller promised to deliver a specialized cutting blade by April 1 and knew, from the contract negotiations, that Buyer would use it to fill a $90,000 order with a firm deadline of April 15; no equivalent blade could be obtained elsewhere. Seller failed to deliver. Buyer lost the $90,000 order's profit of $22,000. Buyer's best argument for recovering the $22,000?
A. Not recoverable; lost profits are never recoverable.
B. Recoverable as consequential damages; Seller had reason to know of the particular needs at contracting and the loss couldn't reasonably be prevented by cover.
C. Recoverable only as incidental damages.
D. Recoverable as punitive damages.
**Best: B.** 2-715(2)(a).

**C8-25 (price deduction).** Buyer owes the second installment of $3,000 on a contract; the first delivery was nonconforming, and Buyer suffered $1,000 in damages. Buyer notifies Seller it intends to deduct $1,000 from the second installment. Seller objects. Result?
A. Buyer may not self-help.
B. Buyer may deduct damages from the price still due under the same contract after notifying Seller.
C. Buyer may deduct only with a court order.
D. Buyer may deduct only if the contract allows.
**Best: B.** 2-717.

# PART G. THIRD-PARTY RIGHTS (MBE VI)

## 33. Third-party beneficiaries (VI.A) [R; V NextGen scope; V UCC 2-318]

- **Intended beneficiary** (Restatement §302): recognition of a right to performance in the beneficiary is **appropriate to effectuate the intention of the parties**, and **either** (a) performance of the promise will **satisfy an obligation of the promisee to pay money to the beneficiary** (the old "creditor beneficiary") **or** (b) the circumstances indicate the **promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit of the promised performance** (the old "donee beneficiary"). Anyone else who benefits is an **incidental beneficiary** with no enforceable rights. The Second Restatement drops the creditor/donee labels but exams still use them as shorthand.
- **Enforcement:** an intended beneficiary can sue the **promisor** on the promise (and the **promisee** too, e.g., for specific performance or, in a creditor case, on the underlying debt). The beneficiary's rights are **derivative**: the promisor can assert against the beneficiary any **defenses** it has against the promisee that arise from the contract (lack of consideration, fraud, failure of a condition, the promisee's breach) [V NextGen: "defenses against claims asserted by intended beneficiaries"].
- **Vesting** (§311): until vesting, the promisor and promisee can **modify or discharge** the beneficiary's rights by agreement. Rights **vest** when the beneficiary (i) **materially changes position in justifiable reliance** on the promise, (ii) **brings suit**, or (iii) **manifests assent** to the promise at the request of the promisor or promisee. The contract may make the rights irrevocable from the start. (Older, minority approach: rights vest at formation.)
- **Typical intended beneficiaries:** life insurance beneficiaries; a lender's right when a buyer assumes the seller's mortgage; a child for whom a parent contracts for services; a third party named to receive payment. **Typical incidental:** neighbors who'd benefit from a landscaping contract; members of the public in a government contract (unless the contract shows intent to benefit them); future owners of a building.
- **UCC warranty extension** 2-318 [V]: see section 19.

**Traps:** "benefit" is not enough; intent to benefit is; vesting limits modification; promisor's defenses; the beneficiary doesn't need consideration; third-party beneficiary vs assignee (TPB's rights arise from the original contract; assignee gets them by transfer).

**Items**

**C9-01 (creditor TPB).** Borrower owed Bank $50,000. Borrower sold his shop to Ryan, and Ryan promised Borrower to pay the Bank debt as part of the price. Ryan failed to pay. Bank sues Ryan. Result?
A. For Ryan; Bank isn't a party.
B. For Bank; Bank is an intended beneficiary because Ryan's performance would satisfy Borrower's obligation to pay Bank.
C. For Ryan; incidental beneficiaries can't enforce.
D. For Bank only if Borrower assigned the claim.
**Best: B.**

**C9-02 (incidental).** The city contracted with ServeCo to resurface Main Street. A shop owner on Main Street lost sales when ServeCo finished three months late. She sues ServeCo for breach. Result?
A. For the shop owner; she was harmed.
B. For ServeCo; the shop owner is merely an incidental beneficiary.
C. For the shop owner; she is a creditor beneficiary.
D. For ServeCo only if the contract excluded third-party claims.
**Best: B.**

**C9-03 (vesting).** Mom and Bank agreed that Bank would pay Mom's son Ted $40,000 upon Mom's death. Ted didn't know of the agreement. Before Ted learned of it, Mom and Bank agreed to cancel the arrangement. Ted sues Bank. Under the Restatement (Second) approach?
A. For Ted; his rights vested at formation.
B. For Bank; Ted hadn't relied, assented, or sued, so his rights hadn't vested and the parties could discharge them.
C. For Ted; a donee beneficiary's rights can never be changed.
D. For Bank only if Mom was insolvent.
**Best: B.** [U: stem should name the vesting approach; a minority follows vesting at formation.]

**C9-04 (promisor's defenses).** Buyer promised Seller to pay $30,000 to Seller's daughter Dee for a boat. Seller delivered a boat Buyer reasonably rejected as nonconforming, and Buyer refused to pay. Dee sues Buyer. Result?
A. For Dee; she is an intended beneficiary and defenses against Seller are irrelevant.
B. For Buyer; an intended beneficiary's rights are subject to the promisor's defenses against the promisee, including the promisee's failure to perform.
C. For Dee; a donee beneficiary is not subject to defenses.
D. For Buyer; Dee can't sue because she gave no consideration.
**Best: B.**

## 34. Assignment of rights and delegation of duties (VI.B) [V UCC 2-210; R Restatement §§317-333; V NextGen scope]

**Assignment** = present transfer of a contract **right** by the **assignor** (obligee) to the **assignee**; the obligor owes performance to the assignee.
- **Form:** no particular words, no consideration needed (consideration affects revocability); intent to transfer **present** rights (not a mere promise to assign in the future); may be oral unless a statute (SoF) requires a writing.
- **What can be assigned:** generally all rights, **except** where the assignment would **materially change the duty** of the obligor, **materially increase the burden or risk**, **materially impair the obligor's chance of obtaining return performance**, or is **forbidden by statute/public policy** or **validly precluded by the contract** [V UCC 2-210(2) language; Restatement §317]. Rights to **personal services** (e.g., a particular person's services), rights under requirements contracts where the assignee's needs differ, and rights where the obligor has a personal interest are not assignable. **Right to payment** is almost always assignable. A **right to damages for breach of the whole contract** or a **right arising from the assignor's due performance of the entire obligation** can be assigned **despite a contract prohibition** [V 2-210(2)].
- **Anti-assignment clauses** [R Restatement §322; V UCC 2-210(3); NextGen "effect of clauses prohibiting assignment or delegation"]: construed narrowly. A prohibition of assignment of "**the contract**" is read as barring only **delegation** [V UCC 2-210(3)]. A clause prohibiting assignment of **rights**, unless a different intention is manifested, gives the obligor a **right to damages** but **does not render the assignment ineffective** (Restatement §322(2)(b)); many courts require **clear, specific language** (e.g., that assignments are "void" or the assignor "has no power") to invalidate the assignment. UCC 9-406/9-408 override anti-assignment terms for accounts and payment intangibles. [U: states vary; item stems should name the rule or use unambiguous "void" language.]
- **Effect on the obligor:** assignee acquires the assignor's rights **subject to all defenses and setoffs** the obligor has against the assignor arising from the contract (and, those arising before the obligor has notice). **Notice:** before notice, the obligor may discharge by paying the assignor; **after** notice, must pay the assignee (paying the assignor afterward doesn't discharge).
- **Successive assignments** of the same right [R Restatement §342]: the **first assignee prevails**, except where the first assignment was gratuitous and revocable and has been revoked, or a later assignee for value without notice gets payment, a judgment, a new promise (novation) from the obligor, or possession of a token/document. (Minority "English rule": first to give notice wins.)
- **Gratuitous assignments** are **revocable** (by assignor's later assignment, death/ incapacity, notice or demand for payment) unless: **consideration** was given, the assignment is in a **signed writing delivered** or evidenced by a token or document, the assignee **detrimentally relied**, or the obligor has **already performed** to the assignee [R §332; V NextGen scope: "requirements for revocation of gratuitous assignments"].
- **Warranties of the assignor** (for value) [R §333]: that the right exists and is subject to no undisclosed defense, that the assignor won't impair it, etc.

**Delegation** = transfer of a **duty** (performance) to a **delegate**.
- **Allowed** unless (i) performance is **personal** (unique skill, judgment, or personal trust; the obligee has a **substantial interest in having the original promisor perform or control the acts** [V 2-210(1)]), (ii) the contract **prohibits** delegation, or (iii) statute/public policy bars it. Routine, impersonal duties (paying money, delivering standard goods, painting a house) are delegable.
- **The delegator remains liable** (no release) unless the obligee agrees to a **novation** [V 2-210(1): "No delegation of performance relieves the party delegating of any duty to perform or any liability for breach."].
- **Assignment of "the contract"** or "all my rights under the contract" = assignment of rights **and** delegation of duties; the assignee's acceptance is a **promise to perform** the duties, enforceable by the assignor or the other original party [V 2-210(4)]. The other party may treat a delegation as **reasonable grounds for insecurity** and demand adequate assurance from the assignee [V 2-210(5), 2-609].
- The obligee (and the delegator) can enforce the delegatee's promise: obligee as an **intended beneficiary** of the assumption.

**Traps:** assignee takes subject to defenses; notice rule; delegation doesn't discharge; "assignment of the contract" = both; personal services; anti-assignment clause scope; revocability of gratuitous assignment; novation vs delegation; payment rights vs performance duties.

**C9-05 (notice).** Contractor assigned to Bank his right to a $50,000 final payment from Owner. Bank notified Owner. Owner, ignoring the notice, paid Contractor. Contractor spent the money. Bank sues Owner. Result?
A. For Owner; payment to the original obligee discharged the debt.
B. For Bank; after notice of assignment, Owner must pay the assignee, and paying the assignor does not discharge the obligation.
C. For Owner; assignments of payment rights are invalid.
D. For Bank only if the assignment was in writing.
**Best: B.** (Had Owner paid before notice, A.)

**C9-06 (defenses).** Seller assigned to Finance Co. the right to payment from Buyer for a defective machine. Buyer refuses to pay Finance Co., asserting the machine's defects. Result?
A. Finance Co. wins; assignee takes free of defenses.
B. Buyer wins; the assignee takes subject to defenses that Buyer has against the assignor arising from the contract (absent a valid waiver-of-defenses clause or a holder-in-due-course situation not at issue).
C. Finance Co. wins because the assignment was for value.
D. Buyer wins only if the machine was not accepted.
**Best: B.**

**C9-07 (personal services).** Dr. Lane, a renowned architect, contracted to design Client's museum. Lane purported to assign her right to the fee to Bank and to delegate the design to a junior architect. Client objects to the delegation. Result?
A. Lane may delegate; delegation is always allowed.
B. The assignment of the right to the fee is generally valid; the delegation of design duties may be barred because Client has a substantial interest in having the particular architect perform; if allowed, Lane remains liable.
C. Both invalid.
D. Both valid; Lane is released.
**Best: B.**

**C9-08 (anti-assignment).** A supply contract said: "Neither party may assign this contract without the other's written consent." Without consent, Supplier assigned to a bank the right to receive payments, and notified Buyer. Under the Restatement/UCC approach, effect on the assignment of payment rights?
A. The assignment of payment rights is effective; a prohibition on assigning "the contract" is read as barring delegation of performance only.
B. The assignment is void; any anti-assignment clause voids assignments.
C. The assignment is effective but Buyer may rescind the contract.
D. The assignment is void unless Buyer consents in writing.
**Best: A.** (If the clause said "any purported assignment of rights is void," then B in many jurisdictions.)

**C9-09 (gratuitous assignment).** Aunt orally assigned to Niece, as a gift, her right to receive a $10,000 payment from Debtor. Before Debtor was notified and before Niece did anything, Aunt collected the $10,000 from Debtor. Niece sues Aunt. Result?
A. For Niece; assignments are irrevocable.
B. For Aunt; a gratuitous assignment is revocable, and Aunt's collection revoked it (no writing delivered, token, or reliance).
C. For Niece; Debtor is liable twice.
D. For Aunt; assignments of debts are invalid.
**Best: B.**

**C9-10 (delegation; liability).** Painter contracted to paint Owner's house for $4,000 and delegated the job to Painter 2, who agreed with Painter to do it. Painter 2 did a poor job. Owner sues. Who is liable?
A. Painter only; Painter 2 was not a party.
B. Painter remains liable on the original contract; Owner may also sue Painter 2 as an intended beneficiary of Painter 2's promise to Painter.
C. Painter 2 only; Painter was released by delegation.
D. Neither; delegation voids the contract.
**Best: B.**

**C9-11 (successive assignments; majority).** On Monday, Alex assigned for value a $20,000 account owed by Debtor to Bank 1. On Tuesday, Alex assigned the same account for value to Bank 2, which had no notice of the first assignment and notified Debtor immediately. Debtor has not yet paid anyone. Under the majority (Restatement) rule, who prevails?
A. Bank 2, the first to notify.
B. Bank 1, the first assignee in time.
C. Bank 2, the later assignee.
D. They share equally.
**Best: B.** (Under the "English rule" minority, A.) [U: the stem must name the rule.]

# 39. Handoff notes for the schema and item authoring

- `status` for our own items: `general` (own authoring from primary law); `src` examples: `"UCC 2-207"`, `"Restatement (Second) of Contracts §90"`, `"NCBE MBE Outline: Contracts I.A"`. Do not use `exam` or `book` unless the item really comes from a released question or a licensed text.
- Item stems should always be graded "best answer" style: "Which of the following is the buyer's best argument?", "Is X likely to prevail?", "Which statement is most accurate?" with four options (MBE) and, for NextGen-tagged items, a select-two-of-six variant.
- When a doctrine is split, include the governing approach in the stem ("assume the jurisdiction follows Restatement (Second) §89"). NCBE does the same.
- Teach-it-in-one-breath lines (draft): "Ads are invitations unless they are definite and say first-come." "Mailbox: acceptance on dispatch, everything else on receipt." "No consideration for past help." "UCC 2-207: acceptance wins unless expressly conditional; extra terms are proposals; between merchants they join unless the offer limits, they materially alter, or objection." "Perfect tender, but cure." "Risk of loss follows delivery, not title." "Expectation = profit plus costs incurred." "Consequential damages need foreseeability at contracting." "Assignee steps into the assignor's shoes, defenses and all." "Delegator never leaves."
- Mascot moods can map to: `happy` on correct, `sad` on wrong, `cheer` on streak or exam-ready milestone.
