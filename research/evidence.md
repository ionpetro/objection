- Older 2022 draft NextGen annotation on confrontation (worth teaching, it is the clearest NCBE statement): the Clause gives criminal defendants (not civil litigants) a right to confront witnesses; it limits hearsay offered by the prosecution; it does not limit evidence offered by a defendant against the state or any evidence in civil cases; "testimonial" includes formal statements made during litigation, statements to law enforcement in connection with a criminal investigation, and statements to government agents to establish facts related to a crime, but not statements offered for a non-truth purpose, opposing-party statements or co-conspirator statements; evidence subject to the Clause must also satisfy the hearsay rules; a prosecutor can introduce statements over Confrontation objection if the declarant testifies (is cross-examinable) or is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine [READ: nextgen.txt p.29, 2023 version; the 2025 version only says "right to confront witnesses and limitations on testimonial hearsay pursuant to the confrontation clause of the Sixth Amendment in criminal cases"].

### 1.5 Which edition of the FRE, and what changed recently
- Current edition: **FRE as amended Dec 1, 2024** [READ: uscourts.gov FRE page, http://www.uscourts.gov/forms-rules/current-rules-practice-procedure/federal-rules-evidence says "last amended in 2024"]. I found no evidence-rule amendment effective Dec 1, 2025 and none listed as pending on that page [READ, but absence-of-evidence: verify once more at uscourts.gov before launch].
- **Amendments the exam can now test (text read at LII):**
  - **FRE 702 (eff. Dec 1, 2023):** proponent must show "more likely than not" that (a) helpful, (b) sufficient facts or data, (c) reliable principles and methods, (d) "the expert's opinion reflects a reliable application." Fixes the "it goes to weight not admissibility" error [READ rule text; amendment history on LII is stale but the text is the 2023 text].
  - **FRE 106 (eff. Dec 1, 2023):** completeness now covers "any other statement" (oral too), and the adverse party may require the other part "over a hearsay objection." Court may still allow completion later [READ rule + 2023 committee note].
  - **FRE 615 (eff. Dec 1, 2023):** sequestration order may also bar disclosure of testimony to excluded witnesses and bar excluded witnesses from accessing testimony; entity party gets only ONE designated representative; includes virtual trials [READ].
  - **FRE 107 (new, eff. Dec 1, 2024):** "illustrative aids" (not "demonstrative evidence"): allowed if utility in assisting comprehension is not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion, delay, etc.; not evidence; not sent to the jury room unless all parties consent or good cause; entered in the record when practicable [READ].
  - **FRE 613(b) (eff. Dec 1, 2024):** extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is not admissible "until after the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny" and the adverse party can examine about it, unless the court orders otherwise; does not apply to 801(d)(2) statements [READ rule + committee note: before 2024 the rule had no sequence requirement].
  - **FRE 801(d)(2) (eff. Dec 1, 2024):** new last sentence: if a party's claim, defense, or potential liability is directly derived from a declarant or the declarant's principal, a statement admissible against the declarant/principal is admissible against the party (estates, assignees, trustees). Does not apply to statements made after the transfer or an agent's post-termination statements [READ rule + note].
  - **FRE 1006 (eff. Dec 1, 2024):** summaries of voluminous materials are admissible as evidence whether or not the underlying materials were admitted; underlying originals or duplicates must be made available; pure illustrative summaries fall under Rule 107 [READ].
  - Earlier but still "new" to some outlines: 801(d)(1)(B)(ii) (2014: prior consistent statements to rehabilitate on other grounds), 803(16) ancient documents cut-off Jan 1, 1998 (2017), 902(13)-(14) electronic records certification (2017), 807 residual restructured (2019), 404(b)(3) notice must state purpose and reasoning, in writing (2020) [READ].
- **Proposed FRE 707 (machine-generated / AI evidence): NOT in force.** Published for comment Aug 2025; public hearings Jan 2026; comments closed Feb 2026 [SECONDARY]. One article says that after May 2026 the Advisory Committee made "significant revisions" and decided the new version must be republished, making Dec 2029 the earliest effective date [SECONDARY, https://texaslawreview.org/?p=10051]; a different blog claimed Dec 1, 2027 effect [SECONDARY, contradicted]. LII has no Rule 707 page [READ: 404]. **Do not teach 707 as law; at most a "not tested" side note.** [UNSURE on exact timeline.]
- **MBE-unfriendly differences to avoid:** The MBE applies the FRE only. Do not teach state evidence code differences (Dead Man's statutes, state privileges, state character rules, Frye states) as tested. FRE 501 and 601 say state law governs privilege and competency for state-law claims in federal civil cases; the MBE ignores that nuance except as a trap about which law applies (see EV-34).

## 2. Presentation of evidence (about 6 items; official weight 1/4)

### 2.1 Rules and elements
- **FRE 103 preserving error:** timely objection or motion to strike + specific ground unless apparent; for excluded evidence, an **offer of proof** unless the substance was apparent; once the court rules definitively (incl. pretrial in limine), no need to renew; plain error can be noticed [READ].
- **FRE 104:** (a) the judge decides preliminary questions on qualification, privilege, admissibility, **not bound by evidence rules except privileges** (so the judge may consider hearsay to decide a hearsay-exception fact; Bourjaily v. United States, 483 U.S. 171 (1987): preponderance standard, [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/483/171]); (b) **conditional relevance**: evidence "sufficient to support a finding" that the fact exists (jury could reasonably find it): used for authentication, personal knowledge, and whether the defendant committed an uncharged act under 404(b) (Huddleston v. United States, 485 U.S. 681 (1988) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/485/681]). (c) hearing outside jury for confessions, when a testifying criminal defendant requests, or when justice requires.
- **FRE 105 limited admissibility:** on timely request the court must restrict the evidence to its proper scope and instruct [READ].
- **FRE 106 completeness:** see 1.5.
- **FRE 201 judicial notice (adjudicative facts only):** not subject to reasonable dispute because generally known in the trial court's territorial jurisdiction OR accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned; court must take notice if requested and supplied with the information; any stage; party entitled to be heard on request; **civil: jury must accept noticed fact as conclusive; criminal: jury may or may not accept as conclusive** [READ].
- **FRE 301 presumptions (civil):** the party against whom a presumption is directed bears the burden of **producing** evidence to rebut it; burden of persuasion does not shift [READ]. (Criminal-case presumptions are a due process matter; leave to Con Law/Crim.)
- **FRE 601:** every person is competent (no Dead Man's statute in federal rules; state law governs competency only for state-law claims in civil cases) [READ]. **602:** personal knowledge, "evidence sufficient to support a finding," may be the witness's own testimony; does not apply to experts under 703 [READ]. **603** oath/affirmation; **604** interpreter; **605** the presiding judge cannot testify (no objection needed); **606(a)** a juror may not testify as a witness at the trial; **606(b)** at an inquiry into a verdict a juror may not testify about statements/incidents in deliberations, effect on votes, or mental processes; may testify about (A) extraneous prejudicial information, (B) outside influence, (C) mistake entering the verdict [READ]. Racial-bias exception (Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado, 2017) and Warger v. Shauers (2014) on voir dire dishonesty are from [MEMORY].
- **FRE 607:** any party, including the party who called the witness, may impeach [READ]. **610:** religious beliefs/opinions not admissible to attack or support credibility [READ].
- **FRE 611:** (a) court controls mode and order (effective, avoid waste, protect witnesses); (b) **cross-examination should not go beyond the subject matter of direct and credibility matters; court may allow additional matters "as if on direct"** (federal restrictive rule, not the wide-open rule) ; (c) **leading questions should not be used on direct except as necessary; ordinarily allowed on cross and when a party calls a hostile witness, an adverse party, or a witness identified with an adverse party** [READ].
- **FRE 612 refreshing recollection:** any writing (or thing) used to refresh memory **while testifying** (or before, if justice requires): the adverse party may have it produced, inspect it, cross-examine on it, and introduce **portions that relate to the testimony**; irrelevant portions deleted after in camera review; if not produced, court may make any order; in a criminal case, if the prosecution does not comply, the court **must** strike the testimony or declare a mistrial [READ]. The writing need not be admissible; the testimony after refreshing is the evidence. **Do not confuse with 803(5)** (record is read into evidence).
- **FRE 613 prior statements:** (a) no need to show the statement to the witness; must disclose to opposing counsel on request; (b) extrinsic evidence only after the witness had a chance to explain/deny and the adverse party can examine (2024), not required for 801(d)(2) statements [READ].
- **FRE 614 court may call/examine witnesses;** objection can wait until the jury is out [READ]. **FRE 615** exclusion of witnesses on request; exceptions: natural-person party, ONE designated entity representative, person essential to the claim, person authorized by statute [READ].
- **FRE 701 lay opinion:** (a) rationally based on perception; (b) helpful; (c) **not based on scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge within Rule 702** [READ]. Examples that are fine: speed of a car, sobriety, handwriting familiarity (901(b)(2)), a business owner's lost-profit opinion [MEMORY: 2000 committee note].
- **FRE 702-706 experts:** see section 3.4.

### 2.2 Impeachment, contradiction, rehabilitation (outline I.D): every method and its traps
1. **Prior inconsistent statement (PIS).** Cross about it needs no showing (613(a)). Extrinsic proof (another witness, document) only after the witness had a chance to explain or deny (613(b), 2024) and only on a non-collateral matter in practice [collateral-matter doctrine is common-law; MEMORY]. **Used only to impeach, not for truth, unless 801(d)(1)(A) applies** (inconsistent statement given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding or **deposition**, e.g., grand jury). A statement to police, in an affidavit, or in an unsworn interview is NOT given "at a proceeding." Trap: a party-opponent's own statement is admissible for truth under 801(d)(2) and needs no 613(b) foundation.
2. **Bias, interest, motive to lie.** Always allowed; extrinsic evidence allowed [READ: United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45 (1984), https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/469/45: bias impeachment permitted although the Rules do not mention it; shared membership in a secret prison gang offered to show bias; extrinsic evidence permitted]. Liability insurance (411) may come in to show bias; settlement (408(b)) may show bias.
3. **Conviction (609).** (a)(1): crime punishable by death or imprisonment **more than one year** ("in the convicting jurisdiction"): **non-defendant witness (civil or criminal): admitted subject to 403** (the court may exclude only if prejudice *substantially* outweighs); **criminal defendant-witness: admitted only if probative value outweighs prejudicial effect to the defendant** (a standard that favors exclusion). (a)(2): **any crime regardless of punishment if an element required proving or admitting a dishonest act or false statement: must be admitted, no balancing** (crimen falsi: perjury, false statement, fraud, forgery, embezzlement, false pretense; the 2006 committee note says bank robbery and larceny are NOT crimes of dishonesty) [READ rule and note]. (b) more than 10 years from conviction or release from confinement (whichever is later): admissible only if probative value **substantially outweighs** prejudice, with specific facts, and reasonable written notice. (c) pardon/annulment on a finding of rehabilitation (and no later felony) or innocence bars it. (d) juvenile adjudication: only criminal case, witness other than the accused, would be admissible for an adult, and necessary to fairly determine guilt. (e) pending appeal does not bar. Scope: normally name of crime, date, sentence, not the underlying details [MEMORY]. Preservation: a defendant must **testify** to preserve a claim that a 609 ruling was wrong (Luce v. United States, 469 U.S. 38 (1984) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/469/38]).
4. **Specific instances of conduct probative of truthfulness (608(b)).** Only on **cross-examination**, in the court's discretion, and **extrinsic evidence is not admissible** to prove them (convictions under 609 excepted); the cross-examiner is stuck with the answer. The witness keeps the Fifth Amendment privilege for testimony that relates only to credibility. Trap: arrests and accusations are not conduct; the conduct itself must bear on truthfulness (lying on a loan application: yes; a bar fight: no).
5. **Character for truthfulness by reputation or opinion (608(a)).** Either side may attack by reputation/opinion witness; **evidence of truthful character is admissible only after the witness's character for truthfulness has been attacked** (by reputation/opinion evidence, bad acts, conviction; whether bias or mere contradiction is an "attack" is for the judge) [READ rule; the "attack" scope: MEMORY].
6. **Defects of perception, memory, narration** (poor eyesight, distance, lighting, drugs, bad hearing): always relevant; extrinsic evidence allowed.
7. **Contradiction** (showing the testimony is wrong with other evidence): fine when the contradicting fact is relevant to the case; barred as "collateral" when it only shows the witness wrong on an irrelevant detail (e.g., what color shirt she wore) [MEMORY]. Trap: the contradiction route cannot be used to sneak around 608(b).
8. **Impeaching a hearsay declarant (806).** If a hearsay statement (or an 801(d)(2)(C), (D), (E) statement) is admitted, the declarant can be impeached as if testifying, including by an inconsistent statement made at **any** time and without a chance to deny. If the party against whom the statement was admitted calls the declarant, the party may examine as on cross [READ].
9. **Rehabilitation.** Truthful character after attack (608(a)); prior consistent statement under 801(d)(1)(B)(i) to rebut an express or implied charge of **recent** fabrication or improper influence/motive, only if the statement was made **before the motive to fabricate arose** (Tome v. United States, 513 U.S. 150 (1995) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-6892.ZO.html]); or (ii) to rehabilitate on other grounds such as inconsistency or faulty memory (2014 amendment). Explanation on redirect.
10. **Impeachment limits:** 610 religion; sexual-conduct limits (412); impeachment cannot be used to put in otherwise inadmissible substantive evidence (a prosecutor may not call a witness mainly as a subterfuge to place inadmissible hearsay before the jury) [MEMORY].

### 2.3 Traps (presentation)
- Refreshing (612) vs recorded recollection (803(5)): the first makes the *testimony* evidence; the second makes the *record* evidence (read aloud; exhibit only if the adverse party offers it).
- 613(b) vs 608(b): extrinsic evidence is barred for specific-instance bad acts (608(b)) but allowed for prior inconsistent statements about material facts (613(b)), bias, and convictions.
- 609(a)(1) has *opposite* balancing standards for defendants vs everyone else.
- "Crimen falsi" crimes come in automatically even as misdemeanors, with no 403 discretion.
- Cross scope: federal court restricts cross to direct + credibility; leading is allowed on cross but if the court lets the cross-examiner go beyond the scope, the questions are "as if on direct" (no leading) [rule text: "as if on direct"].
- Judicial notice in a civil case: conclusive. Criminal: not conclusive.
- Presumption (civil, 301): shifts only the burden of production.
- Offer of proof is required to preserve error when evidence is excluded; a specific objection is required when evidence is admitted.

## 3. Relevancy and reasons for excluding relevant evidence (about 8 items; official weight 1/3)

### 3.1 Relevance, 403, stipulations (FRE 401-403)
- 401: relevant if (a) any tendency to make a fact more or less probable than without it, and (b) the fact is of consequence in determining the action [READ]. 402: relevant evidence admissible unless Constitution, federal statute, FRE, or Supreme Court rules say otherwise; irrelevant evidence is inadmissible [READ].
- 403: the court **may** exclude relevant evidence if probative value is **substantially outweighed** by unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence [READ]. (Note the burden is heavy: "substantially outweighed"; prejudice that merely hurts the opponent is not "unfair".)
- Evidence stays relevant even if the opponent offers to stipulate, but the court weighs the offer as part of probative value. Old Chief v. United States, 519 U.S. 172 (1997): where the only purpose of the prior-conviction evidence is to prove felon status for a felon-in-possession count, the court abuses its discretion under 403 by refusing the defendant's offer to stipulate/admit the status, because the name/nature of the offense risks an improper verdict [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/519/172]. Narrow: limited to felon-status cases. NextGen scope repeats this example.
- 104(b) conditional relevance (see 2.1).

### 3.2 Character and other acts (FRE 404-405, 412-415)
**404(a)(1) general rule:** evidence of a person's character is not admissible to prove action in conformity on a particular occasion [READ].
**404(a)(2) exceptions only in a CRIMINAL case:**
 - (A) Defendant offers a pertinent trait of the defendant (e.g., honesty on a theft charge, peacefulness on assault); if admitted, the prosecutor may rebut (the "mercy rule": the defendant opens the door; the prosecutor cannot start it).
 - (B) Defendant offers a pertinent trait of the alleged **victim** (subject to Rule 412 for sexual cases); if admitted, the prosecutor may rebut AND offer evidence of the defendant's **same trait**.
 - (C) In a **homicide** case, the prosecutor may offer the victim's trait of peacefulness to rebut evidence that the victim was the first aggressor.
 - (a)(3) witnesses: character for truthfulness through 607, 608, 609.
**405 methods:** (a) when character evidence is admissible, prove it by **reputation or opinion** testimony; on cross of a character witness the court may allow inquiry into relevant specific instances ("Have you heard...?" for reputation witnesses, "Do you know...?" for opinion witnesses; the cross-examiner needs a good-faith basis; Michelson v. United States, 335 U.S. 469 (1948) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/335/469]). (b) **Specific instances** may be used only when character is an **essential element** of a charge, claim, or defense (defamation truth defense, negligent entrustment/hiring, child custody, entrapment predisposition [MEMORY]) [READ rule].
**404(b) other crimes, wrongs, acts:** not admissible to prove character to show conformity; **may be admissible for another purpose, such as motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, or lack of accident** (list is illustrative; mnemonics MIMIC / KIPPOMIA are fine but are not in the rule) [READ]. In a criminal case the prosecutor must give reasonable written pretrial notice stating the purpose and reasoning (court may excuse for good cause) [READ, 2020]. Standard for the uncharged act: sufficient evidence for a jury to find the defendant committed it (104(b)), not proof by preponderance and not a conviction (Huddleston [READ]). 403 and a limiting instruction (105) still apply. Applies to civil cases too. Trap: "common scheme" must show something beyond propensity; an act is **not** "intrinsic" other-acts evidence if it is part of the charged episode itself [MEMORY].
**406 habit/routine practice:** a person's habit or an organization's routine practice may be admitted to prove conformity; no corroboration or eyewitness required [READ]. Habit = a specific, regular, semi-automatic response to a particular situation ("every time he leaves the lot he signals and waves to the guard"); character = a general disposition ("careful driver"). Routine practice of an organization (mailroom procedure) is the classic MBE fact pattern.
**Sexual-offense rules:**
 - **412 (rape shield, applies in civil AND criminal cases involving alleged sexual misconduct):** inadmissible to prove the victim engaged in other sexual behavior or to prove sexual predisposition. Criminal exceptions: (A) specific instances to show someone else was the source of semen, injury, or physical evidence; (B) specific instances with the accused, if offered by the defendant to prove consent, or by the prosecutor; (C) evidence whose exclusion would violate the defendant's constitutional rights. Civil: admissible if probative value *substantially outweighs* harm to any victim and unfair prejudice to any party; reputation only if the victim placed it in controversy. Procedure: written motion at least **14 days** before trial, notice to the victim, in camera hearing, sealed [READ].
 - **413 (criminal sexual assault), 414 (child molestation, child = under 14), 415 (civil claim based on sexual assault/child molestation):** the court **may** admit evidence that the defendant/party committed any other sexual assault/child molestation, on any matter to which it is relevant (including propensity); disclosure at least **15 days** before trial; does not limit other rules (403 still applies in practice) [READ].

### 3.3 Policy exclusions that belong here on the MBE outline (III.E-III.G, treated in 4)
See section 4.

### 3.4 Experts (FRE 701-706; outline II.D)
- **702** (2023): qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education; proponent shows **more likely than not** (a) specialized knowledge helps the trier of fact, (b) sufficient facts or data, (c) reliable principles and methods, (d) reliable application to the facts [READ].
- **Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579 (1993):** FRE 702 superseded the Frye "general acceptance" test; the judge is a gatekeeper (104(a)) who ensures expert testimony is relevant ("fit") and reliable; non-exclusive factors: testability/falsifiability, peer review and publication, known or potential error rate, existence and maintenance of standards, general acceptance (still a factor) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/92-102.ZO.html]. **General Electric Co. v. Joiner, 522 U.S. 136 (1997):** abuse-of-discretion review of Daubert rulings; a court may exclude an opinion where there is "too great an analytical gap between the data and the opinion" [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/522/136]. **Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137 (1999):** gatekeeping applies to all expert testimony (technical and experience-based), factors flexible [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/526/137].
- **703 bases:** may rest on facts/data the expert was made aware of or personally observed; if experts in the field would reasonably rely on such facts/data, they need not be admissible; but **the proponent may disclose inadmissible underlying facts/data to the jury only if their probative value in helping the jury evaluate the opinion substantially outweighs their prejudicial effect** (a reverse 403 balancing) [READ]. Constitutional overlay in criminal cases: Smith v. Arizona (see 8).
- **704:** (a) an opinion on an ultimate issue is not objectionable per se; (b) **in a criminal case an expert must not state an opinion on whether the defendant did or did not have the mental state or condition that is an element of the crime or defense** (the expert may describe the diagnosis, the jury decides the mental state) [READ]. Legal conclusions ("the defendant was negligent / violated the contract") are still excluded by 702/403 as unhelpful [MEMORY].
- **705:** expert may give opinion and reasons without first disclosing underlying facts unless court orders; may be required on cross [READ]. **706:** court-appointed experts [READ].
- Qualification is a 104(a) question; one can be an expert by experience.

### 3.5 Authentication and identification (FRE 901-903; outline II.B)
- 901(a): proponent must produce evidence **sufficient to support a finding** the item is what it is claimed to be (104(b) standard; low bar) [READ]. 901(b) lists examples (not exclusive): (1) testimony of a witness with knowledge; (2) nonexpert opinion on handwriting based on familiarity not acquired for the litigation; (3) comparison with an authenticated specimen by an expert or the trier of fact; (4) distinctive characteristics and circumstances; (5) voice identification by anyone who has heard the voice under circumstances connecting it to the speaker; (6) telephone conversations: call to the number assigned at the time to a person (self-identification can help) or to a business for business reasonably transacted by phone; (7) public records; (8) ancient documents (condition creates no suspicion, in a place where it would likely be, at least 20 years old); (9) process or system producing an accurate result; (10) other methods authorized by statute or rule [READ].
- **902 self-authenticating (no extrinsic evidence of authenticity needed):** sealed and signed domestic public documents; unsealed but signed and certified; foreign public documents (final certification); certified copies of public records; official publications; newspapers and periodicals; trade inscriptions; acknowledged documents (notary); commercial paper; presumptions under federal statute; **902(11) certified domestic business records** (must meet 803(6)(A)-(C) by certification, with reasonable written notice and opportunity to inspect); 902(12) certified foreign business records (civil); **902(13)** certified records generated by an electronic process or system; **902(14)** certified data copied from an electronic device, storage medium, or file [READ].
- **903:** a subscribing witness is needed only if required by the law of the jurisdiction governing validity [READ].
- **Trap that decides items: authentication is not admissibility.** A self-authenticating document may still be hearsay, privileged, or irrelevant. Conversely, once authenticated, a business record still needs hearsay foundation (803(6)).
- Chain of custody for fungible items (blood, drugs): sufficient evidence the item is in substantially the same condition; gaps go to weight [MEMORY].
- Real/demonstrative/experimental evidence (outline II.E): real evidence needs authentication; reenactments and experiments require "substantial similarity" of conditions; **illustrative aids are governed by Rule 107 (2024)** [READ 107].
- Social media/text/email: authentication by distinctive characteristics and context (901(b)(4)), by the recipient's testimony, by a reply that only the purported sender would know [MEMORY on case law; rule text READ].

### 3.6 Traps (relevancy)
- "Character" of a *civil* party is never admissible to prove conduct (404(a)(1)); only essential-element cases (405(b)) allow specific instances.
- Mercy rule is one-way: the defendant opens the door.
- Reputation witness vs opinion witness cross questions ("have you heard" vs "do you know").
- 404(b) cannot be used to show propensity, but nearly anything can be dressed as intent/identity: look for the *chain of inference* that does not pass through "he is the kind of person who."
- 405(a) bars specific instances on direct to prove character, even for the victim's violent character in self-defense. A *defendant's knowledge* of the victim's violent acts is admissible for the defendant's state of mind (reasonable fear), not as character evidence.
- Habit needs regularity and specificity; "he often drinks" is character, not habit.
- 412-415 are *exceptions in opposite directions*: 412 shields the victim; 413-415 let in the accused's other sexual offenses.

## 4. Privileges and other policy exclusions (about 3-4 items with Writings)

### 4.1 Privilege framework (FRE 501-502)
- **501:** federal common law (reason and experience) governs privilege **unless Constitution, federal statute, or Supreme Court rule provides otherwise; but in a civil case, state law governs privilege on a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision** [READ]. So: federal question/criminal -> federal common law; diversity (state-law claim) -> state privilege law. The MBE says answer under the FRE, so tested items usually involve federal common law privileges.
- **502:** (a) intentional waiver in a federal proceeding extends to undisclosed material only if same subject matter and ought in fairness be considered together; (b) **inadvertent disclosure in a federal proceeding is not a waiver if the holder took reasonable steps to prevent disclosure and promptly took reasonable steps to rectify**; (c) disclosure in a state proceeding; (d) a federal court order that disclosure is not a waiver binds all other proceedings; (e) party agreement binds only the parties unless incorporated in an order [READ].
- **Attorney-client privilege (elements, [MEMORY] general federal common law):** (1) communication (2) between client (or prospective client) and lawyer or the lawyer's agents (3) made in confidence (4) for the purpose of obtaining or giving legal advice. Held by the client; client or lawyer (for the client) may assert. Lost if disclosed to third parties (unless the third party is necessary to the communication: interpreter, secretary, accountant assisting). Waiver by voluntary disclosure; implied waiver (client sues lawyer; at-issue). Exceptions: **crime-fraud** (client sought advice to further a future or ongoing crime or fraud), **joint clients** (in a later dispute between them), **breach of duty** (fee disputes, malpractice), attesting-witness. **Survives the client's death** (Swidler & Berlin v. United States, 524 U.S. 399 (1998) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/524/399]), with the testamentary exception (disputes among those claiming through the deceased client). **Corporate clients:** Upjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981): communications by employees at any level to corporate counsel, at the direction of superiors, about matters within their duties, to secure legal advice, are privileged; the privilege protects communications, not underlying facts [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/449/383]. The privilege covers communications, not the facts, not physical evidence, not the fact of the representation, identity/fees in most cases.
- **Work product** (FRCP 26(b)(3), Hickman v. Taylor [MEMORY]): documents and tangible things prepared in anticipation of litigation; **opinion work product** (mental impressions) nearly absolute; fact work product yields to substantial need and undue hardship. Upjohn: notes of oral witness interviews get strong protection [READ].
- **Spousal immunity (federal) = two privileges:**
  1. **Spousal testimonial privilege (adverse spousal testimony):** held by the **witness-spouse alone**; in federal court the witness may neither be compelled to testify nor barred from testifying; applies only in criminal proceedings (as the MBE treats it); requires a valid marriage at the time of testimony (ends on divorce); covers all testimony adverse to the spouse, not just communications (Trammel v. United States, 445 U.S. 40 (1980) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/445/40]). Testimonial privilege does not stop the government from using other information; it protects only the witness-spouse from being forced to testify.
  2. **Confidential marital communications privilege:** protects confidential communications made between spouses during a valid marriage; applies in **civil and criminal** cases; survives divorce/death for communications made during the marriage; generally held by **both spouses** and either may block disclosure [MEMORY: majority federal view; Trammel [READ] says only that this privilege was untouched]. Exceptions (federal common law, [MEMORY]): communications in furtherance of a joint crime, suits between spouses, proceedings concerning child abuse (varies). Not confidential if made in front of third parties; acts and observations are not "communications."
- **Psychotherapist-patient:** recognized under 501 in Jaffee v. Redmond, 518 U.S. 1 (1996): confidential communications between a licensed psychotherapist (including licensed clinical social workers) and a patient in the course of diagnosis or treatment are privileged; the Court rejected a case-by-case balancing test; dangerousness exception left open in a footnote [READ summary at https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/518/1]. **There is no general federal physician-patient privilege** under federal common law; the MBE outline heading says "physician/psychotherapist-patient," so teach: psychotherapist yes (federal), physician generally no unless state law governs a state-law claim in federal court [MEMORY on "no federal physician-patient privilege"; consistent with 501 text].
- **Other privileges (outline III.D):** clergy-penitent (recognized at common law), Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination (constitutional, belongs to the witness, not an evidence-rule privilege; a defendant has a right not to testify; the privilege is personal), journalist/accountant (generally not recognized federally) [MEMORY].
- **Waiver and "who decides":** the judge decides privilege questions under 104(a) and is bound by privilege rules in deciding [READ 104(a)].

### 4.2 Policy exclusions (FRE 407-411)
- **407 subsequent remedial measures:** when measures are taken that would have made an earlier injury or harm less likely to occur, evidence of the measures is not admissible to prove **negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product or its design, or a need for a warning or instruction**; admissible for another purpose such as impeachment or, if disputed, **ownership, control, or feasibility of precautionary measures** [READ]. Applies to measures taken *after* the injury; covers strict liability and design defect (1997); pre-accident changes are not "subsequent." Whether it applies to measures by non-parties is a circuit-split point [MEMORY: do not test].
- **408 compromise offers and negotiations:** inadmissible, on behalf of any party, either to prove or disprove the **validity or amount of a disputed claim** or to impeach by prior inconsistent statement or contradiction: (1) furnishing/offering/accepting valuable consideration in compromising or attempting to compromise the claim; (2) **conduct or statements made during compromise negotiations** (exception: in a criminal case, statements in negotiations with a public office exercising regulatory/investigative/enforcement authority). May be admitted for another purpose such as witness **bias or prejudice**, negating a contention of undue delay, or proving an effort to obstruct a criminal investigation [READ]. Requires a *disputed* claim (a bare offer to pay an undisputed debt is not covered) [MEMORY].
- **409 payment of medical expenses:** furnishing, promising, or offering to pay medical, hospital, or similar expenses is inadmissible to prove liability. **Unlike 408, 409 does not protect accompanying statements** ("I'm so sorry, I ran the light, I'll pay your bills": the offer is out; "I ran the light" can come in as an opposing-party statement) [READ rule + advisory note: "the present rule does not extend to conduct or statements not a part of the act of furnishing or offering or promising to pay"].
- **410 pleas and plea discussions (civil or criminal):** not admissible *against the defendant who made the plea or participated in the discussions*: (1) a withdrawn guilty plea; (2) a nolo contendere plea; (3) a statement made during a Rule 11 plea proceeding on those pleas; (4) a statement made during plea discussions **with an attorney for the prosecuting authority** if the discussions did not result in a guilty plea or resulted in a later-withdrawn plea. Exceptions: another statement from the same plea/discussions has been introduced and fairness requires it; criminal perjury/false statement prosecution if made under oath, on the record, with counsel present [READ]. Trap: statements to a police officer with no authority to bargain are not covered (the rule says "attorney for the prosecuting authority") [rule text READ; case law MEMORY].
- **411 liability insurance:** evidence that a person was or was not insured is inadmissible to prove negligence or wrongful conduct; admissible for another purpose such as bias, **agency, ownership, or control** [READ].

### 4.3 Traps (privileges and policy exclusions)
- Spousal testimonial privilege belongs to the **witness**, not the accused. Husband cannot block a willing wife from testifying (but can block a confidential communication).
- "In the presence of a third party" = not confidential. Co-clients or interpreters excepted.
- Crime-fraud: the client's *intent* to use the lawyer's help to commit a crime or fraud, not merely past crime.
- Privilege covers communications, never underlying facts.
- 407 does not apply to pre-injury conditions; 407 does not bar use to show feasibility *if disputed*.
- 408 covers statements in negotiations; 409 does not cover statements; 410 only covers statements to prosecutors (and pleas); 411 only for negligence-type "wrongful conduct" proof.
- 408/409/410/411 are *relevance policy exclusions*, not privileges; each has "another purpose" exceptions.

## 5. Writings, recordings, and photographs (FRE 1001-1008; about 1-2 items)

- **1001 definitions:** writing, recording, photograph; **"original"** = the item itself or a counterpart intended by the executor to have the same effect; for ESI any printout or output readable by sight if it accurately reflects the information; original of a photograph includes the negative or a print; **"duplicate"** = counterpart produced by a mechanical, photographic, chemical, electronic or equivalent process that accurately reproduces the original [READ].
- **1002 best evidence rule:** to **prove the content** of a writing, recording, or photograph, the original is required unless the rules or a federal statute provide otherwise [READ]. The rule is about proving *content*; a witness may testify about a fact she knows independently even if a document also records it (she can testify "I paid rent"; she cannot describe what the lease says without the original or an excuse). A photograph used merely to illustrate testimony is not offered to prove content (Rule 107 aid).
- **1003 duplicates** are admissible to the same extent as originals unless a genuine question is raised about the original's authenticity or it would be unfair [READ].
- **1004 other evidence of content (excuses):** (a) originals lost or destroyed, not by the proponent in bad faith; (b) original cannot be obtained by judicial process; (c) opponent had control, was on notice it would be a subject of proof, and fails to produce; (d) writing not closely related to a controlling issue (collateral) [READ].
- **1005 public records:** certified copy or a copy testified correct by a witness who compared it; otherwise other evidence if no copy obtainable with diligence [READ].
- **1006 summaries** (as amended 2024): admissible as evidence if offered to prove content of voluminous admissible writings/recordings/photographs that cannot be conveniently examined in court, *whether or not they have been introduced*; the proponent must make the originals/duplicates available for examination/copying; illustrative-only summaries go to 107 [READ].
- **1007:** content may be proved by the testimony, deposition, or written statement of the party against whom offered, without accounting for the original [READ]. **1008:** judge decides the 1004/1005 foundation, but the jury decides (under 104(b)) whether the writing ever existed, whether another produced is the original, and whether other evidence of content accurately reflects the content [READ].
- **Completeness (FRE 106):** see 1.5; also applies to writings and oral statements; admissible over a hearsay objection.
- **Traps:** the best evidence rule does not exclude testimony about a person's own perception of an event just because it was videotaped; rule triggers when a witness testifies to what the document says. Secondary evidence (oral testimony about content) is admissible when the original is lost without bad faith. The proponent's own bad-faith destruction removes the excuse. Authentication still required for copies.

## 6. Hearsay and circumstances of its admissibility (about 6 items; official weight 1/4)

### 6.1 Definition (FRE 801-802)
- **Statement** = a person's oral assertion, written assertion, or nonverbal conduct *if the person intended it as an assertion* (801(a)). **Declarant** = the person who made it. **Hearsay** = a statement that (1) the declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or hearing, and (2) is offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (801(c)) [READ]. 802: hearsay is inadmissible unless a federal statute, the FRE, or Supreme Court rules say otherwise.
- **Not hearsay because not a "statement" or not for truth** (non-hearsay uses) [rule text READ; uses MEMORY]: effect on the listener/reader (notice, motive, state of mind of the hearer, "reasonable belief"); **verbal acts / legally operative words** (offer, acceptance, defamation, threat, contract terms, "I'll give you $50"); declarant's state of mind shown *circumstantially* (statements showing insanity, bias); to impeach by inconsistent statement; to show knowledge or to lay a foundation. Conduct not intended as an assertion (a person who flees; a person who opens an umbrella) and statements by machines/animals are not hearsay because there is no "person" assertion [rule text for conduct READ; machine point MEMORY]. Silence is a statement only if intended as assertion.
- Prior testimony at an earlier proceeding is hearsay (not made "at the current trial") unless an exclusion/exception applies [READ definition].

### 6.2 Statements that are not hearsay by definition (801(d)) - the exclusions
**(d)(1) Declarant-witness's prior statement.** The declarant testifies at the current trial **and is subject to cross-examination about the prior statement**, and the statement:
 - **(A) inconsistent** with the testimony and **given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition** (grand jury testimony counts; a police-station statement, an affidavit prepared out of court, or a sworn but non-"proceeding" statement does not). Admissible for truth.
 - **(B)(i) consistent** with testimony, offered to rebut an express or implied charge of **recent fabrication or improper influence or motive**; must have been made **before** the motive arose (Tome [READ]); **(B)(ii)** consistent statement offered to rehabilitate credibility when attacked on **another ground** (2014).
 - **(C) identification** of a person as someone the declarant perceived earlier (lineup, photo array, show-up). The witness need not be able to repeat the identification at trial; "subject to cross-examination" is satisfied even where memory loss prevents explaining the basis (United States v. Owens, 484 U.S. 554 (1988) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/484/554]).
**(d)(2) Opposing party's statement** (formerly "admission of a party-opponent"): offered **against** an opposing party and (A) made by the party in an individual or representative capacity; (B) one the party **manifested that it adopted or believed true** (adoptive admission: express adoption, or silence/conduct when a person would naturally deny the statement; post-Miranda silence cannot be used [Doyle v. Ohio: MEMORY]); (C) made by a person **authorized** to speak on the subject; (D) made by the party's **agent or employee on a matter within the scope of that relationship and while it existed** (no need for authority to speak, no need for personal knowledge, no need that it be against interest); (E) made by the party's **coconspirator during and in furtherance of the conspiracy** [READ]. The statement must be considered but does not by itself establish authority under (C), the existence/scope of the relationship under (D), or the existence/participation in the conspiracy under (E) (the court may use the statement as part of the 104(a) showing; Bourjaily [READ]; need independent corroborating evidence beyond the statement alone). The 2024 sentence lets a successor party (estate, assignee) be bound by the predecessor's statements [READ].
- **Not subject to** Confrontation Clause concerns generally (non-testimonial unless made to authorities) and **not subject to the personal-knowledge requirement or the against-interest requirement**.

### 6.3 Exceptions applicable regardless of availability (FRE 803) - elements and traps
All text [READ]; traps are my teaching notes [MEMORY unless marked].
1. **(1) Present sense impression:** statement describing or explaining an event or condition, made **while or immediately after** the declarant perceived it. No startling event needed. Trap: not narrations of memory ("this morning I saw...").
2. **(2) Excited utterance:** statement relating to a **startling** event or condition made while the declarant was under the **stress of excitement** it caused. Time can lapse as long as stress persists. Trap: the statement need only *relate to* the event, not describe it; no personal-perception requirement for PSI vs excited: both usually require perception.
3. **(3) Then-existing mental, emotional, or physical condition:** statement of **then-existing** state of mind (motive, intent, plan), emotion, sensation, or physical condition (pain, bodily health), but **not a statement of memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed**, unless it relates to the validity or terms of the declarant's will. Statement of intent to do a future act can prove the declarant later did it (Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Hillmon, 145 U.S. 285 (1892) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/145/285]: letters showing his intent to travel with another person, admitted as evidence of his intention). Traps: "I fear Dan" (state of mind) vs "Dan threatened me" (statement of memory to prove Dan did it: excluded); "I felt dizzy last week" is not then-existing.
4. **(4) Medical diagnosis or treatment:** statement made for, and reasonably pertinent to, diagnosis or treatment, describing medical history, past or present symptoms/sensations, inception, or **general cause**. Covers statements to doctors, nurses, EMTs and by parents on a child's behalf; also covers statements to a physician consulted only to testify (the advisory note says the rule dispenses with the old limitation). "Statements as to fault would not ordinarily qualify" [READ note].
5. **(5) Recorded recollection:** record on a matter the witness once knew about but **now cannot recall well enough to testify fully and accurately**; made or adopted when fresh; accurately reflects knowledge. **Read into evidence; received as an exhibit only if offered by an adverse party.** Foundation requires a showing of insufficient memory (contrast refreshing, 612).
6. **(6) Records of a regularly conducted activity (business records):** record of an act, event, condition, opinion, or diagnosis if (A) made **at or near the time** by, or from information transmitted by, someone **with knowledge**; (B) **kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity** of a business, organization, occupation, or calling, whether or not for profit; (C) **making the record was a regular practice** of that activity; (D) conditions shown by the **custodian or another qualified witness**, or by a **902(11)/(12) certification**; (E) the opponent does not show that the source of information or method or circumstances indicate **lack of trustworthiness**. Traps: (i) information supplied by an outsider with no business duty is **hearsay within hearsay** and needs its own exception; (ii) records prepared in anticipation of litigation are usually untrustworthy (Palmer v. Hoffman, quoted in Melendez-Diaz [READ]); (iii) a custodian need not have personal knowledge of the entry; (iv) the foundation is by the custodian *or* certification, so a personal-knowledge sponsor is not required.
7. **(7) Absence of a business record**, to prove the matter did not occur, if records of that kind were regularly kept.
8. **(8) Public records:** record or statement of a public office that sets out (i) the office's activities, (ii) a matter observed under a legal duty to report, but **not, in a criminal case, a matter observed by law-enforcement personnel**, or (iii) **in a civil case or against the government in a criminal case, factual findings from a legally authorized investigation**; opponent can show lack of trustworthiness. "Factual findings" include conclusions and opinions: Beech Aircraft Corp. v. Rainey, 488 U.S. 153 (1988) [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/488/153]. Trap: a police report's recital of an officer's own observations cannot be used by the prosecution against a criminal defendant; statements by bystanders inside a public record are hearsay within hearsay.
9. **(9)** vital statistics (birth, death, marriage, reported under legal duty); **(10)** absence of public record (criminal case notice: 14 days; defendant objects within 7); **(11)-(13)** religious records, marriage/baptism certificates, family records; **(14)** records of property documents; **(15)** statements in property documents; **(16)** ancient documents (prepared before Jan 1, 1998, authenticity established); **(17)** market reports and directories; **(19)-(21)** reputation concerning personal/family history, boundaries/general history, **character** (reputation among associates or in the community: this is the hearsay exception that lets 405(a) reputation testimony in); **(22)** judgment of a previous conviction: after trial or guilty plea (**not nolo**), crime punishable by death or imprisonment over a year, to prove any fact essential to the judgment; when offered by the prosecutor in a criminal case for a purpose other than impeachment, only judgments against the defendant; **(23)** judgments on personal/family/general history or boundaries [READ for all].
10. **(18) Learned treatises:** statement in a treatise, periodical, or pamphlet **called to the attention of an expert on cross-examination or relied on by the expert on direct**, and the publication is established as a reliable authority (by the expert's admission or testimony, another expert, or judicial notice). **May be read into evidence but not received as an exhibit** [READ].
11. **(24)** transferred to 807.

### 6.4 Exceptions that require the declarant to be unavailable (FRE 804)
- **804(a) unavailability:** (1) privilege exempts the declarant (court rules the privilege applies); (2) refuses to testify despite a court order; (3) testifies to **not remembering** the subject matter; (4) death or then-existing infirmity, physical or mental illness; (5) absent, and the proponent could not procure attendance (for (b)(1) and (b)(6)) or attendance or testimony (for (b)(2), (3), (4)) by process or other reasonable means. Not unavailable if the **proponent procured or wrongfully caused** the unavailability to prevent testimony [READ].
- **(b)(1) Former testimony:** testimony given as a witness at a trial, hearing, or **lawful deposition**, in the current or another proceeding, offered against a party who had (or, **in a civil case, whose predecessor in interest had**) an **opportunity and similar motive** to develop it by direct, cross, or redirect [READ]. Grand jury testimony offered against the accused: the accused had no opportunity, so no.
- **(b)(2) Statement under belief of imminent death (dying declaration):** only in a **homicide prosecution or a civil case**; declarant believed death imminent; about the **cause or circumstances** of what he believed to be his impending death. The declarant need not actually die (but unavailability is required) [READ text; "need not die" MEMORY].
- **(b)(3) Statement against interest:** (A) a reasonable person in the declarant's position would have made it only if believing it true because, when made, it was so contrary to the declarant's **proprietary or pecuniary interest** or had so great a tendency to invalidate the declarant's claim against someone else or to **expose the declarant to civil or criminal liability**; (B) if offered in a **criminal case** as one tending to expose the declarant to criminal liability, supported by **corroborating circumstances that clearly indicate trustworthiness** (applies to both prosecution and defense) [READ]. Williamson v. United States, 512 U.S. 594 (1994): only the individual remarks that are genuinely self-inculpatory qualify; non-self-inculpatory statements within a confession (especially blame-shifting) are not covered [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/512/594]. Distinguish from an opposing-party statement: against-interest applies to **non-party declarants** and needs unavailability; the opposing party's own statement needs neither.
- **(b)(4) Personal or family history** (own birth, marriage, etc., even without personal knowledge; another person's if related or intimately associated) [READ].
- **(b)(6) Forfeiture by wrongdoing:** statement offered against a party who wrongfully caused (or acquiesced in wrongfully causing) the declarant's unavailability **and did so intending that result** [READ]. Giles v. California, 554 U.S. 353 (2008): the Confrontation Clause forfeiture doctrine requires the defendant to have acted with the **purpose** of making the witness unavailable; knowledge alone is not enough [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/07-6053.ZO.html]; the Court noted that in domestic-violence cases a pattern of abuse intended to keep the victim from testifying may satisfy it.
- (b)(5) transferred to 807.

### 6.5 Other hearsay provisions
- **805 hearsay within hearsay:** admissible if **each part** conforms with an exception or exclusion [READ].
- **806 attacking the declarant:** see 2.2 item 8.
- **807 residual exception (2019 text):** statement not admissible under 803 or 804 if (1) supported by **sufficient guarantees of trustworthiness** considering the totality of circumstances and corroborating evidence, and (2) **more probative on the point than any other evidence the proponent can obtain through reasonable efforts**; the proponent gives reasonable written pretrial notice including the substance and the declarant's name (court may excuse for good cause) [READ]. MBE use: rarely the right answer; use only when no categorical exception fits.
- **Hearsay and the Confrontation Clause are two separate hurdles:** a statement offered by the prosecution against a criminal defendant must satisfy BOTH (see 7).

### 6.6 Traps (hearsay)
1. **Prior inconsistent statements** are hearsay for truth unless 801(d)(1)(A) (sworn, at a proceeding/deposition). Otherwise impeachment only. Prior consistent statements need a pre-motive timing.
2. **Opposing-party statement vs declaration against interest:** the former has no unavailability requirement, no against-interest requirement, no personal-knowledge requirement and applies only to statements of the *party* (or its agents/coconspirator etc.). The latter applies to *third parties* and requires unavailability and a sufficient against-interest feel (and corroboration if criminal and exposing the declarant).
3. **Co-conspirator statements:** must be during the conspiracy and in furtherance (not narrative statements after arrest, not idle chatter); the declarant need not be charged; statement alone cannot prove the conspiracy.
4. **Agent's statement:** within the scope of the relationship at the time; a statement merely about matters the employee observed outside the job, or made after termination, fails.
5. **Business records foundation:** custodian/qualified witness *or* a certification; entries made at/near the time by someone with knowledge; regular practice; **outsider-supplied information needs its own exception**; litigation-driven records are untrustworthy.
6. **State of mind (803(3)):** forward-looking intent in; backward-looking memory out (except wills).
7. **PSI vs excited utterance:** PSI needs near contemporaneity but no startle; EU needs stress but not contemporaneity.
8. **Medical statements:** fault and identity generally not pertinent; the statement must be for diagnosis/treatment (ER, EMT, therapist).
9. **Recorded recollection:** only if the witness cannot recall; read, don't exhibit (unless offered by the adverse party).
10. **Public records (803(8)):** police observations are excluded when offered against a criminal defendant; investigative findings usable against the government or in civil cases; embedded hearsay still needs an exception.
11. **Unavailability:** lack of memory counts; a proponent who procured the absence cannot use it; privilege must be upheld by the court.
12. **Dying declarations:** only homicide or civil; subject matter limited to cause/circumstances; declarant believed death imminent.
13. **Former testimony:** the *party against whom offered* (or predecessor in a civil case) must have had a similar motive; grand jury testimony is not enough against the accused.
14. **Double hearsay:** analyze each layer.
15. Non-hearsay uses require stating the purpose; the "other purpose" must be actually relevant and the court gives a limiting instruction (105).

## 7. Right to confront witnesses (outline V.L; the "Crawford" cluster)

### 7.1 Framework
- Sixth Amendment: "In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right... to be confronted with the witnesses against him." Applies to **state and federal** criminal trials; **not** to civil cases; chiefly limits **prosecution** hearsay [per NCBE draft scope READ].
- **Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36 (2004)** [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/02-9410.ZO.html]: testimonial statements of a witness who does not appear at trial are inadmissible against the accused unless the declarant is **unavailable** and the defendant had a **prior opportunity to cross-examine**. Rejected Ohio v. Roberts' reliability test (firmly rooted exceptions / particularized guarantees of trustworthiness) for testimonial statements. "Testimonial" core class: ex parte in-court testimony or its equivalent; preliminary hearing, grand jury and former trial testimony; police interrogations; structured police questioning even if unsworn. Footnote 9: when the declarant appears for cross-examination at trial the Clause places no constraints on prior statements. Dying declarations: left open. Forfeiture by wrongdoing is an equitable exception.
- **Davis v. Washington / Hammon v. Indiana, 547 U.S. 813 (2006) (primary purpose test)** [READ summary via https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/05-5224.ZO.html]: statements made to police during interrogation are **nontestimonial** when the circumstances objectively indicate the **primary purpose is to enable police assistance to meet an ongoing emergency**; **testimonial** when there is no ongoing emergency and the primary purpose is to establish or prove past events potentially relevant to later prosecution. Davis (911 call as the assault unfolded): nontestimonial; later parts of a call after the emergency ended may be testimonial. Hammon (statements and affidavit given to officers after the disturbance had ended): testimonial.
- **Michigan v. Bryant, 562 U.S. 344 (2011)** [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/09-150.ZO.html]: objective test; an ongoing emergency can extend beyond the victim to the public (shooter at large); weapon type and the victim's medical condition matter; formality/informality is relevant. Statements by a dying shooting victim to police identifying the shooter were nontestimonial.
- **Ohio v. Clark, 576 U.S. 237 (2015)** [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/13-1352]: a three-year-old's statements to preschool teachers identifying his abuser were nontestimonial; primary-purpose test remains "necessary, but not always sufficient"; statements to non-law-enforcement are much less likely to be testimonial; mandatory-reporting duties do not turn teachers into police; very young children's statements rarely implicate the Clause.
- **Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts, 557 U.S. 305 (2009)** [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/07-591.ZO.html]: sworn forensic lab certificates (drug analysis) created for use at trial are testimonial; the analyst must testify; business and public records created for administering an entity's affairs are generally non-testimonial; simple notice-and-demand statutes are constitutional.
- **Bullcoming v. New Mexico, 564 U.S. 647 (2011):** a surrogate analyst who did not perform or observe the test cannot introduce the absent analyst's testimonial lab report [MEMORY: 5-4, Ginsburg; fetch of the opinion failed, UNSURE on vote only]. **Williams v. Illinois, 567 U.S. 50 (2012):** fractured plurality (4+1+4); no single rationale commanded five votes; its reasoning is of limited precedential value [MEMORY; fetch failed]. **Smith v. Arizona, 602 U.S. 779 (2024)** [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-899]: when an expert conveys an absent analyst's out-of-court statements as the basis for the expert's opinion, and the statements support the opinion only if true, they are offered for their truth; if testimonial, the Clause bars them absent unavailability and a prior opportunity to cross-examine; "basis" labels do not avoid the Clause; the Court did not decide whether the particular statements were testimonial; (Kagan, J.; 9-0 to vacate and remand). The expert may still testify from personal knowledge of lab practices, give general testimony on methods, or answer hypotheticals.
- Reporter citations in this section (U.S. volume and page) were written from memory and not individually checked; case names, holdings and links were checked where marked [READ].
- **Giles v. California** (forfeiture requires intent to prevent testimony) [READ]; **Hemphill v. New York, 595 U.S. 140 (2022)** [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-637]: "opening the door" under a state evidence rule cannot override the Clause; judges may not substitute reliability findings for cross-examination (8-1, Sotomayor, J.). **Samia v. United States, 599 U.S. 635 (2023)** [READ https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-196]: a nontestifying codefendant's confession redacted so it does not directly name the defendant ("the other person") with a limiting instruction does not violate the Clause (Bruton v. United States, 391 U.S. 123 (1968) [MEMORY] bars the naming/obvious-blank version). The NCBE's older NextGen annotation says the exam will not test the codefendant application; the MBE outline does not say, so treat as low priority.
- Newer: Pitts v. Mississippi (2025, per curiam) reaffirmed face-to-face rule for child witnesses behind a screen [SECONDARY, UNSURE]; Franklin v. New York (cert. denied 2025) with statements calling for reconsideration of Crawford [SECONDARY, UNSURE]. I found no 2025-26 merits decision changing the framework; **re-check the Supreme Court docket just before launch.**

### 7.2 How to run a Confrontation analysis (teach as a 4-step checklist)
1. Is this a **criminal** case and is the statement offered **by the prosecution** (or otherwise against the accused)? If civil: Clause irrelevant.
2. Is the declarant **testifying** at trial and available for cross about the statement? If yes: Clause satisfied (Crawford n.9; Owens). 
3. Is the statement **testimonial** (primary purpose: create an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony; police interrogation with no ongoing emergency; formal affidavits, lab reports made for prosecution; prior testimony)? Opposing-party statements, co-conspirator statements, business/public records of ordinary affairs, statements to friends, 911 calls during an emergency are generally not.
4. If testimonial and the declarant is not testifying: admissible only if the declarant is **unavailable** and the accused had a **prior opportunity** to cross, or the accused **forfeited** by wrongdoing intended to prevent testimony. Then also satisfy the hearsay rules.

### 7.3 Traps (confrontation)
- A statement can satisfy a hearsay exception (e.g., excited utterance) and still violate the Clause if testimonial.
- A statement can satisfy the Clause (nontestimonial) and still be barred by hearsay.
- Declarant's availability at trial cures Clause problems for prior statements, but 801(d)(1) must still be satisfied.
- "Testimonial" is about primary purpose, not solemnity or whether the declarant knew a crime had occurred.
- Government forensic reports and surrogate-analyst testimony are the most testable current area (Melendez-Diaz, Bullcoming, Smith).

## 9. Original exam-style fact patterns (our own; MBE phrasing; best answer + why the others fail)

Conventions: Federal Rules of Evidence apply. Best answers are underlined by "BEST".

### Presentation of evidence

**EV-01 Refreshing recollection (612 vs 803(5))**
At trial, Wendell, a bakery clerk, testifies that he cannot remember the license plate of the van that struck the display window. Plaintiff's counsel hands him his own notebook, in which he wrote the plate number that day. Wendell reads it silently, hands it back, and testifies, "Now I remember: 7KLM204." Defense counsel asks to inspect the notebook and to read the whole page to the jury. The court should
A. refuse, because the notebook is hearsay.
B. allow defense counsel to inspect it, cross-examine Wendell about it, and introduce the portions that relate to his testimony. **BEST**
C. refuse, because a writing used to refresh must itself be admissible.
D. require the plaintiff to offer the notebook as a recorded recollection.
Why: 612(b). The refreshing writing need not be admissible; the testimony is the evidence. A is wrong (not offered for truth, and 612 governs). C reverses the rule. D is wrong because Wendell's memory was actually refreshed; recorded recollection (803(5)) applies only if memory remains insufficient, and even then the record would be read, not received as an exhibit by the proponent.

**EV-02 Prior inconsistent statement, unsworn (613, 801(d)(1)(A))**
In a civil trial over a collision, Priya, a bystander called by the plaintiff, testifies the light was red for the defendant. On cross, defense counsel asks, "Didn't you tell Officer Ames at the scene the light was green?" Priya answers, "I never said that." The defendant calls Officer Ames, who will testify Priya said, "It was green." The plaintiff objects. The court should rule the officer's testimony
A. inadmissible, because extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is never allowed.
B. inadmissible, because Priya was not shown the statement before being asked about it.
C. admissible only to impeach Priya's credibility. **BEST**
D. admissible as substantive evidence that the light was green.
Why: Priya had the chance to explain or deny, so 613(b) is satisfied; the statement concerns a central fact. It was an unsworn statement at the scene, not under penalty of perjury at a proceeding, so 801(d)(1)(A) fails and it is non-hearsay only because it is offered for impeachment. A confuses 608(b); B misstates 613(a) (no need to show it, only disclose to opposing counsel on request); D ignores the sworn-proceeding requirement.

**EV-03 Conviction of a testifying defendant (609(a)(1)(B))**
Drake is tried for aggravated assault in federal court. He testifies in his own defense that he acted in self-defense. The prosecutor seeks to impeach him with Drake's four-year-old conviction for burglary, a felony punishable by up to eight years. Burglary does not involve dishonesty or false statement. The evidence is
A. admissible, because it is a felony conviction less than ten years old.
B. admissible only if the court finds that its probative value outweighs its prejudicial effect to Drake. **BEST**
C. inadmissible, because burglary is not a crime of dishonesty.
D. inadmissible, because the prosecution may not impeach the defendant with convictions.
Why: 609(a)(1)(B). A states the rule for non-defendant witnesses (subject to 403 only). C ignores (a)(1): non-dishonesty felonies can come in. D is wrong: defendants who testify may be impeached.

**EV-04 Crimen falsi misdemeanor (609(a)(2))**
In a civil trial, the defendant's key witness, Nora, two years ago was convicted of a misdemeanor (maximum sentence six months) for knowingly giving a false name to a police officer. The plaintiff offers the conviction to attack her credibility. The court should
A. exclude it, because the offense was a misdemeanor.
B. admit it only if its probative value substantially outweighs its prejudicial effect.
C. admit it, because proving the offense required proving a false statement and the court has no discretion to exclude it. **BEST**
D. exclude it, because a conviction cannot be used when the witness is not the accused.
Why: 609(a)(2) is mandatory for crimes whose elements required proving or admitting a dishonest act or false statement, "regardless of the punishment." B states the 10-year rule in (b). A applies (a)(1)'s felony threshold to a crimen falsi crime. D is invented.

**EV-05 Specific instances on cross (608(b))**
Plaintiff's witness Rosa testifies that the defendant's truck ran the stop sign. On cross, defense counsel asks, "Last year didn't you submit an expense report that overstated your mileage?" Rosa says, "No." The defense then calls the accounting clerk, who will say the report was overstated. The court should
A. allow both the question and the clerk's testimony.
B. allow the question, but exclude the clerk's testimony. **BEST**
C. exclude both, because the conduct did not lead to a conviction.
D. exclude the question, but allow the clerk as a contradiction witness.
Why: 608(b): inquiry into conduct probative of untruthfulness is allowed on cross, but extrinsic evidence is not. A and D let the clerk in through the contradiction door, which cannot bypass 608(b). C wrongly requires a conviction.

**EV-06 Bias; extrinsic evidence allowed (Abel)**
In a contract case, the defendant's witness Gil testifies that no promise was made. Plaintiff asks, "Isn't it true the defendant's company is paying you $4,000 for your time?" Gil denies it. Plaintiff offers a check stub showing the payment. The stub is
A. inadmissible, because Gil's denial ends the inquiry on a collateral matter.
B. inadmissible, because it is extrinsic evidence of a specific instance of conduct.
C. admissible to show bias or interest. **BEST**
D. admissible only if Gil is first convicted of perjury.
Why: bias is never collateral; extrinsic evidence is allowed (Abel). A and B incorrectly apply the collateral-matter/608(b) limits.

**EV-07 Leading questions (611(c))**
In the plaintiff's case in chief, plaintiff's counsel calls the defendant manufacturer's regional sales manager and asks leading questions. The defense objects that leading is improper on direct. The court should
A. sustain, because the witness is the plaintiff's witness on direct.
B. overrule, because the witness is identified with an adverse party. **BEST**
C. sustain, because leading is allowed only on cross.
D. overrule, because the court cannot restrict leading questions.
Why: 611(c)(2). C overstates the rule ("ordinarily"); D is wrong because courts control the mode of interrogation under 611(a).

**EV-08 Judicial notice (201)**
In a civil jury trial, the court takes judicial notice, from a published calendar, that March 12 of the accident year fell on a Sunday. The defendant disputes nothing. The court should instruct the jury that
A. it may accept or reject the fact.
B. it must accept the fact as conclusive. **BEST**
C. the fact is evidence only if the plaintiff proves it.
D. it must disregard the fact unless a witness confirms it.
Why: 201(f): civil -> conclusive; criminal -> may or may not accept. The calendar is a source whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned.

**EV-09 Lay vs expert opinion (701(c))**
In a negligence trial, Ellis, a mechanic who happened to be standing on the corner, saw the collision. Ellis is not offered as an expert. Which testimony is most likely to be excluded?
A. "The silver car was going about sixty in a thirty zone."
B. "The driver of the van seemed drunk; he was swaying and slurring his words."
C. "The van's left brake line failed because of metal fatigue from a manufacturing flaw." **BEST (excluded)**
D. "The car's horn was blaring just before the crash."
Why: C requires specialized knowledge (701(c)); the others are rationally based on perception.

**EV-10 Expert bases (703)**
A cardiologist testifies as an expert and bases her opinion partly on a nurse's chart entries and a colleague's report. Cardiologists reasonably rely on such materials in forming opinions. The materials are not otherwise admissible. On direct, the plaintiff's counsel asks her to read the colleague's report aloud to the jury. The court should
A. exclude the opinion, because its basis is inadmissible.
B. allow the opinion but permit disclosure of the report to the jury only if its probative value in helping the jury evaluate the opinion substantially outweighs its prejudicial effect. **BEST**
C. allow disclosure, because the report is the basis of the opinion.
D. exclude the opinion unless the nurse and colleague also testify.
Why: 703. A and D ignore the "reasonably rely" clause. C ignores the reverse-403 balance.

**EV-11 Reliability gate (702, Daubert)**
Plaintiff's expert intends to testify using a new technique not yet widely used. The defendant objects. Which statement best describes the court's task?
A. Exclude, unless the technique is generally accepted in the field.
B. Admit, because the expert is qualified; the defendant may attack weight on cross.
C. Admit only if the court finds it more likely than not that the opinion rests on sufficient facts or data, reliable principles and methods, and a reliable application to the case; general acceptance is one relevant factor. **BEST**
D. Admit if the jury will find it helpful.
Why: 702 as amended 2023 and Daubert/Kumho. A is Frye. B is the "weight not admissibility" error 702(2023) targets. D leaves out reliability.

**EV-12 Ultimate issue (704(b))**
In a federal criminal trial for murder with an insanity defense, the prosecution's psychiatrist is asked, "Doctor, in your opinion did the defendant know right from wrong at the time?" The defense objects. The court should
A. overrule, because 704(a) permits ultimate-issue opinions.
B. sustain, because in a criminal case an expert may not state an opinion on whether the defendant had a mental state that is an element of the crime or defense. **BEST**
C. sustain, because experts may never testify on mental state.
D. overrule, because the defense put sanity in issue.
Why: 704(b). C is too broad (the expert may testify to diagnosis and its characteristics); A ignores the criminal exception.

**EV-13 Juror testimony (606(b))**
After a civil verdict, the losing party offers (1) an affidavit from a juror stating that jurors decided damages by averaging each person's guess, and (2) an affidavit from another juror stating that a juror brought a newspaper article about the defendant's prior lawsuit into the jury room and read it aloud. The court should consider
A. both affidavits.
B. neither affidavit.
C. only affidavit (2). **BEST**
D. only affidavit (1).
Why: 606(b)(1) bars testimony about deliberations and mental processes (the averaging method). 606(b)(2)(A) permits testimony that extraneous prejudicial information was brought to the jury's attention.

### Relevancy

**EV-14 Other acts for absence of accident (404(b))**
Hollis is charged with arson of his restaurant, to collect insurance. Hollis says the fire was electrical. The prosecutor offers evidence that three years earlier Hollis's other restaurant burned and he collected insurance. A fire marshal's report and an accelerant-trained dog both indicated deliberate burning of the earlier building, and a witness saw Hollis near it. The evidence is
A. inadmissible, because it shows only Hollis's propensity to commit arson.
B. admissible, if the jury could reasonably find Hollis set the earlier fire, to show absence of accident or mistake, subject to Rule 403. **BEST**
C. admissible only if Hollis was convicted of the earlier arson.
D. admissible only if Hollis testifies.
Why: 404(b)(2) and Huddleston: sufficient evidence for a finding; no conviction required. A ignores the non-propensity chain (improbability of two accidental fires); C and D add requirements that do not exist.

**EV-15 Cross of a character witness (405(a); Michelson)**
Dara is charged with embezzlement. She calls a neighbor, Mo, who testifies that Dara has a reputation in the community for honesty. On cross the prosecutor asks Mo, "Have you heard that Dara was fired from her last job for taking company funds?" The prosecutor has a good-faith basis. The court should
A. sustain, because specific instances may not be used to prove character.
B. overrule; the question tests Mo's knowledge of the reputation to which he testified. **BEST**
C. sustain, because Dara was not convicted.
D. overrule, and the jury may consider the firing as proof Dara embezzled.
Why: 405(a) cross inquiry. D is wrong because the firing is for evaluating Mo's testimony, not for propensity (limiting instruction).

**EV-16 Victim's character (404(a)(2)(B))**
Vern is charged with murder and claims he shot Ray in self-defense. Vern offers testimony that Ray had a reputation in the community for violence. The prosecutor responds with evidence that Vern has a reputation for violence. The court should admit Vern's evidence and
A. exclude the prosecutor's evidence, because Vern's character is not in issue.
B. admit the prosecutor's evidence, because the defendant who offers the victim's trait opens the door to the defendant's same trait. **BEST**
C. exclude the prosecutor's evidence unless Vern testifies.
D. exclude both, because character evidence is never admissible to prove self-defense.
Why: 404(a)(2)(B)(ii).

**EV-17 Civil case; character is not an essential element (404(a)(1); 405(b))**
Pat sues a trucking company over a collision with one of its drivers, Lee. Pat offers evidence that Lee has three prior speeding tickets, to prove Lee was a careless driver and was probably speeding that day. The complaint pleads only ordinary negligence against Lee and respondeat superior against the company. The evidence is
A. admissible, because propensity is probative in negligence cases.
B. admissible as habit.
C. inadmissible to prove Lee was careless on this occasion. **BEST**
D. admissible because Lee's driving is an essential element of Pat's claim.
Why: 404(a)(1). Character is not an essential element of ordinary negligence (405(b)). Tickets are not a specific, regular response to a situation (habit). *Variant for the lesson:* if Pat sues the company for negligent hiring and the claim turns on the company's knowledge of Lee's record, the tickets are admissible to show notice, not propensity.

**EV-18 Routine practice (406)**
Quinn sues Frontier Corp. for failing to give timely notice. Quinn claims the notice letter was never sent. Frontier offers testimony from the mailroom supervisor that every outgoing notice letter is logged, sealed, stamped, and placed in the 4 p.m. outbound tray, a procedure followed daily for six years. The testimony is
A. inadmissible, because there is no eyewitness to the mailing of this letter.
B. inadmissible character evidence.
C. admissible to show routine practice. **BEST**
D. admissible only if the supervisor personally mailed this letter.
Why: 406 needs neither corroboration nor an eyewitness.

**EV-19 Subsequent remedial measure; control (407)**
Tessa slipped on a staircase at the Marlow Building and sues Marlow, who denies it owns or controls the staircase. A week after the fall, Marlow installed a handrail. Tessa offers the installation to prove that Marlow controlled the staircase. The evidence is
A. inadmissible, because it is a subsequent remedial measure.
B. admissible to prove negligence.
C. admissible to prove control, because control is disputed. **BEST**
D. admissible only if the jury is told it is not an admission of negligence.
Why: 407 permits "another purpose... if disputed... ownership, control." B fails on the prohibited purpose. D is not required for admissibility (limiting instruction on request under 105, but the evidence is admissible for control regardless).

**EV-20 Accompanying statement; 409 vs 408**
After a car crash, the defendant, Gus, says to the plaintiff at the scene, "I ran the red light, I'm so sorry; I'll pay your hospital bills." At trial the plaintiff offers all of it. The court should admit
A. all of it.
B. neither statement, since each is part of a settlement offer.
C. the statement "I ran the red light" but not the offer to pay hospital bills. **BEST**
D. the offer to pay hospital bills but not the admission.
Why: 409 excludes the offer to pay medical expenses but does not exclude accompanying statements; the admission is an opposing-party statement. B misapplies 408 (no disputed claim being compromised).

**EV-21 Plea discussions (410)**
Ines is charged with wire fraud. Her lawyer meets with the prosecutor; Ines, present, says, "If I plead to a single count will you drop the rest? I did move the money." No deal results. At trial the prosecutor offers Ines's statement. The court should
A. admit it, as an opposing-party statement.
B. exclude it, because it was made during plea discussions with an attorney for the prosecuting authority. **BEST**
C. admit it, because Ines was not under oath.
D. exclude it, but only if the plea was formally entered.
Why: 410(a)(4). D is wrong: failure of the discussions suffices.

**EV-22 Insurance (411)**
Plaintiff sues Bram for negligent driving. Plaintiff's counsel asks Bram on cross, "Isn't it true your insurance company will pay any judgment?" The question
A. is proper, since it goes to Bram's motive to testify falsely.
B. is improper to prove negligence; there is no other purpose here. **BEST**
C. is proper to show ownership of the car.
D. is proper if Bram is a party.
Why: 411. Another purpose (bias of an insurance-company witness, agency, ownership/control) must actually be in issue.

**EV-23 Rape shield (412)**
Yoan is charged with sexual assault. He says the encounter was consensual. He seeks to offer testimony from two men that they had consensual sex with the complainant in the past year. The court should
A. admit it, because it bears on consent.
B. exclude it as evidence of the complainant's other sexual behavior, absent an applicable exception. **BEST**
C. admit it if Yoan files a motion 14 days before trial.
D. admit it if the complainant testifies.
Why: 412(a); (b)(1)(B) allows only prior behavior with the *accused* to prove consent. C: the motion is a procedural step, not an exception.

**EV-24 Other sexual assault (413)**
Dax is charged with federal sexual assault. The prosecutor gives notice 20 days before trial that she will call a woman who will testify Dax assaulted her eight years ago in a similar way. The evidence is
A. inadmissible propensity evidence.
B. admissible under 404(b) only.
C. admissible for any matter to which it is relevant, including propensity, subject to Rule 403. **BEST**
D. admissible only if Dax was convicted.
Why: 413(a). Notice must be at least 15 days before trial.

**EV-25 Authentication of a text message (901)**
In a contract case, Pablo claims Evan agreed in a text to deliver goods Friday. Pablo testifies he received the text from the number he had always used to reach Evan, that it referred to the specific order and a delivery location only Evan knew, and that Evan phoned two days later to confirm. Evan objects that the printout is not authenticated. The court should
A. exclude it unless the phone carrier authenticates it.
B. admit it; there is evidence sufficient to support a finding that Evan sent it. **BEST**
C. exclude it unless an expert examines Evan's phone.
D. exclude it until Evan admits sending it.
Why: 901(a), (b)(4), (b)(6); sufficient-to-support-a-finding standard.

**EV-26 Self-authentication is not hearsay clearance (902 vs 803)**
In a title dispute, Rae offers a copy of a 1998 deed that bears the county recorder's seal and signature. The opponent objects on authenticity and hearsay. Which is correct?
A. The copy is self-authenticating, so it is admitted.
B. The copy is self-authenticating, but the court must still decide whether a hearsay exception applies. **BEST** (here 803(14) fits)
C. The copy needs a witness who saw the deed signed.
D. The copy is inadmissible because deeds are hearsay.
Why: 902(4) and 803(14). Authentication and hearsay are separate hurdles.

### Privileges and policy exclusions

**EV-27 Third-party presence (attorney-client)**
Kian meets his lawyer to discuss his tax return. He brings his friend Sol for moral support; Sol is not involved in the representation. Later, the government subpoenas Sol, who is asked what Kian told the lawyer. The attorney-client privilege
A. protects the conversation, because a lawyer was present.
B. protects it, because Sol was Kian's friend.
C. does not protect the conversation, because it was not confidential. **BEST**
D. protects it only if Kian is charged with a crime.
Why: a non-essential third party defeats confidentiality. An interpreter or the lawyer's assistant would not.

**EV-28 Corporate employee (Upjohn)**
Bellmont Co.'s general counsel asks a plant technician, at his supervisor's direction, to describe a chemical leak and the technician's own duties so the lawyer can advise the company. In later litigation, the opponent asks the technician what he told the general counsel and what happened at the plant. The court should
A. protect both.
B. protect neither.
C. protect the communication but not the underlying facts the technician knows. **BEST**
D. protect nothing because the technician was not a manager.
Why: Upjohn rejected the control-group test; facts are not privileged.

**EV-29 Crime-fraud**
Lorna consults a lawyer to ask how to transfer her property into a relative's name so a creditor with a pending lawsuit can never collect. The creditor later seeks to call the lawyer. The attorney-client privilege
A. applies, because Lorna sought legal advice.
B. does not apply, because she sought advice in furtherance of a fraud. **BEST**
C. applies unless Lorna waives.
D. does not apply because the claim is civil.
Why: crime-fraud exception.

**EV-30 Spousal testimonial privilege (Trammel)**
Federal prosecutors charge Hugh with tax fraud. His wife, Dee, is willing to testify for the government about what she saw in the home office. Hugh objects. The court should
A. sustain, because a spouse cannot testify against the other.
B. overrule; the privilege belongs to the witness-spouse, who may choose to testify. **BEST**
C. sustain, unless the prosecutor shows need.
D. overrule, because the privilege exists only in civil cases.
Why: Trammel. D is backwards (the testimonial privilege applies in criminal proceedings).

**EV-31 Marital communications**
During their marriage, Mel privately tells his wife Nan, "I hid the cash in the shed." Years later, after they divorce, Mel is tried for theft and the prosecutor calls Nan to repeat the statement. Mel objects. The court should
A. overrule; the marriage has ended.
B. sustain; the confidential communication was made during the marriage and the privilege survives divorce. **BEST**
C. overrule; communications about a crime are never privileged.
D. sustain only if Nan objects.
Why: marital communications privilege (federal). D: either spouse can assert (majority) [MEMORY]. C is wrong (the exception is for joint crimes).

**EV-32 Psychotherapist-patient (Jaffee)**
In a federal civil rights action, defendant officer wants the plaintiff's sessions with her licensed clinical social worker. Plaintiff objects. The court should
A. order production, balancing need against privacy.
B. order production, because social workers are not psychotherapists.
C. sustain the objection; the privilege covers confidential communications to licensed social workers for treatment. **BEST**
D. sustain the objection only if the plaintiff is a patient of a physician.
Why: Jaffee; no balancing.

**EV-33 Waiver (502(b))**
In a federal case, a defendant's paralegal accidentally emails a privileged memo to the plaintiff in a document production. The defendant had a screening protocol; it asks for return the day it discovers the mistake. The plaintiff argues waiver. The court should hold
A. waiver, because the memo left the defendant's control.
B. no waiver, because the disclosure was inadvertent, reasonable steps were taken to prevent it, and the holder promptly acted to rectify it. **BEST**
C. waiver, but only as to that memo.
D. no waiver in this case, but waiver in any state proceeding.
Why: 502(b) protects in federal and state proceedings.

**EV-34 Which law of privilege? (501)**
In a federal diversity action governed by state contract law, a party asserts a privilege recognized by the state's courts but not by federal common law. The court should apply
A. federal common law.
B. state privilege law. **BEST**
C. whichever is more protective.
D. the Federal Rules of Evidence, which list privileges.
Why: 501's last clause. The FRE do not list privileges. (MBE questions rarely turn on this; keep to a short "which law" sorting item.)

### Writings

**EV-35 Best evidence; content vs fact (1002)**
To prove the terms of a written lease, Trent testifies from memory about what the lease says. The original exists and is in Trent's attorney's file. The landlord objects. The court should
A. overrule; Trent is a witness with personal knowledge.
B. sustain; the original is required to prove its content and no excuse is shown. **BEST**
C. overrule; oral testimony is as good as any evidence.
D. sustain, because the lease is hearsay.
Why: 1002. *Pair this with:* if Trent is asked "Did you pay rent?", no original needed (independent knowledge).

**EV-36 Lost original (1004(a))**
Mina sues for payment under a written invoice. The original was destroyed when a warehouse flooded. She offers her bookkeeper's testimony about its terms. The evidence is
A. inadmissible, because originals are required.
B. admissible, since the original was lost without bad faith by the proponent. **BEST**
C. admissible only if the invoice was a public record.
D. admissible only if the opponent consents.
Why: 1004(a).

**EV-37 Summary (1006, 2024)**
In a fraud trial, the prosecutor offers a one-page chart summarizing 6,000 pages of bank statements, which were not introduced. The statements are admissible, and the defense has had access to them. The chart is
A. inadmissible, because summaries are only illustrative aids.
B. inadmissible unless the underlying statements are first introduced.
C. admissible as evidence, if the originals were made available to the defense and the court finds it cannot be conveniently examined in court. **BEST**
D. admissible only to illustrate the testimony.
Why: 1006(a)-(b) (2024 amendment ends the "introduced into evidence" requirement).

**EV-38 Completeness (106)**
In a trial, the prosecutor plays the part of the defendant's recorded statement in which the defendant says, "I took the money." The defendant asks to play the next two sentences, "but I was told it was mine." The prosecutor objects: the second part is hearsay. The court should
A. exclude it, because it is the defendant's self-serving hearsay.
B. admit it at that time if in fairness it ought to be considered with the first part. **BEST**
C. admit it only if the defendant testifies.
D. exclude it unless the defendant calls the declarant.
Why: 106 (2023): completing statement admissible over a hearsay objection when fairness requires.

### Hearsay

**EV-39 Legally operative words (non-hearsay)**
In a contract case, Wells testifies that on March 3 Ames said to him, "I'll buy your boat for $9,000." The statement is offered to prove a contract was formed. The testimony is
A. hearsay, because it is an out-of-court statement.
B. not hearsay, because it is a verbal act with legal significance. **BEST**
C. admissible hearsay as a statement of intent.
D. inadmissible without Ames.
Why: not offered for truth of an asserted fact; the words themselves create the legal relationship.

**EV-40 Effect on the listener**
Carl sues a store for a fall. A clerk testifies that before the fall a customer told him, "The aisle is slick; somebody should mop." The store offers it to prove the store had notice. The statement is
A. inadmissible hearsay.
B. admissible, offered to show notice and not for its truth. **BEST**
C. admissible as an excited utterance.
D. inadmissible, because the customer is not a party.
Why: notice is a non-truth purpose. If the plaintiff used it to prove the floor was slick, a different analysis.

**EV-41 Opposing-party statement vs against interest**
Plaintiff Joan sues Ford Co. for a crash. At trial, Joan offers the testimony of a witness who heard Ford's delivery driver, immediately after the crash and while still on duty, say, "I fell asleep at the wheel." The driver is available but not called. The statement is
A. inadmissible hearsay; the driver is not a party.
B. admissible as an opposing-party statement of Ford's employee on a matter within the scope of his employment. **BEST**
C. admissible only as a statement against interest, if the driver is unavailable.
D. admissible only to impeach the driver.
Why: 801(d)(2)(D). C confuses doctrines. 

**EV-42 Co-conspirator statement**
Ned is on trial for conspiracy to sell stolen goods. The prosecutor offers a statement by Omar, a co-conspirator, made to an undercover agent while the scheme was ongoing: "Ned's got the next truck; we move it Friday." The prosecutor has independent evidence of the conspiracy and Ned's role. The statement is
A. inadmissible hearsay.
B. inadmissible because Omar spoke to a government agent.
C. admissible, as made by a co-conspirator during and in furtherance of the conspiracy. **BEST**
D. admissible only if Omar testifies.
Why: 801(d)(2)(E); Omar's belief that the listener was a buyer is irrelevant; the conspiracy is ongoing. (Confrontation: non-testimonial in the sense of Crawford because made in furtherance of a conspiracy, not to prove past facts; NCBE says co-conspirator statements are not testimonial [READ].)

**EV-43 Prior inconsistent statement at grand jury (801(d)(1)(A))**
At trial, Vicky, an eyewitness, says she can't be sure who fired the gun. The prosecutor offers her earlier sworn grand jury testimony, "It was Dale." Vicky is on the stand and subject to cross. The grand jury testimony is
A. inadmissible hearsay.
B. admissible only to impeach.
C. admissible as substantive evidence. **BEST**
D. admissible only if the grand jury was open to the public.
Why: 801(d)(1)(A): inconsistent, given under penalty of perjury at a proceeding.

**EV-44 Prior consistent statement timing (801(d)(1)(B)(i); Tome)**
Defense counsel suggests on cross that Fay, a witness who is dating the complainant, is lying to protect him. The prosecutor offers Fay's statement to a detective consistent with her testimony, made after she began dating the complainant. The statement is
A. admissible, to rebut the charge of improper motive.
B. admissible, because Fay is subject to cross.
C. inadmissible to rebut the charge of improper motive, because it was made after the alleged motive arose. **BEST**
D. inadmissible because prior consistent statements are never admissible.
Why: Tome and 801(d)(1)(B)(i): the pre-motive requirement is the usual trap. (B)(ii) would help only if the witness were attacked on some other ground, such as faulty memory or an inconsistent statement.

**EV-45 Excited utterance vs PSI**
Rina witnesses a hit-and-run. Ten minutes later, shaking, she tells a bystander, "A blue truck sped through the crosswalk and hit that man!" The statement is offered at trial after Rina cannot be found. It is
A. inadmissible hearsay.
B. admissible as an excited utterance. **BEST**
C. admissible only as a present sense impression.
D. admissible only because Rina is unavailable.
Why: 803(2): startling event, statement made under continuing stress. Ten minutes is too late for "immediately after" under 803(1), but stress, not elapsed time, is the test for 803(2). D is wrong: 803 applies "regardless of whether the declarant is available." (Check the Confrontation Clause too: a statement to a bystander is nontestimonial.)

**EV-46 State of mind (803(3)): forward vs backward**
Lois is found dead. Her roommate testifies that two days earlier Lois said, "I'm going to meet Cal at the lake on Friday." Cal is charged with murder. Offering the statement to prove that Lois went to the lake Friday, the statement is
A. inadmissible hearsay.
B. admissible, as a statement of Lois's then-existing intent or plan. **BEST**
C. admissible, as a statement against interest.
D. admissible, as a dying declaration.
Why: 803(3) (Hillmon). **Contrast:** If Lois said "Cal threatened me last week," offered to prove that Cal threatened her, that is a statement of memory to prove the fact remembered: excluded by 803(3).

**EV-47 Medical statements (803(4))**
Pia goes to the ER and tells the nurse, "I fell off a stepladder at work and landed on my wrist." The statement is offered by Pia's employer's insurer to show that she fell at work. It is
A. inadmissible hearsay.
B. admissible; it describes the general cause of the injury and was made for treatment. **BEST**
C. admissible only if Pia testifies.
D. inadmissible, because the nurse is not a physician.
Why: 803(4). *Variant:* "...and the ladder had a loose rung because my employer refused to fix it" -> fault and the employer's cause are not ordinarily pertinent.

**EV-48 Recorded recollection (803(5))**
Hana testifies she cannot recall the serial numbers she copied into her log the day of the delivery, though she is sure she recorded them accurately when the delivery occurred. The party who called her wants the log placed in evidence. The court should
A. admit the log as an exhibit.
B. allow the log to be read into evidence. **BEST**
C. exclude the log because she cannot recall.
D. allow the jury to inspect the log during deliberations.
Why: 803(5): read, not exhibit, unless offered by the adverse party.

**EV-49 Business records and an outsider's statement (803(6), 805)**
Orion Shipping's incident log says, "Driver reports pedestrian stepped out against the signal, per bystander." Orion's custodian lays a proper business-records foundation. The plaintiff, injured pedestrian, wants the record excluded as to the bystander's remark. Concerning the bystander's remark, the court should
A. admit it as part of the business record.
B. exclude it unless it independently satisfies a hearsay exception. **BEST**
C. admit it because the driver had a business duty.
D. exclude the whole log.
Why: 805; the bystander had no business duty. The driver's own report is a business-record layer. The remark may qualify under another exception (e.g., excited utterance) if facts support it.

**EV-50 Public record in a criminal case (803(8))**
In a criminal drug prosecution, the government offers a police report in which the arresting officer recorded the defendant's alleged sale. The officer is unavailable to testify. The report is
A. admissible as a public record.
B. inadmissible against the defendant in this form; 803(8) excludes law-enforcement observations offered in a criminal case. **BEST**
C. admissible as a business record.
D. admissible if the defendant had not previously objected.
Why: 803(8)(A)(ii) exclusion; and the Confrontation Clause independently bars a testimonial report.

**EV-51 Learned treatise (803(18))**
On cross, the plaintiff's counsel shows a defense physician a medical textbook the physician agrees is an authoritative source and reads a passage that contradicts her opinion. Plaintiff wants to hand the textbook to the jury. The court should
A. allow the passage to be read to the jury but not receive the book as an exhibit. **BEST**
B. refuse, because treatises are hearsay.
C. receive the textbook as an exhibit.
D. allow the textbook only if the author testifies.
Why: 803(18): reliable authority established; read in; no exhibit.

**EV-52 Former testimony (804(b)(1))**
Gil sues Dyer for a fire. During a deposition in an earlier case involving Dyer, a neighbor, now dead, testified that Dyer's furnace was defective. Dyer's lawyer cross-examined her. Gil offers the transcript in the new case against Dyer. It is
A. inadmissible hearsay.
B. admissible, because the declarant is unavailable and Dyer had a similar opportunity and motive to examine her. **BEST**
C. admissible only if the testimony was before a grand jury.
D. admissible only if Gil was a party to the first case.
Why: 804(b)(1); no requirement that the offering party was in the first proceeding as long as the party against whom it is offered (or predecessor in a civil case) had the opportunity.

**EV-53 Dying declaration (804(b)(2)): wrong proceeding**
Ellie is badly beaten and tells police, believing she is about to die, "Sean did this." She survives, but later dies of an unrelated illness before trial. Sean is tried for aggravated assault. The prosecutor offers the statement. It is
A. admissible as a dying declaration.
B. inadmissible as a dying declaration, because the prosecution is not for homicide and this is not a civil case. **BEST**
C. admissible, since Ellie is unavailable.
D. admissible if Ellie believed she would die.
Why: 804(b)(2) only in homicide prosecutions and civil cases. (Also testimonial under Davis/Bryant analysis if no ongoing emergency.)

**EV-54 Statement against interest, exculpating the accused (804(b)(3))**
Ty is charged with a robbery. Ty offers testimony that Cora, before invoking the Fifth and refusing to testify, told a friend, "I did the robbery; Ty wasn't there." Cora is now unavailable by privilege. The statement is
A. inadmissible hearsay.
B. admissible only if there are corroborating circumstances clearly indicating its trustworthiness. **BEST**
C. admissible automatically as a statement against penal interest.
D. admissible only if Cora also implicated herself in a separate crime.
Why: 804(b)(3)(B) requires corroboration in a criminal case when the statement tends to expose the declarant to criminal liability (applies to defense and prosecution).

**EV-55 Collateral statements within a confession (Williamson)**
Ray, arrested for a drug offense, tells police, "I did the delivery, but the supplier was Sam, and Sam made me do it." Ray is unavailable. The prosecutor offers the portion naming Sam against Sam. The portion naming Sam is
A. admissible as part of a declaration against interest.
B. admissible if the whole statement was against interest.
C. admissible only if the court determines that portion itself was against Ray's penal interest. **BEST**
D. inadmissible, because Ray was in custody.
Why: Williamson: remark-by-remark; blame-shifting is suspect. In practice the naming of Sam is likely inadmissible under (b)(3) and, being testimonial, barred by Crawford too.

**EV-56 Forfeiture by wrongdoing (804(b)(6))**
Lee is prosecuted for assault. The victim, Maya, gave the police a detailed account, then was murdered by Lee's associate, who acted on Lee's instructions to prevent Maya from testifying. The prosecutor offers Maya's statement to police against Lee. The statement is
A. inadmissible hearsay and a violation of the Confrontation Clause.
B. admissible, because Lee wrongfully caused Maya's unavailability, intending that result. **BEST**
C. admissible only if Lee is also convicted of Maya's murder.
D. admissible only if Maya's statement was an excited utterance.
Why: 804(b)(6); Giles intent requirement. Forfeiture defeats both hearsay and the Clause.

**EV-57 Double hearsay (805)**
At a negligence trial, Officer Hale testifies that a bystander told him, "The driver of the red car said 'I never saw the stop sign.'" The statement is offered against the driver, Ward. The testimony is
A. admissible, because Ward's statement is an opposing-party statement.
B. admissible only if the bystander's statement also fits an exception. **BEST**
C. inadmissible, because police may not testify to what a bystander said.
D. admissible, because both declarants are available.
Why: layer 1 (Ward to bystander) = 801(d)(2)(A); layer 2 (bystander to officer) = hearsay needing its own exception.

**EV-58 Residual exception (807)**
A deceased grandmother's detailed, dated, contemporaneous diary, kept for years and corroborated by letters, is offered to prove a boundary arrangement. No 803/804 exception fits cleanly. The proponent gave written notice before trial. The court may admit it
A. never, because no listed exception applies.
B. under the residual exception, if it has sufficient guarantees of trustworthiness and is more probative than other evidence reasonably obtainable. **BEST**
C. only if the court finds it is a business record.
D. only if the diary was found in a public office.
Why: 807 as amended 2019.

### Confrontation

**EV-59 Police questioning after the emergency (Davis/Hammon)**
Officers arrive after a fight, find the complainant calm, separate her from the suspect, and ask what happened. She says the defendant hit her. She later fails to appear for trial. The prosecutor offers the officer's testimony about her statement. The statement is
A. admissible as an excited utterance, and the Confrontation Clause is satisfied.
B. testimonial; the Confrontation Clause bars it unless she is unavailable and Defendant had a prior chance to cross-examine. **BEST**
C. admissible because the officer is available for cross.
D. admissible because it was not under oath.
Why: Hammon: no ongoing emergency; primary purpose to establish past events.

**EV-60 911 call during assault**
Tia dials 911 while her ex-partner is breaking into her home: "He's here now, he's got a knife, it's Marcus Lowe." She cannot be located for trial. The prosecution offers the recording. The Confrontation Clause
A. bars it.
B. does not bar it; the primary purpose was to meet an ongoing emergency. **BEST** (hearsay: present sense impression/excited utterance)
C. bars it unless Tia was sworn.
D. does not apply to recordings.
Why: Davis; Bryant.

**EV-61 Statements to non-police (Clark)**
A four-year-old tells her teacher, who asks why she is crying, "Mommy's boyfriend hit me." The child is found incompetent to testify. The prosecutor offers the teacher's testimony. Which is the best Confrontation Clause analysis?
A. Barred, because teachers are mandatory reporters and therefore law enforcement.
B. Probably nontestimonial; its primary purpose was the child's safety, so the Clause does not bar it (hearsay must still be satisfied separately). **BEST**
C. Barred, because the child cannot be cross-examined.
D. Not subject to the hearsay rule because of the Clause.
Why: Clark; hearsay and Clause are separate hurdles.

**EV-62 Lab report and substitute analyst (Melendez-Diaz, Bullcoming, Smith)**
In a DUI trial the prosecution calls a supervisor at the state lab, who did not perform or observe the defendant's blood test, to read the absent analyst's signed report into evidence and state the BAC. The analyst is unavailable at trial, and the defendant never cross-examined him. The report is
A. admissible as a business record.
B. barred by the Confrontation Clause; the report is testimonial and the defendant had no chance to cross-examine the analyst. **BEST**
C. admissible because the supervisor is subject to cross.
D. admissible if the supervisor says she relies on it.
Why: Melendez-Diaz and Bullcoming. A relies on hearsay doctrine; the Clause independently bars it. D recasts it as "basis," which Smith v. Arizona rejects when the statement is relied on for its truth.

**EV-63 Civil case (no Confrontation Clause)**
In a civil negligence suit, the plaintiff offers an out-of-court statement made by an unavailable witness to a police officer. The defendant objects on Confrontation Clause grounds. The court should
A. sustain; the Clause applies in all cases with state action.
B. overrule the Clause objection (the Clause protects criminal defendants) but still decide the hearsay objection under the FRE. **BEST**
C. sustain, since the statement was made to police.
D. overrule both objections.
Why: Sixth Amendment text; NCBE note [READ].

**EV-64 Declarant testifies (Crawford n.9)**
In a criminal trial, the victim testifies and is cross-examined. The prosecutor also offers a recorded statement the victim gave police the next day, consistent with her testimony. The Confrontation Clause
A. bars the recording because it is testimonial.
B. does not bar it, because the declarant appeared at trial and was cross-examined; hearsay rules must still be satisfied. **BEST**
C. bars it unless she was sworn.
D. does not apply to consistent statements.
Why: Crawford n.9. Hearsay: only if 801(d)(1)(B) or another rule fits.

## 10. Teaching and game-design mapping (suggestions for Objection)

- **Guess-first openers:** "A witness says something she heard from someone else. Admissible?" then reveal; or "Can the jury hear the word 'insurance'?"
- **Transfer items (apply the rule to an unseen fact pattern):** most EV items above; label each with the rule it needs. Keep the `transfer: true` item in the lesson that teaches the rule it needs.
- **Build-from-tiles:** (1) the business-records foundation in order: "made at or near the time -> by someone with knowledge -> kept in the course of regularly conducted activity -> regular practice to make it -> shown by custodian or certification -> opponent shows no untrustworthiness"; (2) 404(b)(2) purposes; (3) "How to impeach with a prior inconsistent statement": commit the witness -> credit the prior statement -> confront -> extrinsic evidence only if she denies (2024 613(b) order); (4) confrontation checklist; (5) 609 decision tree.
- **Match pairs:** rule number <-> rule name (803(2) <-> excited utterance); exception <-> its catch ("read in, not exhibit" <-> recorded recollection and learned treatise).
- **Yes/no sorting:** "hearsay or not hearsay?" lists; "testimonial or not?"; "privileged or not?"; "crimen falsi or not?"; "habit or character?"; "in the MBE outline but dropped from NextGen" sorting as an advanced item.
- **Cross-Examination (speed round):** one miss ends it. Bank: "801(d) exclusion, 803 exception, 804 exception, or not admissible?" 4-way, 10 seconds.
- **Mistakes notebook tags:** by rule number and by outline category (I, II, III, IV, V); weights from 1.3.
- **Daily drill / mock exam proportion:** 25 Evidence items ~ 6 presentation, 8 relevancy, 6 hearsay, 5 privileges/writings (from the NCBE fractions; label as derived).
- **Mascot lines:** short, warm; no jokes about losing the exam.
- **Provenance:** `status: "exam"` should be used only for items that match an NCBE sample/released question in principle; since we are not copying any released item, nearly everything is `"general"` (own fact pattern) or `"book"` (rule text from FRE). Use `src: "FRE 801(d)(2)(D)"` style and `src: "Smith v. Arizona (2024)"` style. Mark `"unsure"` for items flagged [MEMORY]/[UNSURE] in this file until double-checked.
- **Do not teach as tested:** Dead Man's statutes, state hearsay variants, proposed Rule 707, state-specific privileges, and any Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure detail beyond what the evidence rules cite.
