So Property is **25 scored questions = one-seventh (about 14.3%)**; the 14.3% is our arithmetic, NCBE states the 25-per-subject count. Source: https://www.ncbex.org/exams/mbe/preparing

  - "The outline is not intended to list every aspect of a topic mentioned. Although the test items for each MBE are developed from these categories, each topic is not necessarily tested on each examination."
  - Real Property NOTE: **"Approximately one-fifth of the Real Property questions on the MBE will be based on each of the categories I through V."** So about 5 of 25 questions per category.
  - The five categories: **I Ownership of real property; II Rights in real property; III Real estate contracts; IV Mortgages/security devices; V Titles.**

- A. Present estates and future interests: present estates (fees simple, defeasible fees, life estates); future interests (reversions; remainders vested and contingent; executory interests; possibilities of reverter and powers of termination; rules affecting these interests: survivorship, class gifts, waste, cy pres)

## 2. Topic modules

## Category I. Ownership of real property

### MODULE 1. Present estates (I.A.1)

**Rules**
- **Fee simple absolute**: "To A and his heirs" (words of limitation), or just "To A" in modern law (presumption of fee simple unless a lesser estate is clearly expressed). Alienable, devisable, inheritable, unlimited in duration. Fee tail is abolished in nearly every state (converted to fee simple or life estate plus remainder).
- **Fee simple determinable (FSD)**: durational language: "so long as", "while", "during", "until", "for as long as". Ends **automatically** on the stated event; the grantor (or heirs) holds a **possibility of reverter** that becomes possessory automatically.
- **Fee simple subject to condition subsequent (FSSCS)**: conditional language: "but if", "on condition that", "provided that" plus an express right of reentry ("O may reenter"). Ends only when the grantor **exercises** the **right of entry (power of termination)**; the estate continues until then. Courts disfavor forfeitures and construe doubtful language as a covenant or an FSSCS rather than an FSD.
- **Fee simple subject to an executory limitation (FSSEL)**: "To A, but if X, then to B" where B is a third party. B holds an **executory interest**; the estate shifts automatically.
- **Life estate** (for the life of the tenant) and **life estate pur autre vie** (measured by another's life). The life tenant may transfer her estate, but the transferee's estate still ends at the measuring life. A life tenant cannot devise it (it ends at death; for pur autre vie, the interest passes to heirs/devisees for the remaining life).
- **Life tenant duties**: no waste. **Affirmative waste** (voluntary acts that diminish value: e.g., cutting timber for sale, demolishing, opening mines (open-mines doctrine permits continuing mines already open)), **permissive waste** (failure to make ordinary repairs, pay ordinary taxes, pay mortgage interest), **ameliorative waste** (change that increases value; traditionally still waste; modern courts allow when neighborhood has changed and remaindermen are not harmed, **[UNCERTAIN: state split; usually tested only by stating the rule]**). Ordinary taxes and mortgage interest: life tenant pays up to the income or rental value of the land; payments of mortgage principal are shared by life tenant and remaindermen in proportion to the present values of their interests (usual rule); the life tenant's payments beyond that give a right to contribution.
- **Words of purchase vs limitation**: "To A and her heirs" gives A a fee; "heirs" is not a gift to anyone else.

**Majority / minority**
- Where language is ambiguous, courts prefer FSSCS (or no forfeiture) over FSD. In some states a statute limits how long a possibility of reverter or right of entry survives (e.g., 30 or 40 years); and some states convert an FSD to FSSCS by statute **[UNCERTAIN, state-specific; MBE would state it]**.
- Right of entry / possibility of reverter and the RAP: not subject to the common-law RAP in the majority (see Module 3).

**Traps**
- "So long as" vs "but if": FSD vs FSSCS; the second needs reentry.
- A remainder cannot follow a fee simple; "to A, but if X then to B" is an executory interest, not a remainder.
- Reversion belongs to the grantor after a **lesser** estate; after a **fee** (FSD/FSSCS), the grantor holds a possibility of reverter / right of entry, not a reversion.
- "To A for life" with nothing more = A life estate, O reversion.

**One-breath lines**
- "Automatic end means determinable; 'but if' means O must act."
- "After a fee the grantor keeps a reverter or a right of entry; after a life estate the grantor keeps a reversion."

**Fact patterns**

**P1-1 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to the Lakeview School District so long as the land is used for public school purposes." What interest, if any, does Owner retain?
(A) A reversion. (B) A possibility of reverter. (C) A right of entry. (D) An executory interest.
**Best answer: B.** "So long as" is durational and creates a fee simple determinable; the grantor's retained interest is a possibility of reverter. Wrong: A, a reversion follows a lesser estate than a fee; C, a right of entry accompanies an FSSCS ("but if ... reenter"); D, an executory interest is held by a third party, not the grantor.

**P1-2 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana, but if the premises are ever used to sell alcohol, Owner may reenter and retake the premises." Ana opens a bar on the land. A month later Owner has taken no action. Who has the present possessory estate?
(A) Owner, automatically on the first sale. (B) Ana, until Owner exercises the right of entry. (C) Ana, because forfeitures are void. (D) The state, by escheat.
**Best answer: B.** FSSCS: the estate continues until the holder exercises the power of termination (subject to waiver, laches, or statute). Wrong: A, automatic termination is the FSD rule; C, such conditions are valid (only unlawful or total restraints are void); D, nothing escheats.

**P1-3 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life." Ana fails for three years to pay the annual real estate taxes of $4,000 (the land produces rental value of $20,000 a year) and ignores a leaking roof. Owner, who holds the reversion, wants to sue. Which is the best statement?
(A) Owner has no claim because waste applies only to affirmative acts. (B) Owner may recover from Ana for permissive waste and may pay the taxes to protect the property and obtain reimbursement. (C) Only Ana's creditors may sue. (D) Owner must wait until Ana dies.
**Best answer: B.** A life tenant must pay ordinary taxes (up to the land's income) and keep the land in ordinary repair; failure is permissive waste; the holder of the future interest (here a reversion) may sue for damages or an injunction and may protect his interest by paying and recouping. Wrong: A, permissive waste is waste; C, future-interest holders sue; D, waste is actionable during the life estate.

### MODULE 2. Future interests (I.A.2)

**Rules**
- **Reversion**: grantor's interest after conveying a lesser vested estate (life estate, term of years). Always vested.
- **Remainder**: created in a transferee, takes effect on the natural end of a prior estate of lesser duration, and does not divest it early. (1) **Indefeasibly vested** ("to A for life, then to B"; B is alive and ascertained, no condition). (2) **Vested subject to open** ("to A for life, then to A's children" when A has at least one child; the class stays open to later children until it closes). (3) **Vested subject to total divestment** ("to A for life, then to B, but if B fails to graduate, to C"; the "but if" divesting language is an executory interest in C). (4) **Contingent**: either **unascertained/unborn takers** (A has no children yet) or **condition precedent** ("to B if B graduates").
- **Executory interest**: a future interest in a transferee that is not a remainder because it cuts off a prior interest, or follows a fee, or takes effect after a gap. **Shifting** (divests another transferee) or **springing** (divests the grantor, effective at a future date or event).
- **Possibility of reverter / right of entry (power of termination)**: retained by grantor after FSD / FSSCS.
- **Alienability**: Vested remainders, reversions, executory interests, and (in the modern majority) contingent remainders and rights of entry are alienable inter vivos, devisable, and descendible; the ones in unascertained persons cannot be transferred until ascertained. Traditionally the right of entry was not alienable inter vivos; most states now allow transfer or by statute **[UNCERTAIN]**.
- **Abolished doctrines** (nearly all states; assume unless the question says otherwise): Rule in Shelley's Case (remainder to the heirs of the life tenant merges into a fee in the life tenant); Doctrine of Worthier Title (as a rule of law); destructibility of contingent remainders (if the prior estate ends before the remainder vests, the remainder is not destroyed; it takes effect as an executory interest or stays alive).
- **Class gifts**: class gift = gift to a group by a class label ("children", "grandchildren", "heirs"). **Class closes** physically when no more members can be born (e.g., parent dies) and, under the **rule of convenience**, when any class member is entitled to demand possession/distribution (this closes the class to later-born members). A child in gestation counts. "Heirs" of a living person: no one is the heir of a living person; a remainder to the "heirs" of a living person (modern, with Shelley's Case/WT abolished) is a contingent remainder in unascertained persons.
- **Survivorship**: express ("to A for life, then to B if B survives A"), or implied by the structure. A plain remainder to a named person ("then to B") is vested and does **not** require B to survive A: if B dies before A, B's heirs or devisees take at A's death. A remainder "to A's children" when A has children is vested subject to open and does not require survivorship unless worded ("to such of A's children as survive A"). Modern statutory trend (UPC 2-707 for trust future interests) implies survivorship to the time of distribution. **[UNCERTAIN: UPC 2-707 is adopted in a minority of states; common-law MBE answer is no implied survivorship for a vested interest.]**
- **Cy pres** (as listed in the outline's rules affecting future interests): in the future-interests context, the courts' power (and many statutes') to reform a gift that would violate the rule against perpetuities so that it carries out the transferor's intent as nearly as possible (modern reformation statutes and the Restatement (Second) of Property: Donative Transfers take this approach; the same label also describes charitable-trust reformation when the stated purpose is impossible or impractical). Treat both uses as the same idea: "as near as possible".

**Traps**
- Remainder vs executory: does it follow a fee or cut off a prior interest? Then executory.
- Open/closed class: A has two children, a third is born later, all survive A: all three take.
- A remainder "vested subject to divestment" and "vested subject to open" are different labels.
- Vested remainder subject to open for "A's children" is **transferable** and may pass by will; a contingent remainder in unborn children cannot be conveyed (nobody to convey).

**One-breath lines**
- "Remainder waits its turn; executory interest cuts the line."
- "Rule of convenience: the class closes when the first member can take."

**Fact patterns**

**P2-1 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life, then to Ben's children." Ben is alive and has no children. Which statement best describes the interests?
(A) Ana has a life estate; Ben's children have a vested remainder; Owner has no interest. (B) Ana has a life estate; Ben's children have a contingent remainder; Owner has a reversion. (C) Ana has a life estate; Ben's children have an executory interest; Owner has a possibility of reverter. (D) Ana has a fee simple subject to divestment.
**Best answer: B.** Unborn/unascertained takers make the remainder contingent; until it vests, the grantor holds a reversion (the estate would return to Owner if no child is born). Wrong: A, a remainder is not vested if the takers do not exist; C, the interest follows a life estate and does not cut it off, so it is a remainder, and the grantor's interest after a life estate is a reversion; D, nothing in the language gives Ana a fee.

**P2-2 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life, then to Ben for life, then to Cal and his heirs." Cal later dies intestate, survived by his daughter Dee, and then Ana dies while Ben is alive. Who is entitled to possession, and for how long?
(A) Ben, for his life; then Dee in fee simple. (B) Ben, for his life; then Owner. (C) Dee only; Ben's interest was destroyed when Cal died. (D) Ben, for his life; then Dee, but only if Dee survived Ana.
**Best answer: A.** Cal had an indefeasibly vested remainder in fee, which is descendible; it passed to Dee at Cal's death; Ben takes his vested remainder for life when Ana dies; then Dee. Wrong: B, the reversion in Owner never arises because the fee remainder in Cal exhausted the estate; C, Ben's remainder is unaffected; D, no survivorship requirement attaches to a vested remainder.

**P2-3 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life, then to Ana's children." At the time Ana has one child, Xia. Later Ana has two more children, Yan and Zed. All three survive Ana. Which statement is correct?
(A) At the conveyance, Xia held a vested remainder subject to open, and at Ana's death all three children take equally. (B) Xia took a contingent remainder and the others take nothing. (C) Only Xia takes, because the class closed at the conveyance. (D) Owner takes a reversion because the remainder was not vested.
**Best answer: A.** Xia, an existing child, has a vested remainder subject to open; the class stays open until Ana dies. Wrong: B, a living, ascertained taker with no condition is vested; C, a class does not close until it must (Ana's death) or the rule of convenience applies; D, a vested remainder leaves no reversion.

**P2-4 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana and her heirs, but if the land is ever used for commercial purposes, then to Ben and his heirs." Ben's interest is best described as
(A) a contingent remainder. (B) a shifting executory interest. (C) a right of entry. (D) a possibility of reverter.
**Best answer: B.** It follows a fee simple and divests Ana's estate when the event occurs. Wrong: A, a remainder cannot follow a fee simple; C and D, those belong only to the grantor.

**P2-5 [MBE-style] (rule of convenience).** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life, then to the children of Ben." When the conveyance is made, Ben (who is alive) has one child, Xia. When Ana dies, Ben has two children, Xia and Yan. Ben later has a third child, Zed, while still alive. Under the traditional class-closing rule of construction, who takes Blackacre?
(A) Xia only. (B) Xia and Yan. (C) Xia, Yan and Zed, because Ben is still alive and may have more children. (D) Only the children living at the conveyance.
**Best answer: B.** At Ana's death, Xia is entitled to possession, so the class closes under the rule of convenience and excludes later-born Zed. Wrong: A, Yan was a member when the class closed; C, that would apply only if the class had not closed; D, the class stays open for children born before it closes. (Rule of convenience is a rule of construction; a contrary intent in the instrument controls. Mark `status: "general"`.)

### MODULE 3. Rule against perpetuities and reforms (I.D.1; MBE only, not in NextGen)

**Rule (common law)**: "No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after some life in being at the creation of the interest." This is John Chipman Gray's classic formulation (a standard statement; the Cornell LII Wex entry only paraphrases it, vaguely, as "does not vest within 21 years of life-in-being"). The USRAP statutory text below was verified in the West Virginia and Virginia enactments.
- **Applies to**: contingent remainders; executory interests; vested remainders subject to open (class gifts); options to purchase in gross; rights of first refusal (majority); powers of appointment. 
- **Does not apply to**: reversions; possibilities of reverter; rights of entry (majority); vested remainders (absolutely vested); and gift-over from one charity to another.
- **Test at creation, "what might happen"**: not "what actually happens" (that is wait-and-see). The interest is void if there is any possibility, however remote, of vesting beyond lives in being + 21 years.
- **Classic traps**: fertile octogenarian (presumed able to have children at any age), unborn widow (A's "widow" may not be alive at creation), slothful executor (administrative contingency: reasonable; the rule is "reasonable time"), the magic gravel pit (event with no time limit), precocious toddler, **age contingency above 21** ("to A's children who reach 25"), **all-or-nothing rule for class gifts** (if the interest of any potential class member might vest too late, the whole class gift is void).
- **Effect of invalidity**: strike the offending interest and read the conveyance as if it were not there (the prior estates stand; the reversion/possibility of reverter in the grantor or residuary takes).

**Reforms**
- **Wait-and-see** (measure by actual events during lives in being + 21): Restatement (Second) of Property: Donative Transfers.
- **Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities (USRAP, 1986)**: a nonvested property interest is invalid unless (1) when created, it is certain to vest or terminate no later than 21 years after the death of an individual then alive, **or** (2) it actually vests or terminates within **90 years** after its creation. Verified text: West Virginia Code § 36-1A-1, https://code.wvlegislature.gov/36-1A-1/ ("it is certain to vest or terminate no later than 21 years after the death of an individual then alive" / "The interest either vests or terminates within 90 years after its creation"). Includes **reformation** (court reforms a disposition that violates the rule within the 90 years in the way that best approximates the transferor's plan); excludes certain interests (nondonative transfers, fiduciary powers, charitable-to-charitable gifts, pension plans, etc.): see Virginia Code § 55.1-127, https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title55.1/chapter1/section55.1-127/ .
- **Adoption counts**: sources disagree (29 or 30 states or territories per Wikipedia and a Hawaii article; "roughly half the states"). **[UNCERTAIN: do not state a number in the app]**. Several states have abolished the rule or extended the period for trusts (e.g., Virginia's 1,000-year period for trusts created on or after July 1, 2025 per the statute text we read; Florida 360 years and 1,000 years for later trusts per a search summary). These are not MBE rules; the MBE would state the jurisdiction's rule.
- **Other**: the "age-reduction" and "second look" statutory fixes; cy pres reformation.

**Options and rights of first refusal**
- Option to purchase in gross (not tied to a lease): subject to the common-law RAP if it can be exercised beyond lives + 21 (majority); invalid if exercisable at any time with no limit. Option appurtenant to a lease (e.g., renewal, purchase option exercisable during the term) is generally exempt. Rights of first refusal: subject to RAP in the majority view; several states exempt commercial pre-emptive rights by statute or case law. NextGen: the NextGen topic "Alienability" includes options and ROFR but says that topic does "not include the rule against perpetuities".

**One-breath lines**
- "Does it vest, if at all, within a life in being plus 21? Test it at birth, not later."
- "A class gift is all or nothing."

**Fact patterns** (all assume a jurisdiction applying the common-law RAP unmodified, unless stated)

**P3-1 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life, then to Ana's children who reach 25." Ana has a 30-year-old child, Xia, at the time of the conveyance. Is the remainder valid?
(A) Yes, because Xia has already reached 25. (B) Yes, because Ana is a life in being. (C) No, because the class might include a child born later who would reach 25 more than 21 years after the death of every life in being. (D) No, because remainders to classes are always void.
**Best answer: C.** Under the all-or-nothing rule for class gifts, any possible member whose interest might vest late voids the whole gift. Ana could have another child, Yan, shortly before dying; Yan reaches 25 more than 21 years after Ana's (and Xia's) death. Wrong: A, Xia's own satisfaction does not save the class (the class remains open until Ana's death and a later child can come in); B, the validating life must guarantee vesting within 21 years after her death, which is not the case; D, overbroad.

**P3-2 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life, then to such of Ana's children as survive Ana." Is the remainder valid?
(A) No, children may be born after the conveyance. (B) Yes; the class closes and vests at Ana's death, and Ana is a life in being. (C) No, because the class is open. (D) Yes, but only if Ana has a child now.
**Best answer: B.** Vesting is certain, if at all, at Ana's death. Wrong: A and C, unborn takers do not matter when vesting must occur at the death of a life in being; D, no need for an existing child.

**P3-3 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to the City Library so long as it is used for library purposes, then to Ben and his heirs." Which statement is correct in a jurisdiction applying the common-law rule?
(A) Ben's executory interest is valid. (B) Ben's executory interest is void; the Library has a fee simple determinable and Owner has a possibility of reverter. (C) The entire conveyance is void. (D) Ben has a possibility of reverter.
**Best answer: B.** The gift over to a non-charity after a charity may occur at any distant time (no life in being measures it); the executory interest is void and struck; the possibility of reverter is not subject to the RAP. Wrong: A, would vest remotely; C, only the offending interest is struck; D, a possibility of reverter belongs to the grantor.

**P3-4 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life, then to Ana's widow for life, then to Ana's descendants then living." Ana is married to Ben. Which is correct under the common-law rule?
(A) All interests are valid. (B) The remainder in "Ana's descendants then living" is void because Ana's "widow" may be someone not yet born. (C) Only the life estate in Ana's widow is void. (D) The remainder in descendants is valid because Ben is a life in being.
**Best answer: B.** The "widow" is whoever Ana is married to at death, who might be unborn at the creation and outlive all lives in being by more than 21 years. Wrong: A, no; C, widow's life estate is valid (it vests at Ana's death); D, the widow need not be Ben.
Follow-up for a USRAP version: under USRAP the remainder is valid if it in fact vests within 90 years of creation.

**P3-5 [MBE-style].** Owner grants to Corp an option to purchase Blackacre "exercisable at any time by Corp or its successors." The option is not tied to any lease. The jurisdiction follows the common-law rule. The option is
(A) valid, because options are contract rights. (B) void under the rule against perpetuities. (C) valid if exercised within a reasonable time. (D) valid as an easement.
**Best answer: B.** An option in gross exercisable without time limit may be exercised beyond lives + 21. Wrong: A, an option is a nonvested interest in land subject to RAP; C, validity is tested at creation; D, no easement.

### MODULE 4. Cotenancy (I.B)

**Rules**
- **Tenancy in common (TIC)**: unity of possession only; shares may be unequal; each share transferable, devisable, descendible; no survivorship. **Default** for a conveyance "to A and B" (modern law) and for unclear language.
- **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (JT)**: four unities (time, title, interest, possession). Created by express language ("to A and B as joint tenants with right of survivorship"). Survivor takes the whole; a joint tenant's will has no effect on the JT interest. Severed by a conveyance by one joint tenant to a stranger (the grantee becomes a TIC as to that share; the remaining joint tenants stay joint tenants among themselves).
- **Tenancy by the entirety (TBE)**: five unities (the four plus marriage). Available in a minority of states (sources say 24-25 plus DC **[UNCERTAIN count]**). Neither spouse can sever alone; creditors of one spouse alone cannot reach it; divorce converts it to a TIC. NextGen names it; the MBE outline names "tenancy in common and joint tenancy" only.
- **Severance of a joint tenancy**: (a) conveyance of the interest (inter vivos) to a third party or to oneself (modern statutes allow a self-conveyance to sever); (b) **mortgage**: title-theory states treat a mortgage as a conveyance and sever (at least until paid); **lien-theory states (the MBE default): the mortgage does not sever**, and if the mortgagor-joint-tenant dies first, the survivor takes the entire property **free** of the lien (the lien dies with the debtor's interest); (c) **lease** by one joint tenant: majority does not sever; the lease ends when the lessor-joint-tenant dies; (d) **judgment lien**: majority does not sever; if the debtor dies first, the lien dies; if the creditor executes and sells before death, the sale severs; (e) **contract to convey**: may sever through equitable conversion; (f) **murder** of the co-tenant (slayer statutes) severs as to the slayer's share or in effect bars survivorship; (g) mutual agreement; (h) a joint tenant's will cannot sever.
- **Simultaneous death**: under the Uniform Simultaneous Death Act (1993, 2006), when the order of death cannot be established by clear and convincing evidence, each joint tenant's interest is divided as if each survived as to one-half and passes as a TIC half each. **[VERIFY text]**
- **Relations among cotenants**: each has the right to possess the **whole**. No rent between cotenants unless there is **ouster** (wrongful exclusion) or an agreement; a cotenant in sole possession without ouster owes nothing, but if she excludes the other she owes fair rental value. **Rents from third parties** are shared pro rata after expenses (majority). **Contribution**: a cotenant who pays more than her share of taxes, mortgage payments (principal and interest, limited by the value of her possession), and **necessary** repairs gets contribution (often via accounting in partition); **no** contribution for improvements, but credit may be given at partition for increased value. **Ouster and adverse possession**: a cotenant can adversely possess against another only by ouster or unequivocal notice of repudiation.
- **Partition**: any cotenant (TIC or JT) may compel partition (in kind or by sale); a TBE cannot be partitioned by one spouse. Partition **in kind** is preferred; judicial sale ordered if in-kind division is impracticable or would cause great prejudice (majority). **Uniform Partition of Heirs Property Act (UPHPA)**: for "heirs property" (acquired from a relative without a title examination, with no agreement on partition), non-requesting cotenants get a right to buy out the requesting cotenant at appraised value and courts must weigh factors (including sentimental value) before ordering sale; counts vary: ABA update early 2026 says 24 states plus USVI and DC; Louisiana State Law Institute (Dec 2025) says 25; Urban Institute (Mar 2026) says 26. **[UNCERTAIN: say "about two dozen states"]**. Sources: https://www.americanbar.org/groups/real_property_trust_estate/resources/probate-property/2026-january-february/uniform-laws-update-2025-legislative-update/
- A cotenant may transfer, lease or mortgage her **own** share only; a lessee/purchaser takes subject to partition and to the other cotenants' rights.

**Majority/minority**
- Ouster standard; mortgage severance (title vs lien theory); whether rent from a cotenant in sole possession is owed (majority: no, absent ouster; some states by statute: yes on demand).
- Judgment lien severance: majority no.

**Traps**
- "To A and B" (no more) = TIC.
- Conveying one's share to a third party severs only that share. If A, B and C are joint tenants and A sells to D, then D is a TIC (one-third) and B and C remain joint tenants between themselves (they share two-thirds with survivorship).
- Mortgage in a **lien-theory** state does not sever; survivor takes free.
- Cotenants in possession pay no rent absent ouster.

**One-breath lines**
- "Four unities for joint tenancy; selling your share breaks it for that share."
- "A cotenant may use the whole, so no rent without an ouster."

**Fact patterns**

**P4-1 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana and Ben as joint tenants with right of survivorship." Ana then conveys her interest to Cal. Ben dies and leaves a will devising his entire estate to Dee. Who owns Blackacre?
(A) Cal alone. (B) Dee alone. (C) Cal and Dee, one-half each, as tenants in common. (D) Cal and Ben's estate as joint tenants.
**Best answer: C.** Ana's conveyance severed the joint tenancy as to her share; Cal and Ben became TICs. Ben's half is devisable and passes to Dee. Wrong: A and B, Ben's right of survivorship was destroyed; D, no joint tenancy remains.

**P4-2 [MBE-style].** In a lien-theory jurisdiction, Ana and Ben own Blackacre as joint tenants with right of survivorship. Without Ben's knowledge, Ana mortgages her interest to First Bank. Ana dies. Which is correct?
(A) Ben owns all of Blackacre subject to the mortgage. (B) Ben owns all of Blackacre free of the mortgage. (C) Ana's heirs own one-half, subject to the mortgage. (D) First Bank owns one-half.
**Best answer: B.** In a lien-theory jurisdiction a mortgage does not sever; Ana's interest ended at death; the lien on her interest ended with it. Wrong: A, the mortgage was on Ana's interest only; C, no TIC was created; D, a lien theory mortgagee holds only a lien.

**P4-3 [MBE-style].** Ana and Ben own Blackacre as tenants in common. Ana lives on Blackacre; Ben lives elsewhere and has never been excluded. Ben demands rent equal to one-half the fair rental value. Ben is
(A) entitled to the rent, because Ana is in exclusive possession. (B) not entitled to rent, because Ana has not ousted him. (C) entitled to rent only if Ana consents. (D) entitled to rent but only from the date of demand.
**Best answer: B.** Each cotenant may possess the whole; rent is owed only on ouster (or by agreement or statute). Wrong: A, exclusive use is not exclusion; C and D, no basis.

**P4-4 [MBE-style].** Ana and Ben own Blackacre as tenants in common, equal shares. Ana pays $12,000 to replace a failed roof that threatened the house, and $20,000 to add a swimming pool. Ben refuses to contribute to either. If Ana sues now for contribution, the best result is
(A) $6,000 for the roof only. (B) $16,000 for both, pro rata. (C) nothing; contribution is available only at partition for either. (D) $6,000 for the pool only.
**Best answer: A.** Necessary repairs support contribution; improvements do not (although a partition court may credit value added). Wrong: B, improvements are not compelled; C, necessary repair is recoverable by accounting; D, reversed.

**P4-5 [MBE-style].** Husband and Wife own a house as tenants by the entirety in a state recognizing the form. A creditor has a judgment against Husband alone. May the creditor force a sale of the house?
(A) Yes, of Husband's one-half. (B) No, because one spouse's creditor cannot reach entireties property. (C) Yes, if the debt exceeds the equity. (D) Yes, because the marriage unity is destroyed by judgment.
**Best answer: B.** TBE is held by the marital unit; neither spouse can alienate or encumber alone to defeat the other's survivorship (majority). Wrong: A, C, D, those ignore the unity of marriage.

**P4-6 [MBE-style].** Ana and Ben own Blackacre as tenants in common. Ana lives there alone, pays all taxes, and tells nobody she claims the whole. After 25 years (statutory period: 20 years) Ana claims title by adverse possession. Ben was never told. Result?
(A) Ana owns all by adverse possession. (B) Ana owns one-half; Ben retains one-half. (C) Ben still owns one-half, because Ana never ousted Ben or gave notice of repudiation. (D) Ben owns all because Ana is a trespasser.
**Best answer: C.** A cotenant's possession is not adverse without ouster or clear notice of hostile claim. Wrong: A, no ouster; B, not a split; D, Ana is a rightful cotenant.

### MODULE 5. Landlord-tenant law (I.C)

NCBE anchors (MBE): types of tenancies; possession and rent; transfers by landlord or tenant; termination (surrender, mitigation, anticipatory breach, security deposits); habitability and suitability. NextGen anchors: tenancy for years / periodic / at will / at sufferance (incl. creation by operation of law, holdover, invalid lease); possession, rent, actual and constructive eviction, quiet enjoyment; assignment and sublease (covenants that run with the land, reassignment, original tenant liability, sublessee liability); early termination (surrender, mitigation, anticipatory breach, abandonment/repossession); habitability and suitability (independent and dependent covenants, repair, wear and tear, destruction, implied warranty of habitability).

**Rules**

*Types of tenancies*
- **Term of years**: fixed beginning and end; ends automatically with no notice; Statute of Frauds: a lease for **more than one year** must be in writing (some states set the line at three years) **[state detail]**. An oral lease within the statute of frauds is unenforceable as a term, but a tenant who takes possession and pays rent usually holds a **periodic tenancy**, measured by the rent-payment period (monthly rent = month-to-month), on the oral terms consistent with a periodic tenancy; some courts enforce the oral term by part performance.
- **Periodic tenancy** (year-to-year, month-to-month): renews automatically until either party gives proper notice; notice must be at least one full period (common law: six months for year-to-year; many statutes shorten) and must end the tenancy at the end of a period. Created expressly, by implication (oral lease + rent), or by holdover/acceptance of rent.
- **Tenancy at will**: no fixed term; either party may end it at any time (modern statutes often require a notice, commonly 30 days); ends on either party's death or on an attempted transfer by the tenant or a conveyance by the landlord.
- **Tenancy at sufferance** (holdover tenant): tenant remains after the term without consent. The landlord may **elect** to evict as a trespasser/holdover tenant (with damages for fair rental value, or double rent where a statute provides) **or** to treat the tenant as a new periodic tenant (landlord's acceptance of rent after the term is the classic election). The length of the new periodic tenancy varies (common law: year-to-year if the lease was for a year or more; many states: measured by the rent period) **[UNCERTAIN; state-specific]**.

*Possession and rent*
- **Landlord's duty to deliver possession**: **American rule (majority)**: landlord must deliver actual possession at the start (tenant may terminate and/or recover damages if a holdover prior tenant or a trespasser is in possession). English rule (minority): only the legal right to possession.
- **Covenant of quiet enjoyment** is implied in every lease. Breach: **actual eviction** (total: rent stops; partial: at common law the tenant is excused from the entire rent while partially evicted) or **constructive eviction**.
- **Constructive eviction (elements)**: (1) substantial interference with the tenant's use and enjoyment, (2) attributable to the landlord (act or breach of a duty, including failure to repair where there is a duty), (3) the tenant gives notice and a reasonable time to cure, and (4) the tenant **vacates within a reasonable time** after the failure. A tenant who stays cannot claim constructive eviction. Remedy: terminate, stop paying rent, sue for damages.
- **Rent covenant**: at common law, the tenant's covenant to pay rent and the landlord's covenants are **independent**; modern residential law treats them as **dependent** (habitability). Rent is an obligation of the tenant for the term regardless of use unless the landlord breaches; a tenant who abandons stays liable unless surrender, eviction or mitigation limits damages.
- **Landlord's remedies for default**: at common law, **no right to terminate for nonpayment unless the lease contains a reentry/forfeiture clause**; modern statutes provide summary possession actions (notice, hearing, writ). **Self-help**: **majority: a landlord may not use self-help (changing locks, removing tenant's goods, cutting utilities) even when the tenant is in default; must use judicial process** (Berg v. Wiley, 264 N.W.2d 145 (Minn. 1978)); minority allows peaceable self-help. Distraint/landlord's lien: abolished in most states for residential tenants.
- **Security deposits**: statutes control (use, itemization, return period, interest, penalties). Landlord may deduct for unpaid rent and damage beyond **ordinary wear and tear**; wrongful retention can bring multiplied damages **[state-specific figures; MBE gives them]**.
- **Destruction of premises** (not tenant's fault): common law: the lease continues and the tenant still owes rent (the tenant has the land); many states and the Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (URLTA) allow termination or rent abatement. Condemnation: total taking ends the lease; partial taking abates rent **[low frequency]**.

*Transfers*
- **Assignment** = tenant transfers the **entire remaining term**; **sublease** = tenant retains any part of the term (even a day) or a reversion (**majority: the "reversion" test**; a minority uses the parties' intent). 
- **Privity**: Landlord and the **original tenant** are in privity of contract and privity of estate; after an assignment, the original tenant remains liable in contract (like a surety) unless the landlord releases him (novation); the **assignee** is in privity of estate with the landlord and is liable on covenants that run with the land (rent, repair) **for as long as he holds the estate**, and also in contract if he **assumed** the lease. A **sublessee** has neither privity with the landlord, so the landlord cannot sue the sublessee on the head lease (unless the sublessee assumed the head lease covenants); the sublessee owes the sublessor (original tenant). The original tenant, as sublessor, remains liable to the landlord.
- **Reassignment**: an assignee who reassigns is liable to the landlord only for breaches that occur while he holds the estate (privity of estate ends), unless he promised the landlord to perform for the whole term **[UNCERTAIN: states differ on contract liability after reassignment; keep out of "book" items]**.
- **Covenants against assignment/sublease**: valid, but **strictly construed against the landlord** (a clause barring assignment does not bar sublease, and vice versa). **If the lease is silent on reasonableness, the traditional/majority rule lets the landlord withhold consent for any reason; a growing minority (e.g., Kendall v. Ernest Pestana, Inc., 40 Cal.3d 488 (1985)) imposes reasonableness, most clearly in commercial leases** **[VERIFY count]**. Landlord who accepts rent from the assignee with knowledge of the breach **waives** the right to terminate for that breach. A transfer in violation of the covenant is effective between the parties, but the landlord may recover damages or terminate if the lease has a forfeiture clause. Consent to one assignment does not waive the covenant for later ones unless it says so (the old rule in Dumpor's Case, treating one consent as a waiver for all, is rejected in modern law).
- **Landlord's transfer**: the landlord's reversion and rent rights pass to the buyer; covenants that touch and concern run with the reversion (Spencer's Case, 1583).

*Termination*
- **Surrender**: the tenant offers to give up possession and the landlord accepts, expressly or **by operation of law** (landlord's acts inconsistent with the tenant's continuing rights, such as taking the keys, moving in or re-letting for the landlord's own account). A surrender ends future rent liability. A landlord's mere inaction or re-letting expressly for the **tenant's account** is not a surrender.
- **Abandonment**: tenant vacates before the end of term without the landlord's acceptance. Traditional/minority rule: no duty to mitigate; the landlord may leave the premises vacant and sue for rent as it comes due. **Modern majority rule: landlord must use reasonable efforts to re-let (mitigate)**, and recovers rent less what reasonable efforts would have produced (Sommer v. Kridel, 74 N.J. 446 (1977); Restatement (Second) of Property: Landlord and Tenant § 12.1(3)). Many states apply the duty to residential and commercial leases; some limit it to residential **[UNCERTAIN count]**. A lease-clause for liquidated damages is evaluated on reasonableness.
- **Anticipatory breach**: tenant's unequivocal repudiation before performance is due is a breach; the landlord may sue or treat as abandonment (and mitigates) **[doctrine varies; leases historically treated as conveyances]**.
- **Holdover**: see tenancy at sufferance.

*Habitability and suitability*
- **Implied warranty of habitability (residential)**: majority rule; the landlord impliedly warrants that the dwelling is fit for human habitation (measured by housing codes, health and safety, essential services) for the whole term; **not waivable** by lease clause in most states. Breach is a defense to a nonpayment action and supports rent **abatement/withholding**, **repair and deduct** (usually statutory), **termination**, and damages. Javins v. First National Realty Corp., 428 F.2d 1071 (D.C. Cir. 1970) (covenants dependent; housing code standard); Hilder v. St. Peter, 478 A.2d 202 (Vt. 1984); Green v. Superior Court, 10 Cal.3d 616 (1974) **[case citations from memory; VERIFY before putting in an item's `src`]**.
- Tenant must usually give the landlord notice and a reasonable time to repair; defects the tenant caused do not breach. **Ordinary wear and tear** is not the tenant's responsibility.
- **Commercial leases**: tenant generally takes the premises "as is" and bears repairs, but a growing line implies a warranty of **suitability** for the intended commercial use (Davidow v. Inwood North Professional Group, 747 S.W.2d 373 (Tex. 1988)) **[low frequency; Texas case from memory, VERIFY]**.
- **Retaliatory eviction**: landlord may not evict, raise rent or reduce services because the tenant complained to authorities, joined a tenants' organization, or exercised legal rights (statutes in most states; Edwards v. Habib, 397 F.2d 687 (D.C. Cir. 1968) at common law). The landlord's defense: a legitimate reason (nonpayment, etc.).

**Traps**: oral long lease; holdover plus rent acceptance; assignment vs sublease; "original tenant is released by the assignment" (false); "sublessee is liable to the landlord" (false); constructive eviction requires vacating; self-help is not allowed; mitigation applies in the modern majority; waiver of habitability in the lease is ineffective.

**One-breath lines**
- "Assign the whole term and you stay on the hook; sublease and the new tenant answers only to you."
- "Stay and the heat is out? Sue for habitability. Leave and the heat is out? Constructive eviction."
- "The landlord can't lock you out, even for unpaid rent."

**Fact patterns**

**P5-1 [MBE-style].** Landlord orally agrees to lease an apartment to Tenant for three years at $1,000 per month. Tenant moves in and pays rent monthly. The Statute of Frauds requires a writing for leases longer than one year. After four months Landlord, wanting the apartment for a relative, tells Tenant to leave within 10 days. Which is the best statement?
(A) Tenant must leave, because the lease is void. (B) Tenant has a month-to-month periodic tenancy and is entitled to a month's notice ending at the end of a period. (C) Tenant has a three-year lease enforceable by part performance in every state. (D) Tenant is a tenant at sufferance.
**Best answer: B.** An oral lease within the statute creates a periodic tenancy measured by the rent period; termination needs a full period's notice. Wrong: A, "void" overstates; C, part-performance enforcement is a minority; D, Tenant is in possession with consent.

**P5-2 [MBE-style].** A one-year lease ends on June 30. Tenant stays and on July 5 mails rent at the old rate. Landlord cashes the check and says nothing. On July 20, Landlord serves a notice to quit effective immediately, claiming Tenant is a holdover trespasser. The best result is
(A) Landlord wins; a holdover is always a trespasser. (B) Landlord's acceptance of rent after the term was an election to hold Tenant to a new periodic tenancy, so Tenant may not be evicted without notice ending a period. (C) Landlord wins only if the original lease prohibited holdover. (D) Tenant has a fee simple.
**Best answer: B.** The landlord may elect; acceptance of rent is an election for a periodic tenancy. Wrong: A, ignores election; C, the clause does not override the election; D, absurd.

**P5-3 [MBE-style].** Landlord leases a house to Tenant beginning September 1. On that date the prior tenant, Holdover, is still in possession and refuses to leave. Landlord tells Tenant to evict Holdover himself. In a jurisdiction that follows the American rule, which is correct?
(A) Tenant may terminate the lease and recover damages from Landlord, because Landlord must deliver actual possession. (B) Tenant must sue Holdover, and has no claim against Landlord. (C) Tenant owes rent from September 1. (D) The lease is void for failure of consideration.
**Best answer: A.** Under the American rule the landlord must put the tenant in actual possession. Wrong: B and C, the English rule; D, the lease is valid and gives remedies.

**P5-4 [MBE-style].** In a jurisdiction that recognizes an implied warranty of habitability, Landlord leases an apartment to Tenant for a year. In January the heating system fails, in violation of the housing code. Tenant gives notice. Landlord does nothing for two months. Tenant stays and stops paying rent. Landlord sues for the unpaid rent. Tenant's best defense is
(A) constructive eviction. (B) breach of the implied warranty of habitability, which permits abatement of rent. (C) the Statute of Frauds. (D) Landlord's failure to record the lease.
**Best answer: B.** Habitability is dependent on rent; a tenant may remain and seek abatement. Wrong: A, a tenant who stays cannot claim constructive eviction; C and D, irrelevant.

**P5-5 [MBE-style].** Landlord leases Blackacre to Tess for five years. After two years, with Landlord's written consent, Tess assigns the remaining term to Ann. Ann promises Tess (but not Landlord) to pay the rent. Ann then defaults. From whom may Landlord recover?
(A) Ann only. (B) Tess only. (C) Ann or Tess. (D) Neither, because Landlord consented.
**Best answer: C.** Ann is liable through privity of estate; Tess stays liable by privity of contract unless Landlord released her. Wrong: A and B, each ignores one privity; D, consent to an assignment does not release the assignor.

**P5-6 [MBE-style].** Landlord leases Blackacre to Tess for five years. After two years Tess subleases it to Sam for one year, then Sam stops paying rent. Landlord sues Sam directly for the rent due under the original lease. The best result is
(A) Landlord wins, because Sam possesses the land. (B) Landlord wins, because Sam is in privity of estate. (C) Landlord loses; Sam is not in privity with Landlord and Landlord's remedy is against Tess. (D) Landlord wins as a third-party beneficiary of the sublease.
**Best answer: C.** A sublessee has no privity with the landlord (absent assumption of the head lease). Wrong: A, possession alone does not create liability; B, Sam holds only a sub-estate; D, the rent is owed to Tess, not Landlord.

**P5-7 [MBE-style].** Tenant has a 12-month residential lease at $1,000 per month. After six months Tenant leaves without notice. Landlord makes no effort to re-let and sues for the remaining $6,000 at the end of the term. The jurisdiction follows the majority rule on mitigation. Landlord will recover
(A) $6,000. (B) $6,000 less the amount Landlord could have obtained with reasonable efforts to re-let. (C) nothing, because Landlord accepted a surrender by inaction. (D) only the security deposit.
**Best answer: B.** Modern majority imposes a duty to mitigate. Wrong: A, minority rule; C, surrender requires acceptance or acts inconsistent with the tenancy; D, deposit is not a cap.

**P5-8 [MBE-style].** Tenant, after six months of a one-year lease, tells Landlord she is moving out and hands over her keys. Landlord accepts them, moves his own furniture into the apartment, and uses it for storage for the remaining six months. Tenant stops paying. Landlord sues for the unpaid rent. The best result is
(A) Landlord wins; a lease cannot be ended by agreement. (B) Landlord loses; he accepted a surrender by acts inconsistent with the continuing tenancy. (C) Landlord wins, less the amount he could have obtained by re-letting. (D) Landlord wins because Tenant gave no written notice.
**Best answer: B.** Surrender by operation of law ended future rent liability. Wrong: A, surrender is a recognized mode; C, mitigation applies when the lease continues, not when it was surrendered; D, acceptance can be shown by conduct, so a formal written notice is not the test.

**P5-9 [MBE-style].** Tenant is two months behind in rent on an apartment under a month-to-month tenancy. While Tenant is at work, Landlord changes the locks and places Tenant's belongings outside. Tenant sues. In a majority jurisdiction, Tenant will
(A) lose, because rent and possession are independent. (B) win, because Landlord must use judicial eviction process, even though Tenant breached. (C) lose, because the self-help was peaceable. (D) win only if Tenant owes no rent.
**Best answer: B.** Self-help is not permitted in the majority view. Wrong: A and C, minority/old rule; D, Tenant's default does not authorize self-help.

**P5-10 [MBE-style].** A tenant in a month-to-month tenancy reports housing code violations to the city. Three days later the Landlord serves a 30-day notice terminating the tenancy, with no reason given. The jurisdiction has a statute prohibiting retaliatory evictions. Which is Tenant's best argument?
(A) The notice is ineffective because month-to-month tenancies cannot be terminated. (B) The termination is presumptively retaliatory and unenforceable unless Landlord shows a legitimate reason. (C) The warranty of habitability prevents any termination. (D) Landlord must pay moving expenses.
**Best answer: B.** The timing supports retaliation. Wrong: A, periodic tenancies end on notice; C, habitability does not prohibit termination; D, no such rule.

**P5-11 [MBE-style].** A commercial lease says Tenant "shall not assign the lease without Landlord's written consent." The lease is silent on reasonableness. Tenant proposes an assignee with excellent credit. Landlord refuses because it wants to raise the rent for a new tenant. In a jurisdiction applying the traditional rule, Tenant
(A) may assign anyway. (B) may not assign without consent; Landlord may refuse for any reason. (C) may terminate the lease. (D) may sue for damages for bad-faith refusal.
**Best answer: B.** Traditional rule: absent a reasonableness term, the landlord may refuse. Wrong: A, would breach the covenant; C, no right of termination; D, minority (Kendall) imposes reasonableness.

### MODULE 6. Alienability, descendibility, devisability; restraints; options and rights of first refusal (I.D.2)

**Rules**
- Present and future interests are generally **alienable, descendible and devisable**; exceptions: interests that end at the holder's death (life estate for the holder's own life; contingent interests that depend on survival), and unascertained takers.
- **Restraints on alienation**: **total (absolute) restraints on a fee simple are void**: disabling restraint (any attempt to convey is void), forfeiture restraint (conveyance causes forfeiture), promissory restraint (A promises not to convey). **Partial restraints** (limited in time, limited as to class of buyers, or requiring consent not unreasonably withheld) are upheld if **reasonable** in purpose and duration. Restraints on use ("residential use only") are tested as covenants, not as restraints on alienation.
- **Right of first refusal / preemptive right**: right to buy on the same terms if the owner receives a bona fide offer; **valid** if reasonable in price and time. Options are enforceable if supported by consideration and exercised strictly according to terms (time of the essence; acceptance must be unqualified). Rule against perpetuities may apply to options in gross and, in the majority, to ROFRs (Module 3).
- **Due-on-sale clauses** in mortgages are restraints on alienation of the mortgaged property; they are **enforceable** (Garn-St Germain Act, 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(b)) subject to residential exceptions (Module 21).
- **Restraints on marriage / religion**: total restraints void; partial reasonable restraints valid (mostly Wills/Trusts).
- NextGen: "total vs. partial restraints, reasonableness of restraints, restraints on future interests, and options and rights of first refusal" (does not include RAP).

**Majority/minority**: treatment of restraints on life estates and future interests (some states apply the same reasonableness test as to fees; others are more tolerant) **[UNCERTAIN]**.

**Traps**: promissory/forfeiture/disabling all fail when total; a "consent not to be unreasonably withheld" clause is a partial restraint; "right of first refusal" at a fixed price is more suspect than at the offered price.

**Fact patterns**

**P6-1 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana in fee simple, on the condition that she never convey it to anyone." Ana conveys to Ben. Who owns Blackacre?
(A) Owner, who may reenter. (B) Ben, because the restraint is void. (C) Ana, because the conveyance to Ben is void. (D) Owner, by possibility of reverter.
**Best answer: B.** An absolute restraint on a fee simple is void; Ana holds a fee simple absolute and may convey. Wrong: A and D, the condition is struck; C, a disabling restraint is also void.

**P6-2 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana in fee simple; if Ana decides to sell, she must first offer it to Owner at the same price and terms offered by a bona fide purchaser, within 30 days." Ana receives a bona fide offer and ignores Owner's right. Which is the best statement?
(A) The right of first refusal is void as an unreasonable restraint. (B) The right of first refusal is a reasonable partial restraint and is enforceable. (C) Owner has a possibility of reverter. (D) Ana's conveyance is valid and Owner has no remedy.
**Best answer: B.** The price and time are reasonable, so the restraint is valid. Wrong: A, only unreasonable restraints fail; C, no reverter language; D, equity would enforce the right of first refusal.

**P6-3 [MBE-style].** A deed states: "to Ana in fee simple, provided that the land shall be used only for single-family residential purposes." The condition is
(A) a total restraint on alienation and void. (B) a valid restriction on use, not a restraint on alienation. (C) void because uses cannot be restricted. (D) a right of first refusal.
**Best answer: B.** It restricts use; courts test reasonableness and enforcement as a condition or covenant. Wrong: A, C, D.

### MODULE 7. Fair housing / discrimination (I.D.3)

**Rules**
- **Fair Housing Act (FHA), 42 U.S.C. §§ 3601-3619** (Title VIII, 1968; amended 1988 to add familial status and handicap). Protected classes: **race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, handicap (disability)**. § 3604: (a) refusing to sell/rent/negotiate or otherwise making unavailable; (b) discriminating in terms, conditions, privileges, services; **(c) discriminatory notices, statements or advertisements**; (d) falsely denying availability; (e) blockbusting; (f) disability: includes refusal to permit **reasonable modifications** at the disabled person's expense and refusal to make **reasonable accommodations** in rules, policies, practices, or services, and design/construction requirements for covered new multifamily housing. § 3617: coercion, intimidation, threats, or interference (also retaliation). Verified text for §§ 3603 and 3604: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/3603 and https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/3604
- **Exemptions (§ 3603(b))**: (1) **single-family house sold or rented by an owner who owns no more than three such houses**, no use of a broker/agent, no discriminatory ad (§ 3604(c) still applies); for sale by an owner not the most recent resident, only one sale in any 24 months; (2) **owner-occupied building of no more than four units** ("Mrs. Murphy"); these exemptions apply to § 3604 (except (c)). Religious organizations and private clubs: § 3607 (limited preference for members, but not on the basis of race). Housing for older persons: § 3607(b). **Verify § 3607 text before writing items**: the fetch did not return it.
- **42 U.S.C. § 1982** (Civil Rights Act of 1866): "all citizens ... the same right ... to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property" bars **racial** discrimination, private as well as public, with **no exemptions**; Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co., 392 U.S. 409 (1968).
- **Shelley v. Kraemer**, 334 U.S. 1 (1948): judicial enforcement of racially restrictive covenants is state action violating the Fourteenth Amendment (private covenants themselves are not illegal, but cannot be enforced by courts); the FHA makes discriminatory covenants and advertising unlawful.
- **Disparate impact** is cognizable under the FHA: Texas Dept. of Housing & Community Affairs v. Inclusive Communities Project, Inc., 576 U.S. 519 (2015). **[Executive-branch enforcement policy on disparate impact has changed in 2025-26; the legal holding of the Supreme Court is unchanged; do not teach agency policy.]**
- **Limitations**: private action within 2 years of the discriminatory act (42 U.S.C. § 3613(a)(1)(A)); HUD administrative complaint within 1 year (§ 3610(a)(1)(A)(i)) **[VERIFY]**.
- Retaliatory eviction (see Module 5). Occupancy limits (reasonable) are not familial status discrimination.

**Traps**: owner-occupied small building exempt from FHA but not from § 1982 for race; use of a broker voids the single-family exemption; § 3604(c) advertising rule has no exemptions; "reasonable accommodation" vs "reasonable modification": the landlord must permit the modification at the tenant's cost, and must make accommodations in rules at the landlord's cost unless undue burden; refusal to allow an assistance animal despite a "no pets" rule is typically a violation.

**Fact patterns**

**P7-1 [MBE-style].** Owner lives in one unit of a three-unit building and refuses to rent another unit to Dana solely because Dana is Black. Dana sues under every applicable federal statute. Which claim will succeed?
(A) The Fair Housing Act, because race is protected. (B) 42 U.S.C. § 1982, because it prohibits racial discrimination in leasing and has no owner-occupied exemption. (C) Neither, because Owner qualifies for the owner-occupied exemption. (D) Only a state-law claim.
**Best answer: B.** Owner's building qualifies for the FHA's four-unit owner-occupied exemption (so A fails), but § 1982 bars racial discrimination in all rentals. Wrong: A, exempt; C, ignores § 1982; D, federal law applies.

**P7-2 [MBE-style].** A homeowner who owns one single-family house advertises it for sale in a newspaper: "Perfect for professionals. No children." The sale is made without a broker. Is the ad lawful under the FHA?
(A) Yes; the single-family owner exemption covers the sale. (B) No; the exemption does not protect discriminatory advertisements. (C) Yes; familial status is not protected. (D) No, but only if a broker prepared the ad.
**Best answer: B.** § 3603(b)(1) exempts such an owner from § 3604 (a), (b), (d)-(f) but not from § 3604(c), and familial status is protected. Wrong: A, exemption does not cover ads; C, familial status is protected; D, brokers are irrelevant to the ad rule.

**P7-3 [MBE-style].** A 30-unit apartment complex has a no-pets policy. A tenant with a disability asks to keep a trained assistance dog. The landlord refuses. Under the FHA the refusal is
(A) lawful, because the policy is neutral. (B) unlawful, if the accommodation is reasonable and necessary to afford equal use and enjoyment. (C) lawful, because landlords may charge a pet deposit. (D) lawful, because only physical modifications are covered.
**Best answer: B.** § 3604(f)(3)(B) requires reasonable accommodations in rules and policies. Wrong: A, neutrality does not excuse; C, an assistance animal is not a pet for these purposes; D, accommodations in rules are covered.

**P7-4 [MBE-style].** Owner owns two single-family houses and sells one through a licensed real estate broker. Owner instructs the broker not to show it to families with children. A couple with two children is refused a showing and sues under the FHA. Result?
(A) Owner wins; the single-family exemption applies. (B) Couple wins; use of a broker defeats the single-family exemption. (C) Owner wins because familial status is not protected. (D) Couple loses because they have not bought.
**Best answer: B.** The exemption requires no broker; Owner is liable under § 3604(a), (b). Wrong: A, exemption lost; C, protected; D, refusing to show is itself actionable.

### MODULE 8. Conflicts of law in real property disputes (I.D.4)

**Rules**
- The law of the **situs** (the state where the land lies) governs questions about title, interests in land, and the validity and effect of conveyances, mortgages, and deeds (as to the interests created), and, in the standard approach, what methods of transfer will be recognized. Contract and personal obligation questions (contract to sell, covenants, mortgage notes) may be governed by other states' law under choice-of-law rules; courts often apply the situs's law to formalities.
- A court in State X **cannot directly transfer title** to land in State Y by its own decree; State Y need not give full faith and credit to a decree that purports to operate in rem on foreign land. But the court with personal jurisdiction over the parties can **order a party to execute a deed** (in personam), enforceable by contempt, and the deed will be recognized in the situs state. Fall v. Eastin, 215 U.S. 1 (1909).
- Tax, recording, and statute-of-limitations questions follow situs law. 
- Frequency on the MBE: low. Learn the situs rule and the in personam trick. **[low confidence about frequency; no official data]**

**Fact pattern**

**P8-1 [MBE-style].** A State X divorce court that has personal jurisdiction over Husband decrees that Husband's land in State Y "is hereby vested in Wife." Wife presents the decree to the State Y recorder and claims title. Which statement is most accurate?
(A) State Y must give the decree full faith and credit, so title passed directly to Wife. (B) The State X decree does not by itself pass title to the State Y land, but the State X court may order Husband to execute a deed, enforceable by contempt. (C) The decree is void for all purposes. (D) State X law governs who owns land in State Y.
**Best answer: B.** The law of the situs governs title to land and a foreign court cannot directly convey it, but a court with personal jurisdiction can compel the owner to convey (Fall v. Eastin, 215 U.S. 1 (1909)). Wrong: A, full faith and credit does not extend to an in rem transfer of foreign land; C, the decree is valid as an order against Husband personally; D, situs law governs.

## Category II. Rights in real property

### MODULE 9. Easements, profits, licenses (II.B)

NCBE MBE anchors: nature and type; methods of creation (express, implied, prescription); scope and apportionment; transfer; termination. NextGen adds: easement implied from subdivision plat, easement by estoppel, license created by failing to create an easement, irrevocable licenses, duties to repair, effect of subdivision of the dominant estate.

**Rules**

*Nature and types*
- **Easement** = nonpossessory right to use another's land. **Affirmative** (a right to do something: cross, run a pipe) vs **negative** (a right to stop the servient owner from doing something; traditionally only light, air, support, and flow of an artificial stream; anything else is a covenant/servitude). **Appurtenant** (benefits the dominant parcel; passes automatically with it) vs **in gross** (benefits a person; commercial utility easements are assignable; personal ones traditionally are not). **Presumption of appurtenant** when ambiguous (judicial preference).
- **Profit à prendre**: right to enter and take something from the land (timber, minerals, game); treated like an easement for creation and termination.
- **License**: permission to enter; personal, **revocable at will**, not an interest in land; terminates on the licensor's death or conveyance; need not be in writing. Becomes **irrevocable** when (a) the licensee has relied by making substantial expenditures (estoppel, "easement by estoppel") or (b) the license is coupled with an interest (e.g., right to enter and remove purchased chattels).

*Creation*
- **Express**: grant or reservation in a writing that satisfies the Statute of Frauds (signed, identifies land and parties, shows intent). A reservation in favor of a stranger to the deed was invalid at common law; modern law generally allows it.
- **Implied from prior use ("quasi-easement")**: (1) common ownership then **severance**, (2) before severance, the owner used one part for the benefit of the other in an **apparent and continuous** way, (3) the use is **reasonably necessary** to the enjoyment of the benefited part. Implied **grant** (sold parcel needs the use) is easily implied; many courts require greater (strict) necessity for an implied **reservation** (seller keeps a use over the sold parcel). The Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 2.12 treats grant and reservation similarly (a minority/modern position) **[VERIFY exact section text]**.
- **Implied by necessity**: common ownership then severance, and **strict necessity** (e.g., landlocked parcel); no prior use required; the servient owner may choose a reasonable location; lasts only as long as the necessity (Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 2.15, "persuasive" per New Mexico court as relayed by search; some states treat necessity differently).
- **Implied from a plat or map**: lots sold by reference to a recorded plat showing streets, parks etc. give buyers easements in those areas.
- **By estoppel**: owner orally permits a use, the user spends substantial money in reliance; the permission becomes irrevocable for as long as reasonably necessary to avoid injustice.
- **Prescription**: use of another's land that is **open and notorious, adverse (without owner's permission), continuous, for the statutory period**. **Exclusivity is not required** (unlike adverse possession). Tacking allowed with privity. No prescription against government land. **Permissive use is not adverse**: if use began with permission, prescription requires a clear repudiation. Traditional law denies prescriptive negative easements ("ancient lights").

*Scope and apportionment*
- **Express easement**: interpreted by the parties' intent; if silent, the use that is **reasonably necessary** and fits the **normal development** of the dominant estate. Easements implied by necessity cover only what the necessity requires.
- **Overburdening/misuse**: a use outside the scope (for example, using an easement appurtenant to Lot A to reach Lot C) is enjoinable; the easement is **not terminated** merely by misuse. An easement appurtenant cannot be used to benefit non-dominant land.
- **Subdivision of the dominant estate**: each resulting lot may use the easement (apportionment) if no additional burden results (majority).
- **Maintenance and repair**: the holder bears the duty to repair what is needed for use; the servient owner has no duty to maintain absent agreement (and may be liable for interfering); shared use is apportioned by use (Restatement approach).
- **Relocation**: majority: the servient owner may not unilaterally relocate a fixed easement; Restatement (Third) § 4.8 allows relocation if reasonable and no loss of utility (Vermont adopted; Washington has not, per search) **[VERIFY]**.

*Transfer*
- **Appurtenant**: benefit passes automatically with the dominant estate (even if the deed is silent); burden passes with the servient estate to a successor with **notice** (actual, record, or inquiry (e.g., visible use)) or, in recording-act terms, whose predecessor's easement was recorded. **In gross**: commercial assignable (majority); personal not (unless intent shows otherwise).

*Termination*
- **Release** in a writing (Statute of Frauds); **merger** (the same person owns both the dominant and servient estates in fee simple; mere common ownership of lesser estates may not merge); **abandonment** (intent to abandon plus physical acts showing it; **mere nonuse is not enough** for an express easement); **estoppel** (servient owner changes position in reasonable reliance on the holder's conduct); **prescription** (servient owner adversely obstructs for the statutory period); **end of necessity** (easement by necessity); **expiration** by its terms; **condemnation**; **destruction of the servient estate** (not by the servient owner's act); **recording act** (a BFP without notice of an unrecorded easement takes free).

**Traps**: a license is not an easement; an easement by prescription does not need exclusivity; misuse is enjoined, not terminated; nonuse alone doesn't terminate; necessity ends when necessity ends; implied reservation is harder than implied grant; "appurtenant" passes with the land without being mentioned.

**One-breath lines**
- "Necessity needs a landlocked parcel and a common owner; prior use needs a visible, continuous path."
- "Permission kills prescription."
- "Nonuse is not abandonment; abandonment needs intent."

**Fact patterns**

**P9-1 [MBE-style].** Owner owns a 20-acre tract fronting on a public road. Owner conveys the back 10 acres to Ana by a deed that is silent on access. The back parcel has no access to any road except across Owner's retained land. Ana demands to cross Owner's land to reach the road. Ana will probably
(A) lose; the deed grants no access. (B) prevail; an easement by necessity was implied on the severance. (C) prevail only if Owner ever used the path before conveying. (D) prevail as a licensee, subject to revocation.
**Best answer: B.** Common ownership, severance and strict necessity (landlocked). Wrong: A, necessity implies the easement; C, prior use is a different theory; D, an implied easement is not revocable.

**P9-2 [MBE-style].** Owner owns two adjoining lots. A paved driveway across Lot 2 has long been used, visibly and continuously, as the only practical way to reach the garage on Lot 1. Owner sells Lot 1 to Ana by a deed that does not mention the driveway. Later Owner's successor on Lot 2 blocks the driveway. Ana will most likely
(A) lose; no easement was granted in writing. (B) prevail; an easement was implied from prior use. (C) prevail; she has a prescriptive easement. (D) lose; easements by implication require strict necessity.
**Best answer: B.** Prior apparent, continuous use with reasonable necessity, implied on severance in favor of the buyer. Wrong: A, implication does not require writing; C, no prescriptive period elapsed; D, strict necessity is for easements by necessity; prior-use requires reasonable necessity.

**P9-3 [MBE-style].** Owner retains the front lot (which has road frontage) and sells the rear lot to Ben. Before the sale Owner regularly walked a shortcut path across the rear lot to a side street. The deed to Ben is silent. After the sale, Ben blocks the path. Owner sues for an implied easement. In a jurisdiction that applies the traditional rule for implied reservations, Owner will
(A) prevail, because the use was apparent and continuous. (B) lose, because an implied reservation requires strict necessity and Owner's lot has road access. (C) prevail, because Owner kept the path in use. (D) lose, because easements in gross cannot be reserved.
**Best answer: B.** Implied reservation is disfavored and requires strict necessity in the traditional/majority rule; Owner's land has frontage. Wrong: A and C, reasonable necessity suffices only for an implied grant (and Restatement view is a minority); D, irrelevant.

**P9-4 [MBE-style].** Neighbor has openly used a path across Owner's land for 30 years (statutory period: 20 years). In the first year Owner told Neighbor, "You can use the path anytime." Owner has never revoked the permission. Does Neighbor have a prescriptive easement?
(A) Yes, because the use was open, continuous and for more than 20 years. (B) No, because the use began with permission and was never adverse. (C) Yes, because Neighbor has paid property taxes. (D) No, because prescriptive easements cannot exist in real property.
**Best answer: B.** Permission defeats the adversity element. Wrong: A, adversity is missing; C, tax payment is not an element of prescription; D, prescriptive easements exist.

**P9-5 [MBE-style].** Ana owns Lot A, which has an express, recorded easement appurtenant over Ben's Lot B for access to Lot A. Ana buys adjoining Lot C and begins using the easement to reach Lot C as well. Which is Ben's best remedy?
(A) Terminate the easement. (B) Enjoin the use of the easement for the benefit of Lot C; the easement continues for Lot A. (C) Recover no remedy, because the easement is express. (D) Treat Ana as a trespasser on all of Lot B.
**Best answer: B.** Using an appurtenant easement for non-dominant land is outside the scope; the remedy is an injunction (and damages). Wrong: A, misuse does not terminate; C, scope limits apply to express easements; D, Ana retains the easement for Lot A.

**P9-6 [MBE-style].** Ana holds an express easement over Ben's land for a driveway to her house. Ana stops using the driveway for 40 years but never says or does anything showing intent to give up the easement; Ben has not obstructed it. The prescriptive/adverse possession period is 20 years. Ben now builds a fence across the driveway. The easement is
(A) terminated by nonuse alone. (B) terminated by abandonment. (C) still valid; mere nonuse does not terminate an express easement. (D) terminated by merger.
**Best answer: C.** Abandonment requires intent plus conduct; nonuse is not enough. Wrong: A and B, no intent to abandon; D, no unity of title.

**P9-7 [MBE-style].** Ben orally tells Ana she may build a driveway across his land to her property. Ana spends $15,000 building it. Two years later Ben revokes the permission. Which is correct?
(A) Ben may revoke because licenses are revocable at will. (B) The oral permission became irrevocable because Ana made substantial expenditures in reliance. (C) Ana has a prescriptive easement immediately. (D) The permission is void under the Statute of Frauds, so Ana can sue for the $15,000 only.
**Best answer: B.** An oral license relied on becomes irrevocable (easement by estoppel). Wrong: A, ignores estoppel; C, no prescriptive period; D, the Statute of Frauds does not stop estoppel.

### MODULE 10. Restrictive covenants, equitable servitudes, owners' associations (II.A)

NCBE MBE anchors: nature and type; creation; scope; transfer; termination; **property owners' associations and common interest ownership communities**. NextGen adds equitable servitude defenses (unclean hands, acquiescence, estoppel, changed neighborhood conditions) and servitude implied from a common scheme.

**Rules**
- **Real covenant** (at law; remedy **damages**). To **bind a successor to the burden**: (1) writing that satisfies the Statute of Frauds, (2) intent that the burden run, (3) **touch and concern** the land, (4) **horizontal privity** (the covenanting parties had a relationship at the time such as a simultaneous conveyance, landlord-tenant), (5) **vertical privity** (the successor takes the promisor's entire estate in the burdened land), (6) **notice** (actual, constructive, inquiry) to a successor purchaser. To **let a successor enforce the benefit**: writing, intent, touch and concern, and a **relaxed** vertical privity (the successor takes some estate in the benefited land); horizontal privity is not required for the benefit. Mnemonic in a commercial guide: "WITHVN" burden / "WITV" benefit (https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/mbe/real-property-rights-in-land/covenants-servitudes-communities) **[commercial, not authority; the elements match the CALI open textbook at https://turnerpropertytwo.lawbooks.cali.org/?p=22]**.
- **Equitable servitude** (in equity; remedy **injunction**): burden runs if (1) a writing (or an implied common scheme), (2) intent, (3) touch and concern, (4) **notice**. **No privity required** (neither horizontal nor vertical). Tulk v. Moxhay, 2 Ph. 774 (Ch. 1848).
- **Implied reciprocal servitude (common scheme)**: when a developer subdivides under a **general plan** and sells most lots with restrictions, the restrictions may be implied on lots whose deeds omit them, if buyers had **notice** (actual, inquiry from the uniform appearance of the neighborhood, or record) (Sanborn v. McLean, 233 Mich. 227, 206 N.W. 496 (1925) **[Mich. reporter cite from memory; the N.W. cite is from the CALI text; VERIFY]**).
- **Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes** eliminates horizontal privity and replaces "touch and concern" with a validity test based on illegality, unconstitutionality or public policy (§ 3.1) and fewer privity rules. Many jurisdictions still apply traditional law. **MBE answers follow the traditional rule unless the question says the Restatement is adopted.** **[VERIFY exact § numbers: CALI summary did not give them; search summaries conflict on whether touch-and-concern is "dropped" or "re-labelled as a policy filter".]**
- **Affirmative covenants** (to pay money, maintain) can run if they touch and concern and meet privity rules; assessments for common areas run (Neponsit Property Owners' Ass'n v. Emigrant Industrial Savings Bank, 278 N.Y. 248 (1938)) **[citation from memory; VERIFY]**.
- **Remedies**: damages (covenant) or injunction (servitude); equitable defenses: **unclean hands**, **laches**, **acquiescence** (widespread tolerated violations), **estoppel**, **changed neighborhood conditions** (only if the change is so radical that the restriction's purpose can no longer be achieved for the subdivision as a whole).
- **Termination**: release; merger; abandonment or acquiescence; changed conditions; condemnation (of the burdened land); expiration by terms; marketable record title acts in some states; failure of recording (BFP without notice).
- **Common interest communities (CICs)**: **condominium** (fee title to unit plus undivided share of common elements), **cooperative** (shares + proprietary lease), **planned community** (fee in lot; association owns common areas). Declaration recorded; association enforces covenants and levies assessments (a lien on the unit; priority statutes vary; UCIOA gives limited priority). Courts presume recorded restrictions valid: Nahrstedt v. Lakeside Village Condominium Ass'n, 8 Cal.4th 361 (1994) (presumption of validity for recorded restrictions, unless arbitrary, burdens outweigh benefits, or violate public policy) **[citation from memory and from CALI list; VERIFY]**; board decisions get deference. Federal limits: Fair Housing Act reasonable accommodation; racially restrictive covenants unenforceable (Shelley).

**Traps**: real covenant vs servitude is decided by the remedy sought; horizontal privity applies only to the burden of a real covenant; a tenant doesn't have the whole estate and is not strictly bound at law; "touch and concern" not met by purely personal promises; changed conditions rarely succeed; an association need not own benefited land to enforce assessments.

**One-breath lines**
- "Damages? Privity. Injunction? Notice."
- "Common scheme plus notice binds the lot whose deed forgot."

**Fact patterns**

**P10-1 [MBE-style].** Developer sells all 30 lots in Sunrise Estates by deeds that restrict each lot to "single-family residential use for the benefit of all other lots." Ana buys Lot 5. Ben buys Lot 6 from Developer, then sells to Cal without mentioning the restriction; the restriction is in the recorded deed to Ben. Cal opens a retail store on Lot 6. Ana seeks an injunction. She will
(A) lose; Cal is not in privity with Ana. (B) lose; the restriction was not in Cal's deed. (C) prevail; the restriction runs in equity to Cal, who had record notice. (D) prevail only if Ana seeks damages.
**Best answer: C.** Equitable servitude: writing, intent, touch and concern, notice; no privity needed. Wrong: A, privity is not required in equity; B, record notice suffices; D, damages require covenant privity, and the injunction is the equitable remedy.

**P10-2 [MBE-style].** Ana and her neighbor Ben, in a signed writing that is not part of any conveyance, agree that neither "nor their successors" will build above one story. Ben later sells to Cal, who builds a two-story house. Ana sues Cal for money damages. Cal had notice of the agreement. Ana will
(A) prevail, because Cal had notice. (B) lose; the burden of a real covenant does not run without horizontal privity. (C) prevail, because the covenant touches and concerns the land. (D) lose, because covenants never run to successors.
**Best answer: B.** Damages require the real-covenant elements, including horizontal privity, which a stand-alone neighbor agreement lacks (Ana might still seek an injunction as an equitable servitude). Wrong: A and C, necessary but not sufficient at law; D, covenants can run.

**P10-3 [MBE-style].** Developer records a plat for a 50-lot subdivision and sells 40 lots with deeds limiting use to residences. The deed to Lot 41, sold to Ana, omits the restriction, but the neighborhood is uniformly residential and the plat is recorded. Ana plans a convenience store. A neighbor with a restricted lot sues to enjoin. The neighbor will most likely
(A) lose; Ana's deed has no restriction. (B) prevail; an implied reciprocal servitude arises from the common scheme, of which Ana had notice. (C) lose; implied covenants do not exist. (D) prevail only for damages.
**Best answer: B.** Common scheme, restrictions on most lots, and notice (inquiry or record). Wrong: A, omission is not decisive; C, equity implies servitudes; D, injunction is the remedy.

**P10-4 [MBE-style].** Restrictions in a residential subdivision prohibit commercial use. A busy commercial strip has grown up along a highway at the edge of the subdivision. Ben, who owns a lot at the edge, plans a store. The interior of the subdivision remains entirely residential. Ben argues changed conditions. A court will probably
(A) deny the injunction; changed conditions defeat any restriction. (B) enforce the restriction; the change has not defeated the purpose of the restriction for the subdivision as a whole. (C) deny the injunction because Ben's lot is less valuable for residential use. (D) allow damages only.
**Best answer: B.** The defense succeeds only if the restriction's purpose can no longer be achieved. Wrong: A and C, too lenient; D, no basis.

**P10-5 [MBE-style].** Ana buys a lot in a planned community subject to a recorded declaration requiring owners to pay assessments to a homeowners' association for maintaining roads and common areas. The association owns no lot in the community. Ana refuses to pay. The association sues. The association will
(A) lose; it owns no benefited land. (B) prevail; the covenant to pay assessments runs with the land and the association can enforce it. (C) lose; affirmative covenants never run. (D) prevail only if Ana personally signed the declaration.
**Best answer: B.** Recorded assessment covenants touch and concern the land and bind purchasers with notice; the association enforces for the owners. Wrong: A, an association can enforce; C, affirmative covenants may run; D, purchasers with notice are bound.

### MODULE 11. Fixtures (II.C)

**Rules**
- A **fixture** is personal property so attached to or used with the land that it becomes part of the real property. **Test (majority)**: (1) **annexation** (how firmly attached; harm on removal), (2) **adaptation** (customized for the use of the land), (3) **intent** of the person who annexed it, objectively judged (**most important**).
- Presumptions: items in the structure (furnace, plumbing, built-in cabinets, wall-to-wall carpet, installed lighting) are fixtures; freestanding furniture, rugs, appliances not hard-wired are chattels. If the buyer and seller disagree, a **sale of land** passes fixtures with the land unless excluded in the contract; a **mortgage** covers fixtures existing at execution and those later added.
- **Landlord-tenant**: **trade fixtures** installed by a tenant for a business may be **removed** by the tenant during the term (or a reasonable time after, as provided by the lease), but the tenant must repair damage; other attachments by the tenant become the landlord's. Failure to remove trade fixtures before the end of the lease generally forfeits them.
- **Secured creditors**: priority between a fixture security interest and the real-property mortgagee is governed by UCC Article 9 (§ 9-334: purchase-money security interest filed as a fixture filing quickly) **[outside the MBE detail; mention only]**.

**Traps**: intent is the key but is judged objectively; trade fixtures are removable only by tenants, and removal rights are lost if the tenant leaves without removing them.

**Fact patterns**

**P11-1 [MBE-style].** Seller contracts to sell a house to Buyer. The contract is silent about fixtures. Before closing, Seller removes custom-fitted built-in bookcases and the hard-wired chandelier. Buyer sues. Buyer will
(A) lose; the items were Seller's personal property. (B) prevail; the built-ins and hard-wired chandelier are fixtures that pass with the land. (C) prevail only for the chandelier. (D) lose because the contract was silent.
**Best answer: B.** Annexation, adaptation and objective intent point to fixtures. Wrong: A, mislabels; C, built-ins are also fixtures; D, silence leaves the default that fixtures pass.

**P11-2 [MBE-style].** Tenant leases a building for a bakery and bolts a large commercial oven to the floor. The lease is silent. Near the end of the term, Tenant removes the oven, repairs the floor, and returns the key. Landlord sues for the value of the oven. Landlord will
(A) prevail; anything bolted to the floor is a fixture. (B) lose; the oven is a trade fixture that Tenant could remove before the term ended if she repaired damage. (C) prevail because the lease was silent. (D) lose only if Landlord consented.
**Best answer: B.** Trade fixture doctrine. Wrong: A, ignores trade fixtures; C, silence defaults to the trade fixture rule; D, consent not required.

**P11-3 [MBE-style].** Same lease, but Tenant moves out at the end of the term leaving the oven. Six months later, Tenant returns and tries to remove it. Landlord refuses. Landlord will
(A) lose; Tenant may remove trade fixtures any time. (B) prevail; Tenant lost the right by failing to remove it before the end of the tenancy (or a reasonable time after). (C) lose because the oven is personal property. (D) prevail because Tenant breached the lease.
**Best answer: B.** The right to remove trade fixtures must be exercised within the term or a reasonable time. Wrong: A, no; C, it became realty; D, no breach.

### MODULE 12. Zoning (II.D) (fundamentals; regulatory takings are excluded from this outline and belong to Constitutional Law)

**Rules**
- **Euclidean zoning** is a valid exercise of the **police power** if reasonably related to public health, safety, morals or general welfare: Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., 272 U.S. 365 (1926). Zoning is a **legislative** act presumed valid; challengers must show the ordinance is arbitrary and unreasonable (or "not fairly debatable"). Zoning ordinances must conform with the state enabling act and (in many states) the comprehensive plan.
- **Constitutional limits** relevant to residential zoning: Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas, 416 U.S. 1 (1974) (upheld limit of two unrelated persons in single-family zone); Moore v. City of East Cleveland, 431 U.S. 494 (1977) (struck definition of "family" that excluded a grandmother living with two grandsons who were cousins); City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432 (1985) (special permit requirement for a group home for intellectually disabled persons failed rational basis review as applied). **[Cite these in `src` only after rereading; summary descriptions here are from memory and standard casebooks.]** Exclusionary zoning is limited in some states (e.g., Mount Laurel in New Jersey) **[not MBE-core]**.
- **Variance**: administrative relief from the zoning ordinance. **Area (dimensional) variance**: practical difficulties/unnecessary hardship; **use variance**: stricter, typically requires that the property cannot yield a reasonable return under permitted uses because of unique conditions not created by the owner, and the variance won't alter the essential character of the neighborhood. More profit is not hardship.
- **Special (conditional) exception/permit**: allowed in the zone if listed conditions are met (e.g., a church in a residential zone).
- **Rezoning/amendment**: legislative; **spot zoning** (arbitrary rezoning of a small parcel to benefit one owner, inconsistent with the comprehensive plan) is invalid in most states; rezoning is typically upheld if rationally related to the plan and public welfare. Contract zoning and conditional zoning raise issues in some states.
- **Nonconforming uses**: a lawful use existing before the zoning change may continue (**grandfathered**), but may **not be expanded**, and may be lost by **abandonment** (intent plus nonuse) or substantial destruction; some jurisdictions allow **amortization** (phase-out over a reasonable time). Changes of one nonconforming use to another are usually prohibited.
- **Protection of pre-existing property rights / vested rights**: an owner who obtains a valid building permit and, in good faith, makes substantial expenditures or construction in reliance, acquires a vested right to complete the project even if the zoning later changes (state-specific; the "building permit plus substantial reliance" test is typical).
- **Enforcement**: injunction, fines; neighbors who suffer special damage have standing; zoning violations also affect marketability (Module 16).

**Traps**: a use variance is harder to obtain than an area variance; nonconforming users cannot expand; a mere application or permit without reliance doesn't vest; zoning is presumptively valid; takings are not the issue.

**Fact patterns**

**P12-1 [MBE-style].** A gas station lawfully operated in 1990. In 2000 the city rezoned the area to residential only. The station continues. In 2026 the owner plans to double the building's size. The city denies a permit. Which is correct?
(A) The owner wins; the station is a valid nonconforming use that may expand. (B) The city wins; a nonconforming use may continue but may not be expanded. (C) The owner wins because a taking has occurred. (D) The city wins and may close the station immediately.
**Best answer: B.** Nonconforming uses are grandfathered but not expandable. Wrong: A, no right to expand; C, no taking; D, current use can continue unless abandoned or amortized.

**P12-2 [MBE-style].** Owner seeks a use variance to build a store in a residential zone. Owner shows that the store would be more profitable than a house, but the lot can be used for a house at a reasonable profit. The board should
(A) grant, because profit shows hardship. (B) deny, because owner has not shown that the land cannot yield a reasonable return in a permitted use. (C) grant, because an area variance standard applies. (D) deny, because zoning boards cannot grant variances.
**Best answer: B.** Use variances need unnecessary hardship in the strict sense. Wrong: A, more profit is not hardship; C, a use variance is not an area variance; D, boards can grant variances.

**P12-3 [MBE-style].** The city council, over objections, rezones a single one-acre parcel in the middle of a residential neighborhood to commercial, at the owner's request and in conflict with the comprehensive plan, with no benefit to the community. Neighbors sue. The rezoning will probably be
(A) upheld, because zoning is legislative and presumptively valid. (B) invalidated as spot zoning. (C) upheld because the owner's property rights outweigh the neighbors'. (D) invalidated only if the owner paid the council.
**Best answer: B.** Arbitrary rezoning of a small parcel for a private benefit, inconsistent with a plan, is spot zoning. Wrong: A, the presumption can be overcome; C and D, irrelevant.

**P12-4 [MBE-style].** The city issues Owner a valid building permit for a 12-unit apartment building. In good faith reliance, Owner pours the foundation and spends $400,000. The council then rezones the lot to single-family only. The city refuses to let Owner continue. Owner will
(A) lose; a permit creates no rights. (B) prevail; Owner acquired a vested right to complete the project. (C) lose because the new zoning applies to all property. (D) prevail only if the city acted in bad faith.
**Best answer: B.** Permit plus substantial good-faith reliance usually vests rights (state-specific). Wrong: A and C, ignore vested rights; D, bad faith is not required.

### MODULE 13. Water and support rights (NOT in the MBE outline; low priority; included because the assignment asked)

- **Lateral support**: a landowner is entitled to lateral support from adjoining land for her land in its **natural condition** (strict liability for excavation that removes support). For land **burdened by buildings**, liability for removing support is generally **negligence** (unless the land would have subsided even without the buildings). **Subjacent support**: surface owner is entitled to support from underlying minerals (mineral estate owner must leave support unless waived).

- **Watercourses**: **riparian** (East; owners of riparian land share reasonable use) vs **prior appropriation** (West; first in time, first in right; beneficial use).
- **Groundwater**: absolute ownership (English), reasonable use (American majority), correlative rights (California), prior appropriation (West).

## Category III. Real estate contracts

### MODULE 14. Real estate brokerage (III.A; MBE only)

**Rules**
- **Listing types**: **open** (broker earns only if she is the procuring cause / first to produce a qualifying buyer); **exclusive agency** (broker earns if anyone other than the owner sells during the term; owner may sell herself without paying); **exclusive right to sell** (broker earns if **anyone**, including the owner, sells during the term).
- **Commission earned** (majority): when the broker produces a buyer who is **ready, willing and able** to buy on the seller's terms (or on terms the seller accepts); the commission is then earned even if the **seller** wrongfully refuses to close. A minority and many listing forms require **closing** (the commission is "earned" at closing, "payable" from proceeds); if the **buyer** defaults without seller's fault, no commission under the closing rule.
- **Licensing statutes**: an unlicensed broker generally cannot recover a commission. **Statute of Frauds**: many states require a signed writing for the listing agreement (or the commission promise) **[state-specific]**.
- **Duties**: the broker is the seller's agent and fiduciary (loyalty, disclosure, obedience, accounting); **dual agency** requires informed consent of both principals; a broker with a conflict forfeits the commission; the broker must disclose known material defects to buyers (statutes, common law).
- Cooperating-broker and "procuring cause" disputes: facts will give the listing's terms.

**Traps**: the type of listing decides whether the owner's own sale triggers the commission; the "ready, willing and able" buyer standard; the seller cannot avoid the commission by refusing to close for no reason.

**Fact patterns**

**P14-1 [MBE-style].** Owner signs an exclusive right-to-sell listing with Broker. Broker finds Buyer, who signs a binding contract at the listed price with a deposit and is financially able to close. Owner then changes her mind and refuses to close. Broker sues for the commission. Broker will
(A) lose; commissions are earned only at closing. (B) prevail; she produced a ready, willing and able buyer and Owner's refusal does not defeat the commission. (C) lose unless Owner consents. (D) prevail only for her expenses.
**Best answer: B.** Majority rule: the commission is earned on production of a qualifying buyer. Wrong: A, the closing-condition rule is a minority/contract term; C and D, no.

**P14-2 [MBE-style].** Owner gives Broker an exclusive agency listing for 90 days. During the term, a neighbor who saw the sign mentioned a purchase to Owner directly, and Owner sells the house to the neighbor without any help from Broker. Broker demands a commission. Broker will
(A) prevail; every listing gives the broker a commission on any sale. (B) lose; under an exclusive agency listing the owner may sell without paying a commission. (C) prevail because the neighbor saw the broker's sign. (D) lose because exclusive agency listings are void.
**Best answer: B.** Exclusive agency bars only sales through other brokers; the owner's own sale is allowed. Wrong: A, that describes an exclusive right to sell; C, procuring cause does not apply; D, valid.

### MODULE 15. Creation and construction of the real estate contract (III.B)

**Rules**
- **Statute of Frauds**: a contract for the sale of an interest in land must be in a **writing signed by the party to be charged** (the defendant). The writing (memorandum) must identify the parties, the property (with a description that can be identified; a street address or "my farm in X County" is usually enough), and the essential terms (price, and in many states other material terms); it may be several documents; electronic signatures count. Agents' authority to sign: "equal dignities" in many states.
- **Exceptions**: (1) **part performance** (majority requires **possession** plus either **payment** (all or part) or **substantial improvements**; a payment alone is insufficient; a few states treat any two of the three as enough); (2) **estoppel/detrimental reliance** (unconscionable injury if enforcement is denied); (3) full performance by the seller (deed delivered) in some states; (4) admissions in pleadings or testimony in a minority of states. Part performance supports **specific performance** (equity), not damages at law in many states.
- **Essential terms**: parties, property, price and payment terms, and any agreed mortgage financing or contingency; courts will supply reasonable terms for gaps (time of closing: reasonable time) but not essential missing terms; an "agreement to agree" is not enforceable.
- **Time for performance**: In equity, **time is not of the essence** unless the contract says so ("time is of the essence"), the circumstances require it, or a party gives reasonable notice making it so after a delay. A party who is late can still get specific performance but is liable for damages caused by delay. A "time is of the essence" clause makes closing dates strict, and failure to tender at the date allows termination.
- **Remedies for breach**:
  - *Buyer breaches*: seller may sue for damages (contract price less market value at breach), seek specific performance, or keep a **reasonable liquidated deposit** (typically up to 10%); majority allows a defaulting buyer to recover the deposit in excess of actual damages if there is no valid liquidated damages clause **[UNCERTAIN: the Restatement (Third) of Restitution view is contested by some states, e.g., New Jersey; verify before an item]**.
  - *Seller breaches*: buyer may seek **specific performance** (land is unique), **damages** (typically benefit of the bargain; a minority follows the English rule limiting recovery to the deposit and expenses unless the seller acted in bad faith) **[UNCERTAIN count]**, or **rescission** with refund of the deposit and expenses. Specific performance with **abatement** (reduction of price) for minor title defects or small acreage shortfalls.
  - *Tender* is excused when the other party repudiates or cannot perform; **conditions concurrent**: delivery of deed and payment of price.
- **Contingencies**: financing, inspection, sale-of-home; the buyer must act in good faith to satisfy them.
- **Disclosure/representations** (also III.F): see Module 19.

**Traps**: part performance requires more than payment; "time is of the essence" is not presumed; the writing is signed only by the party to be charged; a missing price may be fatal but a missing closing date is not.

**Fact patterns**

**P15-1 [MBE-style].** Seller and Buyer sign a one-page document: "Seller agrees to sell and Buyer agrees to buy my farm at 12 County Road, Smith County, for $300,000, closing within 60 days." Seller refuses to close, arguing the writing lacks a legal description. Buyer sues for specific performance. Buyer will
(A) lose; a legal description is mandatory. (B) prevail; the description can be identified by extrinsic evidence and the writing is signed. (C) lose; only recorded writings qualify. (D) prevail only if a deposit was paid.
**Best answer: B.** The memorandum is sufficient if the property can be identified. Wrong: A, the Statute of Frauds does not require a metes-and-bounds description; C, recording isn't required; D, payment is not an element.

**P15-2 [MBE-style].** Seller orally agrees to sell Blackacre to Buyer for $200,000. Buyer pays a $10,000 deposit but does not take possession or improve the land. Seller refuses to convey. Buyer seeks specific performance. Under the majority view, Buyer will
(A) prevail; payment is part performance. (B) lose on specific performance because payment alone is not enough, but may recover the deposit in restitution. (C) prevail; oral contracts for land are enforceable if clear. (D) lose and forfeit the deposit.
**Best answer: B.** Payment alone does not take the contract out of the Statute of Frauds; restitution is available. Wrong: A, minority; C, oral contract barred; D, no forfeiture.

**P15-3 [MBE-style].** A written contract sets closing for June 1 and says nothing about timing being strict. Buyer, who is financially ready, tenders on June 8 after a short delay due to a lender error. Seller has not changed position, but refuses to close and sells to a third party. Buyer sues for specific performance. Buyer will probably
(A) lose; closing dates are always strict. (B) prevail; time is not of the essence absent an express clause or circumstances, and a brief delay is excused, although Seller may recover any damages caused by the delay. (C) lose because Buyer breached first. (D) prevail only if Seller consents to the delay.
**Best answer: B.** Equity does not presume time is of the essence. Wrong: A, the strict-time rule applies only if so provided; C, minor delay does not forfeit; D, consent not required.

**P15-4 [MBE-style].** Seller, without legal excuse, refuses to convey Blackacre after the parties sign a valid contract. Which remedy is generally available to Buyer?
(A) Only return of the deposit. (B) Specific performance, or damages measured by the difference between market value and contract price. (C) Only punitive damages. (D) No remedy, because the buyer has not yet paid.
**Best answer: B.** Land is unique, so specific performance is available; damages (in the majority) measure benefit of the bargain. Wrong: A, minority English rule only absent bad faith; C and D, no.

**P15-5 [MBE-style].** A contract provides that if Buyer defaults, Seller may keep Buyer's $50,000 deposit (10% of the price) as liquidated damages. Buyer defaults. Seller's actual loss is uncertain at signing and later turns out to be $40,000. In a jurisdiction that enforces liquidated damages that were a reasonable forecast at contracting, Seller
(A) must refund $10,000. (B) may keep the entire deposit. (C) must refund the deposit and sue for damages. (D) may keep the deposit plus $40,000.
**Best answer: B.** Reasonable forecast and difficult to estimate: the clause is enforced. Wrong: A and C, hindsight is not the test; D, double recovery.

### MODULE 16. Marketability of title (III.C)

**Rules**
- **Implied covenant**: absent contrary agreement, every contract for the sale of land includes an implied promise by the seller to deliver **marketable title at closing** (not at signing). **Marketable** = free from reasonable doubt as to law or fact, such that a reasonably prudent buyer, advised by competent counsel, would accept it ("reasonably free from risk of litigation" per NextGen). Contracts may vary this ("good and insurable", "quitclaim only", "subject to easements of record").
- **Unmarketable** (examples): a mortgage or lien not paid at closing; a title with an outstanding **claim** (e.g., a cloud from a missing link or a forged deed); an **easement or covenant** that is not disclosed or excepted (a visible, known easement may be treated as accepted); **zoning violation** (a violation of zoning or building code makes title unmarketable; the zoning restriction itself does not); a **gap in the chain**; title resting on **adverse possession** not yet adjudicated (majority: unmarketable until a court judgment); title that depends on a future event, a contingent remainder or an unprobated will; **encroachments**; a fee simple subject to a right of entry; **dower/homestead** interests of an unjoined spouse in some states.
- **Not (by itself) unmarketable**: a general zoning ordinance, a recorded easement the buyer knew about, minor encroachments, mortgage paid at closing from the proceeds (the buyer must accept payment at closing), title policy exceptions that the contract allows.
- **Timing and cure**: the seller may cure by the closing date, including with the sale proceeds (e.g., paying off a mortgage). The buyer may not rescind before closing merely because a defect exists unless the seller cannot or will not cure (anticipatory repudiation). If the seller cannot deliver marketable title at closing and the contract has no cure period, the buyer may rescind, recover the deposit, or sue for damages, or accept the title with an abatement.
- **Waiver**: buyer who accepts the deed with knowledge of the defect, or fails to object in time under the contract, waives.
- **Merger**: the marketable-title covenant merges into the deed at closing; the buyer's remedies thereafter lie in the deed's covenants of title (Module 27).
- **Title insurance** does not by itself make the title marketable unless the contract calls for "insurable" title.

**Traps**: marketability is tested at closing; the seller may cure at closing; zoning violations vs zoning restrictions; adverse possession title is unmarketable until judicially established; the contract can change the standard.

**Fact patterns**

**P16-1 [MBE-style].** Seller contracts to sell Blackacre to Buyer free of encumbrances. The title search shows an unpaid $100,000 mortgage on Blackacre. The price is $400,000. At closing, Seller arranges for the mortgage to be paid from the proceeds and offers a satisfaction. Buyer refuses to close. Buyer will
(A) prevail; title was unmarketable when the contract was signed. (B) lose; Seller may cure at closing with the proceeds. (C) prevail; mortgages always make title unmarketable. (D) lose only if Buyer has a financing contingency.
**Best answer: B.** Marketability is tested at closing; cure with proceeds is permitted. Wrong: A, timing; C, curable; D, financing contingency is irrelevant.

**P16-2 [MBE-style].** Seller contracts to sell a house to Buyer, with title to be "marketable." Before closing, the city notifies Seller that the house violates the zoning side-yard setback. Seller says the violation is minor and refuses to cure. Buyer refuses to close and demands the deposit back. Buyer will
(A) lose; zoning is a government matter. (B) prevail; a zoning violation makes title unmarketable. (C) lose, because zoning restrictions in general do not affect marketability. (D) prevail only if the violation is in the contract.
**Best answer: B.** A zoning violation (not the mere existence of a zoning ordinance) renders title unmarketable. Wrong: A and C, confuse restriction with violation; D, the implied covenant applies.

**P16-3 [MBE-style].** Seller contracts to sell Blackacre to Buyer. Seller acquired title by 20 years of open possession of land owned by another; the period is 15 years, but Seller never sued to quiet title. Seller offers a deed at closing. Under the majority view, Buyer
(A) must close; Seller owns by operation of law. (B) may refuse to close; title based on adverse possession is not marketable until established by judgment. (C) must close because title insurance is available. (D) may terminate only if the true owner has appeared.
**Best answer: B.** Adverse possession title is unmarketable until judicially established (majority). Wrong: A, theory vs marketability; C, title insurance isn't the test; D, no need for an adverse claimant.

**P16-4 [MBE-style].** Three weeks before the scheduled closing, Buyer's title search shows an unpaid mortgage on the property. Buyer immediately cancels the contract and demands the deposit. Seller responds that she will pay off the mortgage from the sale proceeds at closing and has never said she cannot deliver clear title. Buyer's cancellation was
(A) valid; Seller breached when the mortgage was discovered. (B) premature; marketability is measured at closing and Seller has time to cure. (C) valid, because a mortgage always makes title unmarketable. (D) invalid only because Buyer had no financing contingency.
**Best answer: B.** The implied covenant is to deliver marketable title at closing; absent repudiation, the buyer may not cancel before then. Wrong: A, timing; C, curable by payoff at closing; D, irrelevant.

**P16-5 [MBE-style].** A contract for sale of land requires "marketable title." Buyer's title search reveals a possible claim by a stranger to the chain. Seller offers title insurance that will insure over the claim, but the contract says nothing about insurance. Buyer refuses to close. Buyer
(A) must close; title insurance cures any defect. (B) may refuse to close; marketable title is not the same as insurable title unless the contract so provides. (C) must close because the claim is only possible. (D) loses only if the title company is licensed.
**Best answer: B.** Wrong: A, C and D conflate insurability with marketability.

### MODULE 17. Equitable conversion and risk of loss (III.D; MBE only)

**Rules**
- On the signing of an enforceable contract for the sale of land, the **buyer acquires equitable title** (the right to specific performance and to the land), and the **seller holds legal title as security** for the price. The seller's interest is treated as **personal property** (the right to the purchase money), the buyer's as **real property**.
- **Risk of loss (majority/common law)**: the **buyer** bears the risk of casualty (fire, flood) after the contract is signed and before closing, **unless** the contract says otherwise, the seller caused the loss, or a statute applies. The buyer must close and pay the full price (the seller's insurance, if any, may be held for the buyer's benefit or applied to the price; rules vary **[UNCERTAIN]**). **Minority/statutory**: the **seller** bears the risk until legal title or possession passes (Massachusetts is the classic common-law example; **Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act** (UVPRA) in some states (e.g., New York G.O.L. § 5-1311) **[VERIFY list]**; California Civil Code § 1662 shifts the risk to the seller until possession or title passes **[VERIFY]**). Many states: if the buyer is in possession, the buyer bears the risk.
- **Death before closing**: if the **seller** dies, the buyer's contract is enforced; the proceeds pass as personal property (to the **residuary legatee**, not to the specific devisee of the land) under the traditional doctrine; modern statutes (UPC § 2-606(a)(1)) may give a specific devisee the unpaid balance. If the **buyer** dies, the land passes to the buyer's devisee/heir (real property), and the estate pays the price.
- Equitable conversion also explains why a **vendee's interest** is subject to judgment liens and why a **contract of sale** can sever a joint tenancy.
- Exceptions: no conversion if the contract is unenforceable (Statute of Frauds), if a condition remains unsatisfied (financing contingency not yet satisfied: courts split), or if the contract says otherwise.

**Traps**: majority = buyer bears risk; minority/statute = seller; equitable conversion answers the "who gets the proceeds" question.

**Fact patterns**

**P17-1 [MBE-style].** Seller and Buyer sign an enforceable contract to sell Blackacre, with a house, closing August 1. The contract says nothing about casualty. On July 10 lightning destroys the house. Neither party is at fault. The jurisdiction follows the common-law majority rule. Buyer
(A) may rescind and recover the deposit. (B) must close and pay the full price; the risk of loss was on Buyer after the contract was signed. (C) may reduce the price to the land's value. (D) may sue Seller for negligence.
**Best answer: B.** Equitable conversion puts the risk on the buyer. Wrong: A and C, minority/statutory rule; D, no fault.

**P17-2 [MBE-style].** Same facts, but the jurisdiction has enacted the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act, which places the risk of loss on the seller until legal title or possession passes to the buyer. Buyer is not in possession. Buyer
(A) must close and pay in full. (B) may rescind and recover the deposit. (C) may sue only the fire department. (D) must close but may reduce the price by the value of the house.
**Best answer: B.** The statute shifts the risk to Seller. Wrong: A and D, majority rule; C, nonsense.

**P17-3 [MBE-style].** Applying the traditional doctrine of equitable conversion (ignoring statutes that alter it), Seller signs a valid contract to sell Blackacre to Buyer for $400,000, then dies before closing. Seller's will leaves "Blackacre to Ann" and "all the rest of my property to Ben." Who is entitled to the $400,000 purchase price paid at closing?
(A) Ann. (B) Ben. (C) Seller's heirs. (D) Buyer keeps it.
**Best answer: B.** Seller's interest became personal property (the right to the price) at contract signing; the devise of Blackacre adeems, so the proceeds pass with the residue. Wrong: A, modern statutes may give her the balance but the traditional doctrine does not; C, there is a will; D, absurd. **[Mark `status: "general"` and say "traditional doctrine" in the stem.]**

### MODULE 18. Options and rights of first refusal (III.E)

**Rules**
- An **option** is an irrevocable offer supported by consideration (or in a lease). **Time is strictly of the essence**; exercise must be **timely and unqualified** and in the manner stated. A late exercise is ineffective; equity may relieve only in narrow cases (e.g., mistake plus unfair forfeiture).
- Exercise creates a bilateral contract; equitable conversion begins.
- **Right of first refusal (ROFR)**: the holder may buy on the same terms the owner receives from a bona fide third party; if the owner sells without offering, the holder can get **specific performance** against a buyer who took with notice (recorded or actual). A ROFR typically lacks a fixed price and may be subject to the RAP (majority; see Module 3).
- **Option appurtenant to a lease** is generally exempt from RAP; **option in gross** is not.
- **Options in security transactions** (mortgage): may be a clog on the equity of redemption (Module 22).

**Fact patterns**

**P18-1 [MBE-style].** Owner grants Ana a one-year option to buy Blackacre for $100,000 "exercisable by written notice delivered no later than 5 p.m. on June 30." Ana delivers notice and tender at 9 a.m. on July 1, explaining she had been traveling. Owner sells to Ben on July 2. Ana sues. Ana will
(A) prevail; courts relieve every late exercise. (B) lose; the option lapsed when it was not exercised timely. (C) prevail because Owner suffered no prejudice. (D) prevail only against Ben.
**Best answer: B.** Options are strictly construed as to time. Wrong: A and C, no general relief; D, no.

**P18-2 [MBE-style].** Owner grants Ana a recorded right of first refusal on Blackacre at the price Owner is offered by a bona fide purchaser. Owner, without notifying Ana, sells to Cal at $200,000; Cal knew of the recorded right. Ana sues for specific performance. Ana will
(A) lose; she has only a contract claim against Owner. (B) prevail against Cal, who took with notice. (C) lose because a ROFR is void. (D) prevail only for damages against Owner.
**Best answer: B.** A purchaser with notice takes subject to the ROFR. Wrong: A and D, equity can reach Cal; C, ROFRs are valid (subject to RAP in some states).

### MODULE 19. Fitness and suitability of the property sold (III.F)

**Rules**
- **New residences by builder-vendors**: **implied warranty of habitability/good workmanship** (majority): the builder-vendor of a new home impliedly warrants that it is built in a workmanlike manner and is fit for habitation. Many states extend it to **subsequent purchasers**; **disclaimers** are effective only if conspicuous, clear and (in many states) specifically bargained for; many statutes limit duration and require notice-and-repair procedures. It does **not** normally apply to **sellers of used homes**.
- **Existing property (non-builder sellers)**: traditional **caveat emptor**; modern exceptions: (1) **fraud/misrepresentation**, (2) **active concealment**, (3) **failure to disclose known latent material defects** that the buyer could not reasonably discover (majority trend; Stambovsky v. Ackley, 169 A.D.2d 254 (N.Y. App. Div. 1991) (nondisclosure of a "haunted" house was actionable) **[from memory; VERIFY]**), (4) statutory disclosure forms. 
- **"As is" clauses**: bar implied-warranty claims but **do not bar fraud or active concealment**.
- Disclosure by brokers; inspection contingencies.
- NextGen: "distinctions between builders and sellers of existing property as to misrepresentation, fraud, active concealment, and failure to disclose; and disclaimers of liability."

**Fact patterns**

**P19-1 [MBE-style].** Builder constructs a house and sells it to Buyer. Buyer discovers that the foundation was poured improperly and the house is unfit for habitation. The contract does not mention warranties or disclaimers. In a jurisdiction recognizing implied warranties for new homes, Buyer will
(A) lose; caveat emptor applies. (B) prevail; the builder-vendor impliedly warranted a habitable, workmanlike home. (C) lose because the deed contains no warranty. (D) prevail only if Builder lived in the house.
**Best answer: B.** Wrong: A, caveat emptor yields to the new-home warranty; C, warranty is implied; D, irrelevant.

**P19-2 [MBE-style].** Seller, an individual, sells a used home "as is." Seller knows that the basement leaks and, before showing it, paints over water stains to hide them. The leak is not apparent to a reasonable inspection. Buyer sues. Buyer will
(A) lose; "as is" bars all claims. (B) prevail; "as is" does not protect against fraud or active concealment. (C) lose; caveat emptor applies to used homes. (D) prevail only if the contract has a seller disclosure form.
**Best answer: B.** Wrong: A and C, no protection for fraud; D, common law concealment is actionable without a form.

### MODULE 20. Merger (III.G)

**Rules**
- **Doctrine of merger**: when the seller delivers and the buyer accepts the deed, the **contract merges into the deed**; the buyer's rights are then determined by the deed (and its covenants of title). Contract terms **relating to title, possession, quantity or the quality of the estate conveyed** that are not carried into the deed are generally **merged** and cannot be enforced.
- **Exceptions**: (1) terms **collateral** to the conveyance and not performed at closing (e.g., promise to build, to repair after closing, to maintain a road, to purchase insurance), (2) **fraud or mistake**, (3) contract terms intended to survive (express survival clause), (4) deed delivered under mistake; some states: purchaser may recover for matters outside the deed's terms.
- Merger interacts with Module 16 (title covenants) and Module 27 (covenants of title).

**Fact patterns**

**P20-1 [MBE-style].** A contract says Seller will convey "5.0 acres." The deed Seller delivers and Buyer accepts describes the land by metes and bounds that actually encloses 4.5 acres. After closing, Buyer discovers the shortfall. Buyer sues for breach of the contract's 5-acre term. Buyer will
(A) prevail; contract terms always survive. (B) lose; the acreage term merged into the deed, which is a quitclaim of the described land. (C) prevail only for fraud. (D) lose, unless the deed contains no description.
**Best answer: B.** Quantity relates to the estate conveyed; it merged. Wrong: A, merger exists; C, fraud would be an exception but no fraud is alleged; D, the deed's description controls.

**P20-2 [MBE-style].** A contract says Seller will maintain the private access road at her expense for five years after closing. The deed is silent. After closing, Seller stops maintaining it. Buyer sues. Buyer will
(A) lose; the contract merged into the deed. (B) prevail; the promise is collateral to the conveyance and survives. (C) lose; promises of future performance are unenforceable. (D) prevail only if the road is described in the deed.
**Best answer: B.** A collateral promise to be performed after closing does not merge. Wrong: A, merger applies to terms about title/possession/quantity; C and D, no.

## Category IV. Mortgages and security devices

Common vocabulary: **mortgagor** (borrower/owner), **mortgagee** (lender), **note** (the debt), **mortgage** (the lien), **deed of trust** (three-party device with a trustee holding a power of sale; used in Texas and many western and southern states). The mortgage is **security for an obligation**; without a valid obligation, there is nothing to secure.

### MODULE 21. Types of security devices; theories; rights before foreclosure; redemption (IV.A, IV.B)

**Rules**
- **Mortgage** = a written, signed instrument creating a lien on land to secure an obligation; must satisfy the Statute of Frauds. **Deed of trust**: borrower conveys to a trustee, who holds title for the lender and sells on default (power of sale); in functional terms treated as a mortgage (statutory rules about foreclosure and deficiency may differ). **Purchase-money mortgage (PMM)**: given by the buyer to the seller or to a lender that finances the purchase. **Future advance mortgage**: secures obligations to be advanced later.
- **Mortgage theories**: **title theory** (mortgagee holds legal title until satisfaction; mortgagee entitled to possession and rents on default or even before), **lien theory** (mortgagee holds only a lien; mortgagor holds title and possession until foreclosure; **majority**), **intermediate theory** (mortgagor holds title and possession until default; the mortgagee may take possession after default). **Per NCBE for NextGen (Real Property Sourcebook excerpt): assume a lien-theory jurisdiction unless the question says otherwise.** Source: https://thebarexaminer.ncbex.org/article/fall-2025/nextgen-ube-sourcebook-series ("Under the title theory, legal 'title' is in the mortgagee until the mortgage has been satisfied or foreclosed"; lien theory: "the mortgagee holds no 'title' but has security only"). The same convention is the safest assumption on the MBE.
- **Rights before foreclosure**: In lien-theory states the mortgagor keeps possession and rents; the mortgagee may seek a **receiver** or injunction if the mortgagor commits **waste** that impairs the security. Rents: mortgagee's right to rents is limited (lien theory: only after default and appointment of receiver or an assignment of rents). **Leases**: a lease created **before** the mortgage is senior; a lease created **after** is junior and can be cut off in foreclosure (unless the foreclosure doesn't name the tenant).
- **Equity of redemption**: the mortgagor can redeem **before the foreclosure sale** by paying the entire debt (principal, interest, costs) — cannot be waived in the original mortgage; **clogging the equity of redemption** (any agreement made at the time of the loan that gives the mortgagee an option to buy the property or automatic title on default) is void. A deed in lieu after default, entered voluntarily and fairly, is permitted (Module 23). Statutory **post-sale redemption** in about half the states.
- **Installment land contract (contract for deed)**: the seller keeps title until the final payment; the buyer takes possession and pays in installments. Traditional rule: upon default, the seller may **forfeit** the buyer's payments and possession (time is of the essence). Modern rule (for substantial equity): treat like a mortgage and require **judicial foreclosure**, or grant a grace period or restitution; many states restrict forfeiture by statute. Skendzel v. Marshall, 261 Ind. 226, 301 N.E.2d 641 (1973) **[citation from memory; VERIFY]**. The buyer is the equitable owner (risk of loss on buyer in majority).
- **Absolute deed as security**: a deed absolute on its face given as security for a debt is treated as a **mortgage** if the parties so intended; parol evidence is admissible to show intent; the grantor keeps the equity of redemption and the grantee must foreclose. Evidence: grantor stays in possession, price inadequate, debt exists. A BFP from the grantee without notice takes free.
- **Necessity of an obligation**: a mortgage secures a debt; payment of the debt discharges it; a mortgage without a valid underlying debt is unenforceable.

**Traps**: assume lien theory; clogging; equity of redemption cannot be waived at the outset; installment contracts and deeds absolute as mortgages are the two "disguised mortgage" devices; leases created after the mortgage are cut off by foreclosure.

**One-breath lines**
- "Lien theory: the lender holds a lien, the borrower holds the land."
- "You can't bargain away your right to redeem when you sign the loan."

**Fact patterns**

**P21-1 [MBE-style].** In a lien-theory jurisdiction, Owner mortgages Blackacre to Bank. Owner is not in default. Bank demands that Owner pay the rents from the tenants on Blackacre to Bank. Bank
(A) is entitled to the rents, because it holds legal title. (B) is not entitled to the rents before default; Owner keeps title and possession. (C) may collect rents if the mortgage is a deed of trust. (D) may collect rents because the mortgage is a first lien.
**Best answer: B.** In lien theory the mortgagor keeps possession and rents until foreclosure or an enforceable assignment of rents on default. Wrong: A, title theory; C and D, no.

**P21-2 [MBE-style].** Owner borrows $100,000 from Lender and signs a mortgage on Blackacre. The mortgage provides that if Owner defaults, Lender may purchase Blackacre for $60,000 (far below market value). Owner defaults. The clause is
(A) valid; freedom of contract. (B) void as a clog on the equity of redemption. (C) valid if recorded. (D) valid only in a title-theory state.
**Best answer: B.** Wrong: A, C, D.

**P21-3 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre by a deed absolute on its face to Lender for $50,000, orally agreeing to reconvey if Owner repays within a year. Owner remains in possession. Owner offers to repay on day 300; Lender refuses. The property is worth $200,000. Owner will
(A) lose; the deed is absolute. (B) prevail; the transaction is a mortgage and Owner may redeem. (C) lose because the parol evidence rule bars the oral term. (D) lose because the debt was undocumented.
**Best answer: B.** Parol evidence may show the deed was intended as security; the transaction is a mortgage with a right to redeem. Wrong: A and C, courts look through form; D, a loan exists.

**P21-4 [MBE-style].** Buyer buys a house under a 20-year installment land contract; after paying 70% of the price over 14 years, Buyer misses three payments. The contract says Seller may declare a forfeiture on default and keep all payments. In a jurisdiction that follows the modern trend, Seller will
(A) succeed; forfeiture is enforced as written. (B) be required to foreclose or otherwise protect Buyer's accrued equity. (C) lose all rights. (D) succeed because the buyer has no equity.
**Best answer: B.** Wrong: A, traditional rule; C, no; D, Buyer has substantial equity.

### MODULE 22. Transfers by mortgagor and mortgagee (IV.C)

**Rules**
- **Mortgagor transfers the property**: the mortgage **stays on the land**; transfer does not release the mortgagor's personal liability on the note.
- **Assumption** (grantee agrees to pay the debt): grantee becomes **personally and primarily liable**; the original mortgagor becomes a **surety** (secondarily liable) as between the two, although she remains liable to the lender. The lender may sue either. If the lender and grantee **modify** the loan without the original mortgagor's consent, she is discharged to the extent of prejudice (suretyship), unless the lender releases or retains rights. **Subrogation**: a surety who pays is subrogated to the lender's rights against the primary obligor. 
- **Transfer "subject to"** the mortgage: grantee is **not personally liable** on the debt; grantor remains liable; the lender can foreclose; if the sale yields a deficiency, the lender can sue the **original mortgagor**, not the grantee.
- **Due-on-sale clauses**: enforceable (Garn-St Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982, 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(b)). For residential property with **fewer than five dwelling units**, the lender **may not** exercise due-on-sale on (verified list, § 1701j-3(d)): creation of a junior lien not transferring occupancy; a PMSI for household appliances; a transfer by **devise, descent or operation of law on the death of a joint tenant or tenant by the entirety**; a lease of three years or less without a purchase option; transfer to a **relative** upon the borrower's death; transfer where the **spouse or children** become owners; a transfer from a **divorce decree** or separation agreement; transfer into an **inter vivos trust** where the borrower remains a beneficiary (no transfer of occupancy rights); others by regulation. Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1701j-3
- **Transfers by the mortgagee**: the mortgagee may assign the note and mortgage (assignment of the **debt carries the mortgage**; the mortgage follows the note); an assignee takes **subject to defenses** of the mortgagor unless the assignee is a **holder in due course** (for a negotiable note, free from personal defenses). If the mortgagor pays the original lender **without notice** of the assignment, the debt is discharged to that extent. Recording an assignment protects the assignee against a later assignee. A **partial** assignment/participation: pro rata priority unless agreed.
- **Equitable subrogation**: a lender (or third party) who pays off a senior lien at the borrower's request, without notice of an intervening junior lien, may be subrogated to the senior's priority to avoid unjust enrichment (majority; Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 7.6) **[VERIFY section number]**.

**Traps**: assumption vs subject to (personal liability); the original mortgagor is a surety after assumption; due-on-sale exceptions; the mortgage follows the note.

**Fact patterns**

**P22-1 [MBE-style].** Owner mortgages Blackacre to Bank for $300,000, and sells Blackacre to Buyer, who expressly "assumes the mortgage." Buyer defaults. Foreclosure yields a $60,000 deficiency. From whom can Bank collect the deficiency?
(A) Owner only. (B) Buyer only. (C) Owner or Buyer. (D) Neither; the mortgage was transferred.
**Best answer: C.** Buyer is primarily liable by assumption; Owner remains liable on the note as a surety. Wrong: A, B and D ignore one obligor or the release.

**P22-2 [MBE-style].** Same facts, but Buyer takes Blackacre "subject to" the mortgage and does not assume it. From whom can Bank collect the deficiency?
(A) Owner only. (B) Buyer only. (C) Owner and Buyer jointly. (D) No one.
**Best answer: A.** Buyer has no personal liability; Owner remains liable on the note. Wrong: B and C, Buyer is not personally liable; D, deficiency judgments are available against the maker (subject to anti-deficiency statutes).

**P22-3 [MBE-style].** Homeowner (single-family residence) transfers the house into an inter vivos living trust of which Homeowner remains a beneficiary and continues to live in the house. The mortgage has a due-on-sale clause. The Lender
(A) may accelerate the loan under the clause. (B) may not exercise the due-on-sale clause, because the Garn-St Germain Act bars it for this transfer. (C) may accelerate only if the trust is irrevocable. (D) may accelerate only if the Homeowner agrees.
**Best answer: B.** The transfer is within the 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d)(8) exception. Wrong: A, C, D.

**P22-4 [MBE-style].** Bank holds a mortgage and note from Owner. Bank assigns the note and mortgage to Assignee, who does not record or notify Owner. Owner, without notice, pays the next three installments to Bank. Assignee demands them from Owner. Owner
(A) must pay Assignee again. (B) is discharged as to those payments made to Bank without notice of the assignment. (C) is liable to both. (D) is not liable on the note.
**Best answer: B.** Payment to the original mortgagee without notice discharges. Wrong: A and C, double payment; D, the debt remains.

### MODULE 23. Discharge of the mortgage (IV.D)

**Rules**
- **Payment in full** discharges; the mortgagee must give a **satisfaction** (recorded release). **Prepayment**: at common law, the borrower has **no right** to prepay a mortgage with a fixed term unless the instrument permits; prepayment clauses and penalties are in the contract or state statute.
- **Deed in lieu of foreclosure**: borrower conveys the property to the lender after default in satisfaction of the debt. Valid if voluntary, with fair consideration and no overreaching; it does **not** cut off junior liens (unlike foreclosure) and may leave the lender with a title burdened by junior liens; it may preserve or extinguish the debt (depends on the instrument). Merger doctrine: the mortgage merges with the fee when the same person holds both, unless the parties intend otherwise or a junior lien intervenes.
- **Novation**, **release**, **satisfaction of record**; **statute of limitations** on the debt may bar suit while the lien may persist in some states.

**Fact pattern**

**P23-1 [MBE-style].** Owner defaults on a first mortgage held by Bank. Owner has also given a second mortgage to Second Lender. Owner gives Bank a deed in lieu of foreclosure for Blackacre. Which is correct?
(A) The deed in lieu extinguishes the second mortgage. (B) The deed in lieu does not extinguish the second mortgage; Bank takes subject to it. (C) The second mortgage is extinguished if Bank records first. (D) Second Lender must be paid out of Owner's other assets only.
**Best answer: B.** Unlike a foreclosure, a deed in lieu does not cut off junior liens. Wrong: A and C; D, the junior lien remains on the property.

### MODULE 24. Foreclosure (IV.E)

**Rules**
- **Types**: **judicial foreclosure** (court action, sale ordered, usually by a sheriff or commissioner; the standard model in many states), **power of sale** (nonjudicial; under a deed of trust or a mortgage with a power-of-sale clause; statutory notice and public sale; Texas and many western and southern states), **strict foreclosure** (court sets a deadline; if the debt is not paid the mortgagee's title becomes absolute; rare), **entry and possession** (rare). 
- **Acceleration**: upon default, if the instrument contains an acceleration clause, the whole debt is due; the lender must follow notice requirements; many states and contracts give a right to **reinstate** (cure arrears before sale).
- **Defenses**: lack of standing (the party does not hold the note), payment, failure to give statutory notice, fraud, unconscionable sale procedures; a sale price that is merely low usually will not be set aside unless it is so low as to shock the conscience (and some procedural defect exists). 
- **Parties**: to cut off an interest, the foreclosing mortgagee must **join** it; junior lienholders and subsequent purchasers must be named. **Omitted junior**: the foreclosure does not extinguish its lien; the purchaser takes subject to it; the omitted junior may **redeem** by paying the senior debt or foreclose its own lien. **Omitted senior**: unaffected; a foreclosure by a junior leaves the senior lien intact; the purchaser takes subject to it. Interests that are **senior** to the foreclosing mortgage (including leases prior in time) survive.
- **Distribution of proceeds (order)**: (1) **costs of sale** and foreclosure expenses; (2) the **debt of the foreclosing mortgage** (principal, interest, fees); (3) **junior liens** in order of priority; (4) **surplus** to the mortgagor (the owner of the equity of redemption). Seniors do not get paid from a junior's foreclosure sale (their liens continue on the land).
- **Deficiency**: if proceeds are less than the debt, the mortgagee may obtain a **deficiency judgment** against the personal obligor (the mortgagor or an assuming grantee), unless: the loan is **nonrecourse**; an **anti-deficiency** statute applies (e.g., many states bar deficiency for purchase-money residential loans or after nonjudicial foreclosure); or the state requires a **fair value** credit if the sale price is grossly inadequate. A junior lienholder who is paid less than its debt may sue the debtor on the note for its deficiency (as an unsecured creditor).
- **Redemption after foreclosure**: the mortgagor's **equity of redemption** (pay the debt before the sale) ends at the sale. About half of the states give **statutory redemption** after the sale (typically 6-12 months; redemption price is the sale price plus interest and costs, or the debt); during the period the mortgagor may keep possession in some states; some states let junior lienors redeem.
- **Tax foreclosures** (not on the MBE outline): Tyler v. Hennepin County, 598 U.S. 631 (2023) (government cannot keep surplus equity after a tax foreclosure sale; Takings Clause) **[cite from memory; VERIFY; Constitutional Law material]**.

**Traps**: omitted junior survives; omitted senior is untouched; surplus and deficiency; statutory redemption exists only where a statute says; the senior mortgagee is paid first from the sale, but seniors are never paid in a junior's foreclosure.

**One-breath lines**
- "Costs, then the foreclosing lender, then the juniors in order; any leftover is the owner's."
- "Skip a junior in the lawsuit and its lien rides on."

**Fact patterns**

**P24-1 [MBE-style].** Blackacre is subject to a first mortgage of $200,000 held by Bank 1 and a second mortgage of $100,000 held by Bank 2. Bank 1 forecloses by judicial sale, joining Bank 2 and Owner. The sale brings $280,000. Costs of sale are $10,000. Bank 2 receives
(A) $100,000. (B) $70,000, and may sue Owner for the remaining $30,000. (C) nothing. (D) $90,000.
**Best answer: B.** Costs ($10,000), then Bank 1 ($200,000), leaving $70,000 for Bank 2; Owner receives nothing; Bank 2's remaining $30,000 is an unsecured claim on the note. Wrong: A and D, miscalculate the costs/priority; C, Bank 2 is paid after Bank 1.

**P24-2 [MBE-style].** Bank 1 forecloses its first mortgage on Blackacre but does not name Bank 2, the holder of a recorded second mortgage, as a party. Pam buys at the sale. Which statement is correct?
(A) Bank 2's mortgage is extinguished. (B) Pam takes Blackacre subject to Bank 2's mortgage; Bank 2 may redeem or foreclose. (C) Bank 2 may only sue Bank 1. (D) Bank 2's lien moves to the sale proceeds.
**Best answer: B.** A junior not joined is not bound by the foreclosure. Wrong: A, C, D.

**P24-3 [MBE-style].** The holder of a second mortgage forecloses and a buyer purchases at the sale. The first mortgage was not part of the action. The buyer
(A) takes free of the first mortgage. (B) takes subject to the first mortgage, which remains a lien. (C) must pay the first mortgage from the buyer's own funds immediately. (D) takes the property only if the first mortgagee consents.
**Best answer: B.** A senior lien survives a junior's foreclosure. Wrong: A, C, D.

**P24-4 [MBE-style].** In a state with a six-month statutory redemption period, Bank forecloses and sells Blackacre to Bidder for $150,000 (debt: $160,000). Four months later Owner tenders to Bidder the sale price plus the interest and costs that the redemption statute prescribes. Owner will
(A) lose; the sale extinguished all rights. (B) prevail; Owner may redeem within the statutory period. (C) lose unless Owner pays the full $160,000 debt plus fees. (D) prevail only if Bidder consents.
**Best answer: B.** In a state that grants a statutory right of redemption, the mortgagor may redeem by paying the price (plus interest and costs) within the period. Wrong: A, statute grants the right; C, the statute's stated redemption price controls and the stem gives it; D, no consent needed. **[UNCERTAIN: the redemption price differs by state (sale price vs. full debt), so the stem states the statute's price.]**

**P24-5 [MBE-style].** A foreclosure sale of Blackacre is held on Monday. The jurisdiction gives no statutory redemption. On Tuesday Owner offers to pay the full debt and costs. Owner
(A) may redeem; the equity of redemption lasts for a year. (B) may not redeem; the equity of redemption ended at the sale. (C) may redeem only with the purchaser's consent. (D) may redeem if the purchaser has not recorded.
**Best answer: B.** The equity of redemption is cut off by the foreclosure sale. Wrong: A, C, D.

### MODULE 25. Priorities among liens; purchase money mortgages; future advances; subrogation (IV; cross-over with V.D)

**Rules**
- **Basic rule**: first in time, first in right, subject to the **recording act**: a later mortgagee that is a BFP (for value, without notice) and that records (as the statute requires) may defeat an earlier **unrecorded** mortgage (Module 29).
- **Purchase-money mortgage (PMM)**: a PMM has priority over **all other claims arising through the buyer/mortgagor**, such as a prior judgment lien against the buyer or a prior mortgage on after-acquired property clause, even though those claims are earlier in time ("instantaneous seisin"; the PMM lender supplied the funds that enabled the buyer to acquire the land). A **seller's PMM** has the same priority. As between a PMM and a lien arising from the seller, ordinary priority rules apply.
- **Future advances**: a mortgage may secure advances not yet made. **Obligatory advances** (the lender is bound to make them) have priority from the mortgage's recording date. **Optional advances** (lender's discretion): under the traditional rule, the lender loses priority for any advance made **after it has notice** of an intervening lien (such as a second mortgage); many states have **open-end mortgage statutes** that give priority up to a stated maximum from recording regardless of notice **[state-specific; UNCERTAIN as to count; the stem will say which rule applies]**.
- **Subordination**: a mortgagee can voluntarily subordinate to another interest by agreement; priority is then modified. Failure to record may subordinate.
- **Equitable subrogation**: a lender who pays off a senior mortgage (e.g., refinancing) without actual knowledge of an intervening lien, and with a reasonable expectation of first priority, is subrogated to the senior lien's priority (majority; Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 7.6) **[VERIFY]**.
- **Tax liens and mechanics' liens**: real property tax liens have **priority over all private liens** by statute (superpriority); mechanics' liens may relate back to the start of work in many states **[outside the exam detail]**.
- **Suretyship principles**: an assuming grantee is the principal debtor; the original mortgagor is a surety; a surety who pays is subrogated to the lender's rights.

**Traps**: PMM beats earlier-filed judgment liens; optional vs obligatory advances; refinancing lender can step into the old lender's first position.

**Fact patterns**

**P25-1 [MBE-style].** Creditor obtains and dockets a money judgment against Buyer in County X, creating a lien on all of Buyer's real property in the county. A month later Buyer buys Blackacre in County X, financing the purchase with a purchase-money mortgage from Bank, promptly recorded. Whose lien has priority on Blackacre?
(A) Creditor, whose judgment was first in time. (B) Bank, because a purchase-money mortgage has priority over liens arising against the buyer. (C) They share pro rata. (D) Creditor, because judgment liens are always superior.
**Best answer: B.** A PMM has priority over liens against the buyer, including earlier docketed judgments. Wrong: A and D; C, no sharing rule.

**P25-2 [MBE-style].** Bank records a mortgage on Blackacre securing a $500,000 line of credit, advances to be made at Bank's option. Bank advances $200,000. Then Lender 2 takes and records a second mortgage on Blackacre. Bank, with actual knowledge of the second mortgage, makes an additional $150,000 optional advance. Under the traditional common-law rule, as to the $150,000 advance
(A) Bank has priority from the date it recorded. (B) Lender 2 has priority over Bank. (C) they share equally. (D) Bank has priority if the second mortgage was recorded after Bank's.
**Best answer: B.** Optional advances made with notice of an intervening lien lose priority to it. Wrong: A and D describe the open-end statute view; C, no sharing.

**P25-3 [MBE-style].** Owner has a first mortgage with Bank A and a recorded second mortgage with Lender B. Bank C lends Owner funds to pay off Bank A's mortgage and takes a new mortgage, recorded after Lender B's. Bank C had no actual knowledge of Lender B's mortgage. In a majority jurisdiction applying equitable subrogation, Bank C's mortgage has priority
(A) over Lender B, to the extent of the Bank A debt it paid off. (B) only over later liens. (C) never; recording order controls. (D) only if Lender B consents.
**Best answer: A.** Bank C is subrogated to Bank A's first-lien position. Wrong: B, C, D.

## Category V. Titles

### MODULE 26. Adverse possession (V.A)

**Rules**
- **Elements**: possession that is **actual**, **open and notorious**, **exclusive**, **continuous** and **hostile (adverse, without the true owner's consent)** for the **statutory period**. The Cornell LII Wex entry lists the same elements (https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/adverse_possession). Statutory periods vary widely (commonly 10-20 years; the MBE gives the number). Texas, for local color only: 3 years under title or color of title (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.024, confirmed), 5, 10 and 25 years in §§ 16.025-16.027 **[from memory, VERIFY before use]**.
- **Continuous** = the use an average owner would make of such land (seasonal use can be continuous); **tacking** of successive possessors needs **privity** (voluntary transfer by deed, will, descent, or even a parol transfer of possession: Howard v. Kunto, 3 Wn. App. 393 (1970)) **[VERIFY cite]**.
- **Hostile**: majority **objective** test (possession inconsistent with the true owner's rights; the possessor's state of mind, including good-faith mistake about a boundary, is irrelevant; Mannillo v. Gorski, 54 N.J. 378 (1969)); minority: require good faith or intent to claim title. Permission (license, lease) defeats hostility. A tenant cannot adversely possess against the landlord during the lease; a **cotenant** needs ouster or clear repudiation.
- **Color of title** (a deed or instrument that appears valid but is defective): shorter period in some states; **constructive adverse possession** — possession of part of the described tract under color of title can give constructive possession of the rest, if the remainder is not in the actual possession of another.
- **Disabilities**: if the true owner is under a disability (minority, insanity, sometimes imprisonment) **when the cause of action accrues**, the period is tolled until the disability ends (or the statute's maximum); disabilities **do not tack**, and a disability arising after accrual does not toll.
- **Future interests**: the period does not run against a remainderman or reversioner until the interest becomes possessory (adverse possession of land held by a life tenant gives the possessor only the life tenant's estate).
- **Effect**: title vests automatically at the end of the period; the possessor has a new original title (no court action required, but a judgment is needed for marketable title/insurance). Cannot adversely possess government land.
- **Boundary disputes**: mistaken boundary (marketable) is usually adverse under the objective test; also doctrines of acquiescence and agreed boundary.

**Traps**: permission (even implied) defeats hostility; a tenant's possession isn't adverse to landlord; disability must exist at accrual; tacking needs privity; adverse possession against a life tenant does not bar the remainderman; "good faith" matters only in the minority.

**One-breath lines**
- "Open, actual, exclusive, continuous, hostile, for the full period; permission kills it."
- "A mistaken fence is still hostile in most states."

**Fact patterns**

**P26-1 [MBE-style].** Ana occupies a cabin on Ben's land under an oral arrangement in which Ben lets her stay "as long as you like." Ana farms the land openly for 25 years (statutory period: 15 years). Ben sues to eject her. Ana will
(A) prevail; she meets all elements. (B) lose; her possession was with Ben's permission and not hostile. (C) prevail because Ben never objected. (D) lose because she did not pay taxes.
**Best answer: B.** Permission defeats hostility. Wrong: A and C, adversity is missing; D, taxes are not an element in most states.

**P26-2 [MBE-style].** Ana openly possesses Ben's vacant lot for 8 years (statutory period: 12 years). Ana then orally tells Cal, "I'm moving; the lot is yours," and puts Cal in possession. Cal openly possesses for 5 more years, then claims title. Ben sues. Under the majority view, Cal will
(A) lose; Ana's possession cannot be tacked without a deed. (B) prevail; tacking is permitted because Ana voluntarily transferred possession to Cal. (C) lose; only the last possessor's time counts. (D) prevail only if Ana was in good faith.
**Best answer: B.** Privity of estate for tacking can be satisfied by a voluntary transfer of possession, even oral (Howard v. Kunto). Wrong: A, minority; C, tacking is allowed; D, good faith is not required in the majority.

**P26-3 [MBE-style].** Ben, the owner of a vacant lot, is 10 years old when Ana begins open and hostile possession. The statute of limitations is 15 years, but if the owner is a minor when the cause of action accrues, the period begins to run when the owner turns 18. Ana possesses continuously for 20 years, at which time Ben is 30. Ben sues for possession. Ben will
(A) prevail; the period did not begin until he turned 18, and only 12 years have run since then. (B) lose; Ana possessed for more than 15 years. (C) lose; disabilities never extend the period. (D) prevail only if Ana knew Ben's age.
**Best answer: A.** The disability existed when the cause of action accrued, so the period started at age 18 (12 years elapsed by age 30, fewer than 15). Wrong: B and C, ignore tolling; D, knowledge is irrelevant.

**P26-4 [MBE-style].** Ana and Ben own adjoining lots. Both believe a fence marks the boundary, but it is 10 feet inside Ben's lot. Ana farms up to the fence openly for 20 years (statutory period: 15). Ben sues to move the fence. Under the majority objective test, Ana will
(A) lose; she did not intend to take Ben's land. (B) prevail; her possession was actual, open, exclusive, continuous and hostile in the objective sense. (C) lose; good faith is required. (D) lose; a boundary dispute is not adverse possession.
**Best answer: B.** The majority ignores subjective intent. Wrong: A and C, minority rules; D, boundary disputes are classic adverse possession.

**P26-5 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre "to Ana for life, then to Ben and his heirs." Cal begins adverse possession of Blackacre during Ana's life; the statute is 15 years. After 20 years Ana dies. Ben sues Cal for possession. Ben will
(A) lose; Cal's title is complete. (B) prevail; the period did not run against Ben's remainder until Ana's death. (C) lose unless Ben was a minor. (D) prevail only if Ana consented.
**Best answer: B.** Cal acquired title only against Ana's life estate; Ben's cause of action accrued at Ana's death. Wrong: A, C, D.

**P26-6 [MBE-style].** Ana holds a defective deed (void for improper execution) that describes 100 acres. She openly occupies and farms 20 acres for the statutory period and pays taxes. The remaining 80 acres are wild and unoccupied; no one else possesses them. The true owner sues for all 100 acres. In a jurisdiction recognizing constructive adverse possession under color of title, Ana
(A) owns only the 20 acres. (B) owns all 100 acres. (C) owns none. (D) owns the 20 acres plus a lien on the rest.
**Best answer: B.** Color of title plus actual possession of part gives constructive possession of the rest of the described tract. Wrong: A, no color-of-title rule; C, no; D, no.

### MODULE 27. Transfer by deed; deed requirements; types of deeds; covenants for title; estoppel by deed (V.B, V.E)

**Rules**

*Requirements*
- **Writing** (Statute of Frauds), **signed by the grantor**; identifies the **grantor** and a **grantee** who is **in existence and identifiable** (a deed to a dead person or non-existent entity is void); **words of conveyance** showing present intent ("grant", "convey", no magic words); an adequate **description** of the land (the property can be identified; ambiguities resolved by parol evidence; monuments prevail over courses and distances, then courses over distances, then area); **delivery** and **acceptance**. **Consideration** is not required. **Acknowledgment** is not required for validity but is needed to record. A **seal** is not required in modern law.
- **Delivery**: the grantor's **intent** that the deed take effect as a **present transfer of title**; physical handing over is neither necessary nor sufficient. Presumptions: delivery is presumed if the deed is **handed to the grantee**, **recorded** by the grantor, or in the grantee's possession; recording raises a presumption. **Conditional delivery** to the **grantee** is **unconditional** (oral conditions are ineffective; parol evidence rule bars them) — NextGen states: parol evidence may not show conditional delivery. **Delivery to a third party (escrow)**: valid if the grantor **relinquishes control** and the escrow agent is instructed to deliver on a condition or event (the deed takes effect on the condition; relation back); if the grantor **retains the right to recall**, there is no delivery and the instrument is an invalid will substitute. **Deed to be delivered at grantor's death** with no right of recall: valid (present remainder/ future interest with a life estate in the grantor). **Acceptance** is presumed for beneficial transfers; a grantee's refusal ends the transfer. Death of the grantor between execution and delivery: no delivery, void; death of the grantor after valid delivery to an escrow agent: effective by relation back.
- **Void vs voidable**: **void** deeds (forged, undelivered, grantee non-existent, grantor never intended to sign the instrument, fraud in the factum) pass no title even to a BFP; **voidable** deeds (fraud in the inducement, duress, undue influence, incapacity of an adult or minor grantor) pass title that is subject to rescission, and a **BFP** takes free of the right to rescind.
- **Persons authorized to execute documents**: authority of an agent to sign a deed must usually be in a **writing** (equal dignities rule) in states that retain it; powers of attorney, corporate officers, trustees and personal representatives must have actual authority; public records and recorded power of attorney matter. Spousal joinder in some states for homestead/dower.
- **Types of deeds and covenants**: **general warranty deed** — six covenants: **present** covenants: (1) **seisin** (grantor owns the estate conveyed), (2) **right to convey**, (3) against **encumbrances**; **future** covenants: (4) **warranty** (grantor will defend against lawful claims), (5) **quiet enjoyment** (no eviction by paramount title), (6) **further assurances**. **Special warranty deed** (statutory in many states, e.g., Texas): covenants only against the **grantor's own acts**. **Quitclaim deed**: no covenants; conveys whatever interest the grantor has.
- **Present covenants**: breached at **delivery** (if at all); the statute of limitations runs from delivery; the **majority rule: they do not run with the land**, so a remote grantee cannot sue the original grantor on them; the covenant against encumbrances is breached by mortgages, liens, easements and restrictions that existed at delivery. **Future covenants**: **run with the land**; breached upon **actual or constructive eviction** by paramount title; the cause of action accrues then. **Damages**: generally limited to the purchase price paid (many states: price paid by the plaintiff's grantor or the covenantee) plus interest and costs; no benefit-of-the-bargain damages **[UNCERTAIN: remote-grantee measure varies]**.
- **Estoppel by deed (after-acquired title)**: if a grantor conveys by a **warranty deed** land the grantor does not own, and later acquires title, title passes to the grantee automatically (the grantor is estopped to deny the deed). A **quitclaim** deed does not carry after-acquired title (the grantor purported to convey only what he then had), unless the deed's language shows otherwise. As against a later BFP who relies on the record, the first deed may be "wild" and may not bind (Module 29).
- **Delivery and the recording act**: an undelivered deed that is recorded does not pass title (Module 29).

**Traps**: oral conditions on delivery to grantee are ignored; no right of recall; consideration not required; present vs future covenants; remote grantee can sue on future covenants only; quitclaim = no covenants and no after-acquired title; a deed by a minor or an incompetent is voidable, a forged deed is void.

**One-breath lines**
- "Future covenants travel with the land; present covenants stay with the first buyer."
- "Hand over the deed with strings attached and the strings fall off."

**Fact patterns**

**P27-1 [MBE-style].** Owner signs a deed to Ana and hands it to a friend, Fran, saying, "Give this to Ana when I die, but I can change my mind and ask for it back." Owner dies. Fran delivers the deed to Ana. Owner's will leaves Blackacre to Ben. Who owns Blackacre?
(A) Ana, because the deed was delivered. (B) Ben, because Owner retained the right to recall the deed, so there was no present delivery, and the deed fails as a will. (C) Ana and Ben, as tenants in common. (D) Owner's heirs.
**Best answer: B.** Retention of control means no delivery; the attempted transfer at death is testamentary and fails for lack of will formalities. Wrong: A, no present delivery; C, no; D, Ben takes under the valid will.

**P27-2 [MBE-style].** Owner signs and hands a deed for Blackacre to Ana, saying, "This is yours only if you graduate from law school." Ana never graduates. Owner sues for the property. Owner will
(A) prevail; the delivery was conditional. (B) lose; delivery to the grantee is unconditional and the oral condition cannot vary the deed. (C) prevail; the deed lacks consideration. (D) lose because a deed cannot contain conditions.
**Best answer: B.** Delivery to the grantee with an oral condition is absolute. Wrong: A, parol evidence rule bars the condition; C, consideration is not required; D, a deed may state conditions in its text.

**P27-3 [MBE-style].** Owner signs a deed to Ana but keeps it in his desk. Thief steals the deed, forges nothing, and gives it to Cal, who records it and sells Blackacre to Dee, who pays full value and has no notice. Who owns Blackacre?
(A) Dee, as a BFP. (B) Owner, because the deed was never delivered and is void. (C) Ana, as the named grantee. (D) Cal.
**Best answer: B.** An undelivered deed is void; recording acts and BFP status do not cure it. Wrong: A, a BFP takes nothing from a void deed; C, no delivery to Ana; D, no title.

**P27-4 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre to Ana by a general warranty deed. In fact, Xavier owns Blackacre. Ana sells to Ben by general warranty deed. Xavier evicts Ben. Ben sues Owner. Which claim can Ben bring?
(A) The covenant of seisin. (B) The covenants of warranty and quiet enjoyment, which run with the land. (C) The covenant against encumbrances. (D) None; Ben did not deal with Owner.
**Best answer: B.** Future covenants run to remote grantees and are breached on eviction. Wrong: A and C, present covenants generally do not run (majority); D, the future covenants give a claim.

**P27-5 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre to Ana by a general warranty deed, although Xavier, not Owner, owns Blackacre. Two years later Owner purchases Blackacre from Xavier. Disregarding any later purchaser, as between Owner and Ana, who owns Blackacre?
(A) Ana, because Owner's after-acquired title passes to her under the warranty deed. (B) Owner, because the first deed was void when made. (C) Xavier, who never conveyed to Ana. (D) Owner and Ana as tenants in common.
**Best answer: A.** Estoppel by deed: a grantor who conveys with warranty land he does not own is estopped to deny the grantee's title when he later acquires it. Wrong: B, the deed was not void as between the parties; C, Xavier conveyed to Owner; D, no cotenancy. (How a later BFP from Owner fares depends on the recording act and chain of title, Module 29.)

**P27-6 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre by deed to Ana. The deed contains no covenants and recites "Owner quitclaims all of Owner's interest." Owner owns nothing. Two years later Owner buys Blackacre from the true owner. Ana claims title. Ana will
(A) prevail; after-acquired title passes. (B) lose; a quitclaim conveys only the grantor's existing interest and does not pass after-acquired title. (C) prevail because quitclaim deeds are warranted. (D) lose because Owner never delivered.
**Best answer: B.** Wrong: A and C; D, nothing says no delivery.

### MODULE 28. Transfer by operation of law and by will: intestacy, ademption, exoneration, lapse (V.C; MBE only)

**Rules**
- **Intestacy**: real property passes to the decedent's heirs under the state statute of descent (spouse, descendants, parents, siblings, remote kin; order and shares vary by state; many follow the Uniform Probate Code). **Real property is governed by the law of the situs**. Heirs take at the decedent's death (title vests in the heirs subject to administration).
- **Wills**: attested writing required; a will may devise land. A **specific devise** (a particular parcel) vs **general** vs **residuary**.
- **Ademption by extinction**: if the specifically devised property is **not in the testator's estate at death** (sold, destroyed, given away), the devise **fails**; the devisee takes nothing (the traditional **identity** theory; the modern/UPC approach (UPC § 2-606) allows the devisee to take any unpaid balance of the purchase price, insurance proceeds, a replacement or condemnation award, unless the testator's intent is contrary).
- **Lapse and anti-lapse**: if a devisee **predeceases the testator**, the gift lapses. Absent a contrary will provision, the lapsed gift falls into the **residue**; a lapsed residuary gift passes by intestacy (the old "no residue of a residue" rule) or, in modern law, to the remaining residuary devisees. **Anti-lapse statutes** save the gift for the **devisee's descendants** (in many states only if the devisee is a descendant of the testator, or of the testator's grandparents, e.g., UPC § 2-603), unless the will shows a contrary intent. A gift to a **class** (children of A) is not lapsed by one member's death if the statute treats it as saved by the survivors.
- **Exoneration**: at common law a specific devisee could demand that the estate pay off a mortgage on the devised land (exoneration of the specific devise). **Modern/UPC § 2-607**: **no exoneration**; the devisee takes the land **subject to the mortgage** unless the will says otherwise.
- **Equitable conversion** at death: see Module 17.
- Related modern devices: transfer-on-death (beneficiary) deeds in many states (Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act); joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety survivorship (Module 4).

**Traps**: ademption applies only to specific devises, not to general devises of money; anti-lapse only saves certain relatives; exoneration is abolished in modern law.

**Fact patterns**

**P28-1 [MBE-style].** Testator's will devises "my farm, Blackacre, to Ann" and "all the rest of my property to Ben." After executing the will, Testator sells Blackacre for $300,000, deposits the proceeds in a savings account, and dies. Applying the traditional rule (identity theory of ademption), who takes the $300,000?
(A) Ann. (B) Ben. (C) Testator's heirs. (D) Ann and Ben equally.
**Best answer: B.** The specific devise adeemed; the proceeds are part of the residue. Wrong: A, D, modern/UPC rules would not give Ann a bank account; C, there is a residuary clause.

**P28-2 [MBE-style].** Testator's will devises "Blackacre to my brother Bob" and "the residue to Cy." Bob predeceases Testator, leaving a daughter, Dee. The jurisdiction has an anti-lapse statute covering devises to the testator's siblings and their descendants. Who takes Blackacre?
(A) Cy. (B) Dee. (C) Testator's heirs. (D) Bob's estate.
**Best answer: B.** The statute saves the gift for Bob's descendant. Wrong: A, absent the statute the lapsed gift falls into the residue; C and D, no.

**P28-3 [MBE-style].** Testator's will devises Blackacre, which is subject to a $100,000 mortgage, to Ann, and the residue to Ben. The will does not mention the mortgage. Testator dies. Under the modern/UPC rule, Ann takes Blackacre
(A) free of the mortgage; Testator's estate must pay it. (B) subject to the mortgage. (C) only if she assumes it in writing. (D) free of the mortgage, if Ben consents.
**Best answer: B.** Exoneration has been abolished. Wrong: A, common law exoneration; C and D, no.

### MODULE 29. Recording acts, indexes, chain of title, hidden risks (V.D.1)

**Rules**
- **Common-law baseline**: first in time, first in right. Recording acts protect **bona fide purchasers** (BFPs) from prior **unrecorded** interests. Applies to deeds, mortgages, easements, options, leases beyond a certain term, and other interests in land.
- **Types**:
  - **Pure race**: the first to **record** wins, regardless of notice. (A small number of jurisdictions; **[UNCERTAIN list]** Louisiana is a civil-law public-records system; North Carolina and Delaware are often listed as race jurisdictions.)
  - **Notice**: a subsequent **BFP without notice** (for value and without notice at the time of purchase) wins over a prior unrecorded interest, **whether or not she records first**; recording still protects against later purchasers.
  - **Race-notice**: a subsequent BFP without notice wins **only if she records before** the prior grantee records.
  - Most jurisdictions are notice or race-notice; the facts will say which (**[UNCERTAIN count]**).
- **Who is protected**: a **purchaser for valuable consideration (not nominal)** or a mortgagee; **donees, heirs, and devisees are not** BFPs; **creditors** are protected only if the statute so provides (judgment creditors are often included).
- **Notice**: (1) **actual**; (2) **record** (constructive) notice of instruments properly recorded **in the chain of title**; (3) **inquiry notice**: facts that would lead a reasonable purchaser to inquire (possession by someone other than the seller, visible easements, references in the chain such as a recital of an unrecorded interest, or a gap in the chain).
- **Shelter rule**: a person who takes from a BFP acquires the BFP's rights, even with notice of the earlier unrecorded deed; protects the BFP's ability to sell. (The original grantor cannot re-acquire and use the shelter.)
- **Indexes**: **grantor-grantee index** (the majority; search by the names of parties) and **tract index** (by parcel). Searchers must trace each grantor from the time that grantor acquired title until the grantor conveyed. **Chain of title**: unbroken links of record from the root of title to the present owner.
- **Wild deed**: a recorded deed that is not connected to the chain of title (e.g., O to A unrecorded, then A to B recorded) does **not** give constructive notice to a later purchaser from O.
- **Hidden risks (not protected by the recording act)**: **forged deeds** (void); **undelivered deeds** (void); **fraud in the factum**; **deeds by minors/incompetents** (voidable; BFP protected); **adverse possession**; **prescriptive easements**; **rights of persons in possession**; **unrecorded easements/covenants visible on inspection**; **liens** for taxes; **mechanics' liens**; marital rights; **lost or unknown heirs**; **mistakes in the record**; errors in indexing; **unacknowledged instruments** (not entitled to record in many states; their recording generally gives no constructive notice); and **competing claims by impaired capacity**.
- **Mistakes by the recorder / misindexing**: **courts split**: the majority rule that a deed properly delivered to the recorder is "recorded" even if misindexed (so the risk of clerk error falls on the later purchaser) is contested; some states (e.g., New York, Maryland per cited cases) treat the index as part of the record, so a misindexed instrument gives no constructive notice. **[UNCERTAIN: marked as a "testable jurisdictional split" candidate; NextGen lists "issues related to mistakes by the recorder"; the question will state the rule.]**
- **Marketable record title acts** (some states): after 30-40 years, old interests not re-recorded are extinguished.
- **Quitclaim grantees**: a purchaser by quitclaim is a BFP in most states; a minority treats the quitclaim as notice of a defect **[UNCERTAIN]**.
- **Mortgages**: recording acts apply; a PMM and priority rules in Module 25.
- **Timing**: notice is judged at the time of purchase and payment.

**Traps**: notice statute protects the BFP even if she records late; race-notice requires recording first; donees aren't protected; inquiry notice from possession; wild deed/chain; recording does not validate a void deed; the shelter rule helps the one who takes from a BFP.

**One-breath lines**
- "Notice: no notice, you win. Race-notice: no notice and first to the record office. Race: first to the record office."
- "Recording protects buyers, not thieves' deeds."

**Fact patterns**

**P29-1 [MBE-style].** Owner conveys Blackacre to Ana for value; Ana does not record. Owner then conveys Blackacre to Ben for value; Ben has no notice of the earlier conveyance. Ana records first; Ben then records. In which kinds of jurisdictions will Ana prevail?
(A) Notice jurisdictions only. (B) Race and race-notice jurisdictions only. (C) Notice and race-notice jurisdictions only. (D) All three kinds.
**Best answer: B.** Ana recorded first, so she wins in a pure race and in a race-notice jurisdiction (Ben did not record first); under a notice statute Ben, a BFP without notice at purchase, wins. Wrong: A, reversed; C, race-notice favors the first to record; D, notice favors Ben.

**P29-2 [MBE-style].** In a race-notice jurisdiction, Owner conveys Blackacre to Ana, who does not record but moves in and farms the land. Owner then conveys Blackacre to Ben for value; Ben never visits the land and records his deed. Ben had no actual knowledge of Ana. Who owns Blackacre?
(A) Ben, because he recorded first. (B) Ana, because Ben had inquiry notice from Ana's visible possession. (C) Ben, because Ana did not record. (D) Ana, because she paid first.
**Best answer: B.** A reasonable purchaser would have inspected; Ben is charged with inquiry notice and is not a BFP. Wrong: A and C, ignore notice; D, order of payment is irrelevant.

**P29-3 [MBE-style].** In a notice jurisdiction, Owner conveys Blackacre to Ana (unrecorded). Owner conveys Blackacre to Ben for value, without notice, and Ben records. Ben then conveys to Cal, who knows of Ana's unrecorded deed. Who owns Blackacre?
(A) Ana. (B) Cal, under the shelter rule. (C) Ben. (D) Owner.
**Best answer: B.** Cal takes Ben's BFP status; Ben could convey good title. Wrong: A, Ana's claim was cut off by Ben; C, Ben conveyed; D, Owner conveyed to Ben.

**P29-4 [MBE-style].** In a race-notice jurisdiction, Owner conveys Blackacre to Ana, who does not record. Ana conveys to Ben, who records. Owner then conveys Blackacre to Cal, who pays value, has no actual knowledge of Ana or Ben, and records after searching Owner's grantor index. The grantor-grantee index shows no conveyance by Owner. Who owns Blackacre?
(A) Ben, who recorded the deed from Ana. (B) Cal, because Ben's recorded deed lies outside Cal's chain of title and gave no notice. (C) Ana, because she bought first. (D) Owner.
**Best answer: B.** Ben's deed from Ana is a wild deed because Ana's deed from Owner was never recorded; Cal is a BFP who records. Wrong: A, a wild deed gives no constructive notice; C, Ana never recorded; D, Owner conveyed to Cal.

**P29-5 [MBE-style].** Owner mortgages Blackacre to Bank 1, which does not record. Owner then gives a mortgage to Bank 2, which lends in good faith without notice and records. Bank 1 then records. In a race-notice jurisdiction, which mortgage has priority?
(A) Bank 1, because it came first. (B) Bank 2, because it was a BFP that recorded first. (C) Both pro rata. (D) Bank 1, because it recorded second but first in time.
**Best answer: B.** Wrong: A, D, ignore the recording act; C, no.

**P29-6 [MBE-style].** In a notice jurisdiction, Owner conveys Blackacre to Ana for value (unrecorded). Owner then makes a gift of Blackacre to Ben, who records. Ana later sues. Who owns Blackacre?
(A) Ben, who recorded. (B) Ana, because Ben gave no value. (C) Ben, because Ana did not record. (D) Owner.
**Best answer: B.** Donees are not protected. Wrong: A and C; D, Owner conveyed.

**P29-7 [MBE-style].** Forger signs Owner's name to a deed of Blackacre to Cal. Cal records the deed and sells Blackacre to Dee, a BFP who pays full value and has no notice. In any recording-act jurisdiction, who owns Blackacre?
(A) Dee, as a BFP. (B) Owner, because a forged deed is void and conveys no title. (C) Cal. (D) Forger.
**Best answer: B.** Recording acts do not protect against void deeds. Wrong: A, C, D.

**P29-8 [MBE-style].** In a jurisdiction whose statute entitles only acknowledged instruments to be recorded, Owner conveys Blackacre to Ana by a deed that is not acknowledged. The recorder nevertheless records it. Owner then conveys to Ben for value, without actual notice; Ben records. In the majority view, who owns Blackacre?
(A) Ana, because her deed was recorded first. (B) Ben, because the recording of an unacknowledged deed gives no constructive notice. (C) Ana, because Ben could have found the deed. (D) Owner.
**Best answer: B.** The recording of an unacknowledged instrument is ineffective as constructive notice (majority; state statutes vary). Wrong: A and C, as to constructive notice; D. **[UNCERTAIN: state-specific statutes; mark `status: "general"`.]**

### MODULE 30. Title insurance (V.D.2; MBE only)

**Rules**
- **Title insurance** is a contract of **indemnity** against loss from title defects existing at the **policy date** (and described in coverage); the insurer defends covered claims and pays loss up to the policy limit. **Owner's policy** protects the buyer; **lender's policy** protects the mortgagee.
- **Not covered**: matters **excluded** by the policy (standard exclusions: governmental regulation/zoning, eminent domain, matters known to the insured but not disclosed to the insurer, matters **created or agreed to by the insured**, matters arising **after** the policy date), and **standard exceptions** in a standard-coverage policy (survey matters/boundary discrepancies, rights of parties in possession, unrecorded easements/liens, taxes and assessments); **extended coverage** (with survey and inspection) removes some of the standard exceptions.
- **Search**: the policy is issued after a title search (an abstract or commitment); the policy covers hidden risks such as forgery and undelivered deeds only if the policy does not exclude them.
- **Contract implications**: a contract for "marketable" title is not satisfied by a title insurance policy (Module 16); a contract for "insurable" title is.
- NextGen does not list title insurance in its scope.

**Fact patterns**

**P30-1 [MBE-style].** Buyer purchases Blackacre and obtains an owner's title insurance policy. A year later, Buyer grants Neighbor an easement across Blackacre. Neighbor's claim later reduces the property's value, and Buyer files a claim under the policy. The insurer will
(A) pay; the easement burdens the title. (B) deny; the easement was created by the insured after the policy date and is excluded. (C) pay because title insurance covers all defects. (D) pay only if Neighbor recorded.
**Best answer: B.** Matters created or agreed to by the insured are not covered. Wrong: A, C, D.

**P30-2 [MBE-style].** A year after Buyer's purchase and owner's title insurance, a forged deed in Seller's chain of title (before the policy date) surfaces and the true owner sues. The policy has no exclusion for forgery. The insurer
(A) has no duty to defend, because the defect is in the past. (B) must defend and indemnify covered loss up to the policy limit. (C) must pay the full market value regardless of the limit. (D) is liable only if Buyer searched the records.
**Best answer: B.** Title insurance covers hidden defects that existed on the policy date. Wrong: A, C, D.

### MODULE 31. Special problems: judgment liens, tax liens, estoppel by deed (V.E)

**Rules**
- **Judgment lien**: a statutory lien on the judgment debtor's real property in the county where the judgment is **docketed/recorded** (some states require a recorded abstract); the lien attaches to land owned at that time and, in many states, to **after-acquired** land; priority is by time of docketing, subject to the recording act and to purchase-money mortgages (Module 25); a **BFP** who bought and paid before the docketing takes free; the lien usually lasts for a period (e.g., 10 years) unless renewed; the creditor can enforce by execution sale.
- **Tax liens**: **real property taxes** create a lien with **superpriority** over prior private liens, including recorded mortgages; the property may be sold at a **tax sale** (due process notice to mortgagees is required). **Federal tax liens** (IRC § 6321) attach to all of the taxpayer's property but are **not valid against purchasers, holders of security interests, mechanic's lienors and judgment lien creditors until notice is filed** (IRC § 6323(a)) **[VERIFY]**.
- **Estoppel by deed**: Module 27. **Estoppel by judgment** (res judicata): a judgment determining title binds parties and privies; a quiet-title action binds those joined.
- **Mechanics' liens**: statutory liens for unpaid contractors; relation back to commencement of work in many states.
- **Marital and homestead interests**: dower/curtesy abolished in most states; homestead and elective share may still require joinder; in a TBE both spouses sign.

**Fact pattern**

**P31-1 [MBE-style].** Bank holds a recorded first mortgage on Blackacre. The owner then fails to pay the county's real estate taxes for two years. In jurisdictions where unpaid real property taxes create a lien with statutory priority over all other liens, the county's tax lien
(A) is junior to the earlier recorded mortgage. (B) has priority over Bank's mortgage despite Bank's earlier recording. (C) is equal to the mortgage. (D) is valid only if Bank consents.
**Best answer: B.** Tax liens are superpriority by statute. Wrong: A, C, D.

## 3. Cross-cutting traps (a "trap list" the app can turn into guess-first openers)

1. **Read the grammar of the conveyance**: "so long as" (determinable), "but if" (condition subsequent / executory), "then to B" after a fee (executory), "then to B's children" with no children yet (contingent).
2. **Remedy decides the covenant rule**: money = real covenant (privity); injunction = equitable servitude (notice).
3. **Time of testing**: RAP is tested at creation; marketability at closing; notice at purchase; severance when the act occurs; delivery on the grantor's intent when the deed leaves the grantor's control.
4. **Void vs voidable**: forged, undelivered, nonexistent grantee = void; fraud in the inducement, incapacity, duress = voidable (BFP takes free).
5. **Who is a BFP**: value (not nominal), no notice (actual, record, inquiry); donees and heirs do not count.
6. **Recording act type**: notice (late recorder can still win), race-notice (first to record wins among those without notice), race (first to record wins).
7. **Assume the majority/modern rule unless the stem says otherwise**: lien theory, mitigation, habitability, American rule, objective hostility, no self-help eviction.
8. **Privity map for leases**: original tenant (contract + estate until assignment; contract after), assignee (estate; contract only if assumed), sublessee (neither).
9. **Equitable conversion** answers four questions: who bears risk (buyer), who gets the money if seller dies (residuary), who owns the land if buyer dies (devisee of the land), what happens to a joint tenancy (severance).
10. **Foreclosure waterfall**: costs, foreclosing mortgage, juniors in order, owner; omitted juniors survive; seniors survive junior foreclosures.
11. **Federal overlay**: Fair Housing Act exemptions never excuse discriminatory ads (§ 3604(c)); § 1982 has no exemptions for race; Garn-St Germain protects certain transfers from due-on-sale.
12. **Two-step easement analysis**: how created (express, implication, prescription, estoppel), then scope; termination needs intent or a specific event (nonuse alone is not enough).
