### 0.2 MBE format [NCBE: ncbex.org/exams/mbe]
- 200 four-option multiple-choice questions, 175 scored + 25 unscored pretest, two 3-hour sessions of 100.
- Seven subjects, **25 scored questions each**: Civil Procedure, Constitutional Law, Contracts, Criminal Law and Procedure, Evidence, Real Property, Torts. (Torts is 25/175, about 14.3% of the MBE.)
- Instruction text: "choose the best answer from the four stated alternatives"; no penalty for wrong answers; questions are answered "under generally accepted fundamental legal principles unless the question states otherwise" [NCBE: preparing-mbe page].
- Next MBE dates on the NCBE site: Feb 24, 2027; July 28, 2027; Feb 23, 2028.

### 0.3 Official Torts outline (MBE Subject Matter Outline, copyright 2020, dated 11/16/20) [NCBE, read in full]
**Headline NOTE (the most important sentence in the whole outline):**
> Examinees are to assume that survival actions and claims for wrongful death are available. **Joint and several liability and pure comparative fault are the relevant rules unless otherwise indicated.** Approximately half of the Torts questions on the MBE will be based on category II [Negligence], and approximately half on categories I, III and IV.

Outline:

- **I. Intentional torts**
  - A. Harms to the person (assault, battery, false imprisonment, infliction of mental distress) and harms to property interests (trespass to land and chattels, conversion).
  - B. Defenses to claims for physical harms: 1. consent; 2. privileges and immunities (protection of self and others; protection of property interests; parental discipline; protection of public interests; necessity; incomplete privilege).
- **II. Negligence**
  - A. The duty question, including failure to act, unforeseeable plaintiffs, obligations to control the conduct of third parties.
  - B. Standard of care: 1. reasonably prudent person incl. children, physically and mentally impaired, professionals, other special classes; 2. rules of conduct from statutes and custom.
  - C. Problems of proof of fault, incl. res ipsa loquitur.
  - D. Causation: 1. but-for and substantial causes; 2. harms traceable to multiple causes; 3. apportionment of responsibility among multiple tortfeasors, incl. joint and several liability.
  - E. Limitations on liability and special rules: 1. remote/unforeseeable causes, legal or proximate cause, superseding causes; 2. owners and occupiers of land; 3. mental distress not arising from physical harm, other intangible injuries; 4. pure economic loss.
  - F. Liability for acts of others: 1. employees and other agents; 2. independent contractors and nondelegable duties.
  - G. Defenses: 1. contributory fault (common law contributory negligence, last clear chance, forms of comparative negligence); 2. assumption of risk.
- **III. Strict liability and products liability:** common law strict liability incl. abnormally dangerous activities, and defenses; claims against manufacturers and other defendants arising from manufacture and distribution of products, and defenses.
- **IV. Other torts:** A. nuisance and defenses; B. defamation and invasion of privacy, defenses, **constitutional limitations**; C. misrepresentation and defenses; D. intentional interference with business relations and defenses.

**Derived (not official) per-category counts.** NCBE only says "about half" for category II. Taking 25 questions: about 12-13 negligence, and the other 12-13 spread over I, III, IV (about 4 each). Third-party sites (jdadvising.com) print the same 12-13 / 4-5 / 4-5 / 4-5 split but cite only the outline, so it is arithmetic from the NCBE sentence, not independent frequency data. **I found no official per-topic frequency table.** Do not print any "X% of questions are about Y" claim in the app beyond "about half of Torts is negligence" [NCBE]. The jdadvising claim that defamation, privacy and nuisance are the most-tested "other torts" and that battery/assault/false imprisonment/IIED are the most-tested intentional torts is [SEC, source unknown]; treat as a hint for ordering, not as a statistic.

### 0.5 Rules vs Restatements: how the MBE actually decides
- NCBE's MEE analyses cite **Restatement (Second) of Torts** and **Restatement (Third)** side by side, plus Kionka's *Torts in a Nutshell*, e.g. the July 2015 torts analysis cites Rest. 2d Torts §§ 283A, 329, 333, 339, 342, 324 and Rest. 3d §§ 10 cmt. f and 42, and Feb 2015 cites Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07 and Rest. 2d Agency §§ 219, 220, 228 [NCBE, read]. So the safe approach: teach the rule both Restatements agree on, and flag where they diverge (landowner categories; design-defect test; scope of risk).
- When the examiners say "in most jurisdictions" they mean it. Example from that MEE analysis: "about half of the states have modified the [land-entrant] classifications ... only a handful have fully abolished the categories; most have retained the trespasser status" [NCBE, Kionka cited]. This tells us the **MBE-safe default for landowners is still the traditional trespasser/licensee/invitee scheme** unless the question states the jurisdiction has adopted a general reasonable-care standard.
- Contributory-negligence jurisdictions per NCBE: **AL, DC, MD, NC, VA** [NCBE MEE July 2015 analysis]. (LII Wex agrees: Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia + DC.)

## 2. INTENTIONAL TORTS (MBE category I; about 4 of 25 questions by arithmetic)

### 2.1 Intent (applies to all of them)
- **Intent = purpose to bring about the result, OR knowledge to a substantial certainty that it will occur.** [NCBE NextGen text names "knowledge to a substantial certainty, transferred intent, the distinction between intent and motive, and who can form intent (e.g., minor children)"]. Rest. 2d §8A. Garratt v. Dailey (child pulls chair) is the standard illustration [SEC].
- **Motive is irrelevant** (a "joke" or kindness does not negate intent). **Mistake of fact is no defense** to the intent element (belief the land was yours; belief it was your own chattel) [SEC; Rest.].
- **Children and the mentally ill** can form tortious intent if they have the capacity to intend the contact/entry; no special age cutoff [NCBE NextGen text; SEC]. Insanity is generally no defense to intentional torts [SEC].
- **Transferred intent** applies among the five "classic" torts: assault, battery, false imprisonment, trespass to land, trespass to chattels (victim to victim and tort to tort). It does NOT make conversion or IIED transferable [SEC; Rest. 2d §§16, 20-21; I have not opened Rest. text]. [UNSURE on edge cases such as IIED.]
- **Single vs dual intent for battery [UNSURE]:** the common MBE-level statement is "intent to cause a harmful OR offensive contact (or imminent apprehension of one)." Whether the intended contact must itself be harmful/offensive (dual intent, Rest. 2d §13; also Rest. 3d Intentional Torts to Persons, which ALI approved May 16, 2021) or merely a contact (single intent, the Vosburg line) is a doctrinal split; MBE questions avoid it. In our items always make the defendant's purpose obviously harmful/offensive or plainly substantially certain.
- Rest. 3d Torts: Intentional Torts to Persons: ALI members approved Tentative Draft No. 6 on 2021-05-16 [ali.org news]; adds "purposeful infliction of bodily harm", a broader false-imprisonment definition (reaches confinement by mere assertion of legal authority) and updated privileges. NCBE outlines still speak the traditional language. Teach the traditional rules.

### 2.2 Torts to the person

**Battery** (Rest. 2d §§13, 18): (1) act (2) intending to cause harmful or offensive contact (or imminent apprehension thereof) with the plaintiff's person (3) causing such contact. Offensive = would offend a reasonable sense of personal dignity (objective). "Person" extends to things so closely attached that touching them is touching the person (cup in hand, clothing worn, a chair someone sits on) [SEC; Fisher v. Carrousel Motor Hotel is the usual case, not opened]. Plaintiff need not be aware of the contact (asleep or unconscious is still battery). No physical injury required.

**Assault** (Rest. 2d §21): (1) act (2) intending to cause harmful/offensive contact or imminent apprehension of it (3) causing the plaintiff's **reasonable apprehension of imminent** harmful/offensive contact. Needs plaintiff's awareness. Words alone usually don't suffice (words can also negate: "I would hit you if the judge weren't here" = no assault). Future threats not imminent. Apprehension is not fear: knowing contact is coming (even if brave) suffices. Apparent ability counts: an unloaded gun that looks loaded can support assault [SEC].

**False imprisonment** (Rest. 2d §35): (1) act or omission (2) intending to confine (3) causing confinement within boundaries fixed by the defendant (4) plaintiff **aware of the confinement or harmed by it**. Means of confinement: physical barriers; physical force or threat of force (to person, or immediate threat to family/property); **invalid assertion of legal authority**; duty to release but omission. NOT confinement: moral pressure; threats of future harm; economic pressure. A reasonable, known means of escape defeats confinement (not reasonable if dangerous, humiliating, or damaging property) [SEC]. **Shopkeeper's privilege** (named in NextGen scope; common law/statute): reasonable grounds to suspect theft + reasonable manner + reasonable time [SEC]; applies even if the person is innocent.

**IIED** (Rest. 2d §46): (1) extreme and outrageous conduct (2) intent or recklessness (3) severe emotional distress (4) caused. No physical impact or symptoms required; severity is required. Outrageousness: "beyond all possible bounds of decency." Insults, indignities, threats rarely suffice; factors: abuse of a position of power, knowledge of special vulnerability, repetition, common carriers/innkeepers. **Third-party (bystander) IIED:** plaintiff must be present when it occurs; for non-family bystanders also bodily harm (Rest. 2d §46(2)) [SEC]. **Constitutional limits:** public figures cannot recover IIED for a parody/publication unless it contains a false statement of fact made with actual malice (Hustler v. Falwell, 485 U.S. 46 (1988)) [SCOTUS, holding text read]. Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443 (2011) (funeral picketing on matters of public concern, IIED claim barred by the First Amendment) [from memory; I could not fetch the opinion: UNSURE].

### 2.3 Torts to property

**Trespass to land** (Rest. 2d §158): intentional physical invasion of land in another's possession. Intent only to be on the land (not to trespass). Includes causing a thing or third person to enter, remaining after license ends, failing to remove a thing one must remove. No harm needed (nominal damages). Mistake as to ownership or boundary is no defense. Intangible invasions (smoke, noise, odor, light) are traditionally nuisance, not trespass [SEC]. Only the possessor sues for present possession; an owner out of possession sues only for harm to the reversion.

**Trespass to chattels** (Rest. 2d §§217-218): intentionally dispossessing another of a chattel or using/intermeddling with it. Liability requires actual harm of one of four kinds in §218: **dispossession**, impairment of condition/quality/value, deprivation of use for a substantial time, or bodily harm [REST §218 from my memory; secondary source dropped "dispossession": UNSURE]. Damages: lost use or reduction in value/repair cost, not nominal.

**Conversion** (Rest. 2d §222A): intentional exercise of dominion or control over a chattel that so seriously interferes with the owner's right to control it that the actor may justly be required to pay its **full value** (a "forced sale"). Factors (§222A(2)): extent and duration of control; actor's intent to assert a right inconsistent with the owner's; actor's good faith; extent and duration of interference; harm done to the chattel; inconvenience and expense caused to the owner. Good faith is no defense; a good-faith purchaser from a thief converts [SEC]. Not every minor interference is conversion. Remedy: fair market value at the time/place of conversion (or replevin).

### 2.4 Defenses and privileges (outline I.B)
- **Consent:** express or implied (by conduct or custom, including sports contact); not effective if it is exceeded in scope, obtained by fraud about the essential nature of the act or by duress, or given by someone lacking capacity. **Emergency**: consent presumed for an unconscious patient when delay would be dangerous and no representative is available [NCBE NextGen text]. Fraud about a collateral matter does not vitiate consent [SEC]. Consent to a crime: majority treats consent as ineffective for criminal-statute fights (e.g., mutual combat) vs Restatement which allows defense unless statute protects the plaintiff's class [SEC, UNSURE].
- **Self-defense:** reasonable force against a reasonably apparent imminent unlawful harm; **proportionate** (deadly force only to meet threatened deadly force / serious bodily harm); reasonable mistake is OK; initial aggressor has no privilege until withdrawal. Duty to retreat before deadly force: majority/modern and "stand your ground" statutes: none; Rest. 2d §65 requires retreat only if completely safe, never in the home [SEC; UNSURE on counts]. Not a defense to unintended injury to bystanders in negligence.
- **Defense of others:** majority/Restatement: privileged if the actor reasonably believes the third person would be privileged to defend herself (reasonable mistake protected, Rest. 2d §76). Minority "alter ego" rule: the intervenor stands in the third person's shoes [SEC; UNSURE which the MBE prefers; a stem should say].
- **Defense of property:** reasonable non-deadly force after a request to desist (unless futile or dangerous). **Never deadly force, and no deadly mechanical devices (spring guns), solely to protect property** (Katko v. Briney, Iowa 1971 [SEC]). Inside an occupied dwelling the self-defense rules govern.
- **Recapture of chattels:** reasonable non-deadly force, in fresh pursuit, after demand, only against a wrongdoer who took the property tortiously (not where owner voluntarily surrendered it, e.g., defaulting buyer). Mistake = liability. **Entry on land to retake chattels** allowed at reasonable time/manner after demand, unless the owner's own fault put the chattel there [SEC].
- **Necessity:** only against property torts (never to harm a person). **Public necessity** (to avert a community-wide disaster, e.g., blowing up houses to stop a fire): complete privilege, no liability. **Private necessity** (protect self/own property): privilege to use the property, owner may not eject you, but you **must pay for actual damage** (Vincent v. Lake Erie Transportation Co., Minn. 1910) [SEC].
- **Parental discipline:** reasonable force/confinement for a child's proper control, judged by age, offense and proportion. Schools: much narrower; many states bar corporal punishment [SEC; Ingraham v. Wright holds the Eighth Amendment does not reach school paddling, from the secondary source].
- **Arrest:** police may arrest with a warrant, for felonies on probable cause, and for misdemeanors committed in presence, with reasonable force; deadly force against a fleeing felon only if probable cause of serious physical danger (Tennessee v. Garner [SEC]). Private citizens: arrest only for a felony actually committed (mistake as to whether any felony was committed is no protection) or breach of the peace in presence [SEC; UNSURE on edge].
- **Incomplete privilege** (NCBE term): privileges that excuse the intrusion but impose a condition (private necessity: pay damages; shopkeeper: reasonable grounds/manner/time; excessive force loses protection).

`say:` candidates: "Intent means purpose or substantial certainty. Motive is a different thing." / "Assault needs apprehension of imminent contact, not just fear." / "Private necessity gives you the privilege, but you pay for the damage."

### 2.5 Item bank: intentional torts (own fact patterns; "best answer" style)

**INT-1 (transferred intent).** Marta, angry at her roommate Lena, throws a mug at Lena's head. Lena ducks, and the mug hits Omar, a guest standing behind her, bruising his arm. Omar sues Marta for battery. Will he prevail?
(A) Yes, because Marta's intent to hit Lena transfers to Omar. (B) No, because Marta did not intend to contact Omar. (C) Yes, but only if Marta knew Omar was behind Lena. (D) No, because Omar's remedy is limited to negligence.
**Best: A.** Intent to commit battery against anyone supports battery against the person actually hit. B ignores transferred intent; C adds a knowledge requirement that doesn't exist; D confuses an intentional act with negligence.

**INT-2 (children and intent).** Six-year-old Theo sees his aunt about to sit in a kitchen chair and pulls it away because he thinks it will be funny. She falls and fractures a wrist. Her claim for battery against Theo should:
(A) succeed, because Theo knew with substantial certainty she would hit the floor. (B) fail, because a child of six cannot form tortious intent. (C) fail, because Theo acted from a playful motive. (D) succeed only if Theo wanted her to break a bone.
**Best: A.** Intent is purpose or substantial certainty; motive is irrelevant, and a child who can understand the consequence is capable of intent. D demands intent to cause the actual injury.

**INT-3 (battery to a held object).** During an argument at a food truck, Rafael knocks the coffee cup out of a stranger's hand without touching her hand. She is not hurt, but is deeply insulted. Her best claim is:
(A) battery, because contact with an object closely connected to her person can be an offensive contact. (B) none, because there was no touching of her body. (C) none, because she suffered no physical injury. (D) assault only, because no contact with her body occurred.
**Best: A.** Battery protects the person's dignity, and contact with something attached to or held by the person counts. B and C add requirements (bodily touch, injury) that battery does not have. D is wrong because contact did occur.

**INT-4 (assault: imminence).** Nia, standing 200 yards across a river from Sam, shouts, "I'm going to shoot you tomorrow!" Sam is terrified and moves out of town. Is Nia liable to Sam for assault?
(A) No, because the threatened harm was not imminent. (B) Yes, because Sam was genuinely afraid. (C) Yes, because Nia intended to frighten him. (D) No, because words alone can never be assault.
**Best: A.** Assault requires reasonable apprehension of *imminent* contact. B and C ignore imminence; D overstates (words plus conduct can be assault).

**INT-5 (assault: apparent ability).** In a parking lot, Kai points an unloaded pistol at Dee and says, "Give me your keys." Dee, reasonably believing the gun is loaded, hands them over. Dee sues Kai for assault. She will:
(A) prevail, because she reasonably apprehended an imminent harmful contact. (B) not prevail, because the pistol was unloaded. (C) not prevail, because Kai never touched her. (D) prevail only if Kai intended to fire.
**Best: A.** The test is the plaintiff's reasonable apprehension, not the actual ability. C confuses assault with battery.

**INT-6 (false imprisonment: awareness).** To play a prank, security guard Hal locks the door of a storeroom, knowing janitor Ivy is inside napping. He unlocks it 20 minutes later. Ivy slept the whole time, never learned of the lock, and suffered no harm. Ivy's false imprisonment claim:
(A) fails, because she was neither aware of the confinement nor harmed by it. (B) succeeds, because Hal intended to confine her. (C) succeeds for nominal damages, because the tort is actionable per se. (D) fails, because 20 minutes is too short.
**Best: A.** Confinement requires awareness or actual harm. D is wrong: any appreciable time may suffice.

**INT-7 (shopkeeper's privilege).** A store manager sees a customer slip a candy bar into her bag and exit the checkout area without paying. The manager politely asks her into the office and holds her there for 10 minutes until police can come. She then produces a receipt showing she had paid; she is released at once. Her false imprisonment claim:
(A) fails, because the manager had reasonable grounds and detained her in a reasonable manner and for a reasonable time. (B) succeeds, because she was innocent. (C) succeeds, because the manager did not first call the police. (D) fails only if she was actually guilty.
**Best: A.** The privilege protects reasonable detention on reasonable suspicion, even if wrong. B and D wrongly make the outcome depend on actual guilt.

**INT-8 (IIED: outrageousness).** A creditor, to scare a 78-year-old widow into paying a disputed $200 bill, phones her at 3 a.m. over six nights, knowing she has a heart condition, and says, "Your house will burn." She suffers severe anxiety and vomiting for weeks. Her IIED claim against the creditor will:
(A) succeed, because the conduct was extreme and outrageous and directed at a known vulnerable person. (B) fail, because IIED requires physical impact. (C) fail, because debt collection is privileged. (D) succeed only if she can prove a physical illness.
**Best: A.** B and D add requirements IIED does not have; C: no privilege to threaten arson.

**INT-9 (IIED: insults not enough).** A supervisor, annoyed by an employee's mistakes, loudly calls him "useless" and "an idiot" twice in a staff meeting. The employee feels humiliated and loses sleep for a week. IIED claim:
(A) fails, because the conduct is not extreme and outrageous. (B) succeeds, because the supervisor intended to humiliate him. (C) succeeds, because humiliation in front of colleagues is severe distress per se. (D) fails, because employers are immune.
**Best: A.** Ordinary insults and petty indignities do not meet the outrageousness standard.

**INT-10 (trespass: mistaken boundary).** A neighbor relies on a surveyor's mistaken stake and builds a fence three feet onto Priya's lot, believing it his own. Priya sues in trespass. She will:
(A) prevail, because the neighbor intended the entry and good-faith mistake is no defense. (B) not prevail, because he acted in good faith. (C) prevail only if the fence caused her substantial damage. (D) not prevail, because the surveyor is responsible.
**Best: A.** Trespass requires intent to enter or place something on the land, not intent to trespass; nominal damages are available.

**INT-11 (trespass to chattels vs conversion).** Gus takes Hana's kayak without asking for three hours of fishing, scratches the hull (repair: $80), and returns it. Hana's best claim is:
(A) trespass to chattels, for the harm done to the kayak. (B) conversion, for the kayak's full value. (C) battery. (D) none, because the kayak was returned.
**Best: A.** Brief use and small damage = trespass to chattels. Conversion requires a serious interference warranting a forced sale. D ignores the harm.

**INT-12 (conversion: good faith).** Ines buys a bicycle for $200 from a seller who had stolen it, rides it for a month, and then refuses to return it after the true owner shows proof and demands it. The owner sues Ines for conversion. She will:
(A) lose, because good faith is not a defense to conversion. (B) win, because she paid value. (C) win, because she did not know it was stolen. (D) lose only if she knew it was stolen when she bought it.
**Best: A.** A buyer from a thief acquires no title; refusal after demand is conversion.

**INT-13 (consent exceeded; emergency exception).** Dr. Ortiz obtains consent to remove a cyst from a patient's left forearm. While the patient is anesthetized, Ortiz also removes an unrelated, benign mole from the right forearm without any urgency. Ortiz is:
(A) liable for battery as to the mole, because the procedure exceeded the scope of consent and no emergency existed. (B) not liable, because the patient was unconscious. (C) not liable, because a mole removal helps the patient. (D) liable only in negligence.
**Best: A.** (Contrast: an unconscious accident victim needing life-saving surgery with no representative available is treated as having consented.)

**INT-14 (self-defense proportion).** In a tavern, Perry lightly shoves Quill. Quill pulls a pocketknife and stabs Perry in the arm. Perry sues for battery. Quill's self-defense claim will:
(A) fail, because deadly force may not be used against nondeadly force. (B) succeed, because Perry started the fight. (C) succeed, because Quill reasonably feared more shoves. (D) succeed if Quill had no chance to retreat.
**Best: A.** Self-defense permits only force proportionate to the threat.

**INT-15 (spring gun).** Tired of break-ins at his vacant farmhouse, Wes rigs a shotgun to fire at anyone who opens the back door. A burglar opens the door and is wounded. The burglar sues Wes. Wes is:
(A) liable for battery, because deadly force may not be used to protect property. (B) not liable, because the burglar was a trespasser and criminal. (C) not liable, because he had warned of previous break-ins. (D) liable only if the burglar was a first-time offender.
**Best: A.** Katko-type rule. (The wrong answers all treat the burglar's wrongdoing as removing the limit.)

**INT-16 (private necessity).** Caught in a sudden gale, boater Dell ties his boat to Eve's private dock. The storm gouges and wrecks the dock. Eve sues Dell. She is entitled to:
(A) compensation for the actual damage to the dock. (B) nothing, because Dell's entry was privileged. (C) damages and punitive damages for trespass. (D) nothing, unless Dell was negligent in handling the lines.
**Best: A.** Private necessity privileges the entry (Eve may not cast the boat loose) but the user pays for harm caused.

## 3. NEGLIGENCE (MBE category II; about half of the 25 Torts questions) - part A: duty and standard of care

Elements: duty, breach, actual cause, proximate cause, damages (physical harm or property damage; no nominal damages) [SEC; LII]. Hand formula (United States v. Carroll Towing): breach if B < P x L; a court-made aid, not a required test [SEC/LII].

### 3.1 The duty question (outline II.A)

**Foreseeable plaintiffs / Palsgraf.** Majority (Cardozo, 4-3): duty runs only to plaintiffs within the zone of foreseeable danger created by the defendant's negligence; "negligence in the air" is not enough. Minority (Andrews dissent): duty to the world, with proximate cause as the limit. The Restatement (Third) recasts it as a **scope-of-the-risk** question under proximate cause (§29), which NextGen names [NCBE NextGen text]. For the MBE, the answer key follows "unforeseeable plaintiff = no liability." [Palsgraf facts and 4-3 vote from search summaries; Cardozo's majority/Andrews dissent from my knowledge: [REST/SEC]]. **Rescuers** are foreseeable plaintiffs ("danger invites rescue") unless the rescue is wanton [SEC].

**No duty to act (nonfeasance).** No general duty to rescue or aid. Exceptions create a duty [NCBE NextGen text: "special relationships, previous actions exacerbating a risk of harm, and voluntary undertakings"]:
1. **Special relationship** (common carrier/passenger, innkeeper/guest, business/invitee, custodian/ward, employer/employee in some cases, school/student).
2. **Defendant created or exacerbated the peril** (even innocently).
3. **Voluntary undertaking.** Rest. 3d §42 (cited in an NCBE analysis): an actor who undertakes services to reduce another's risk owes reasonable care only if (a) failure to use care **increases the risk** beyond what existed without the undertaking, or (b) the person or another **relies** on the actor's exercising care. Rest. 2d §324: one who takes charge of a helpless person must not leave him worse off. In NCBE's July 2015 MEE analysis a woman who called 911 and left owed no duty: she neither increased the risk nor induced reliance [NCBE].
Good Samaritan statutes protect volunteers from ordinary negligence; they don't create a duty [SEC].

**Duty to control third parties (outline II.A).** Generally none, except by special relationship: parent-child (knows of dangerous propensity, can control), custodian of dangerous persons, employer-employee on premises/with employer's chattels, **therapist-patient (Tarasoff)**: reasonable care to protect a **reasonably identifiable victim** from a serious danger of violence (warn or take other steps); states vary on the exact scope [SEC; Tarasoff opinion not opened]. Possessors of land/businesses may owe duties to protect lawful entrants from foreseeable third-party crime; foreseeability tests vary (prior similar incidents; totality of circumstances; balancing) [SEC]. **Negligent entrustment** (supplying a car, gun to someone known or likely to misuse it) is the supplier's own negligence [SEC]. **Dram shop**: common law no liability for serving alcohol; many states have dram-shop statutes; **social hosts** mostly not liable to adult guests' victims [SEC]. These are state-law rules the MBE will not make you guess: the stem will say if a statute exists.

**Owners and occupiers of land (outline II.E.2; NextGen: "natural vs artificial conditions", "traditional and modern approaches")**

Traditional status-based scheme (still the MBE-safe default when the question says nothing; NCBE's own analysis says "most [states] have retained the trespasser status and the rules that go with it" [NCBE MEE July 2015]):

| Entrant | Duty on conditions | Key authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Undiscovered/unforeseeable trespasser** | No duty to make safe or warn; only refrain from willful/wanton injury | Rest. 2d §333 [NCBE] |
| **Discovered or anticipated (known) trespasser** | Warn of/make safe **known, concealed, artificial, highly dangerous** conditions (and reasonable care in active operations) | Rest. 2d §§334-337 [SEC; I did not open] |
| **Child trespasser (attractive nuisance)** | Reasonable care to eliminate dangerous **artificial** condition if all five: (a) possessor knows/has reason to know children likely to trespass; (b) knows/should know condition poses unreasonable risk of death/serious bodily harm; (c) children because of youth don't discover it or realize the risk; (d) utility of condition and burden of fixing are slight compared with risk; (e) fails to use reasonable care | Rest. 2d §339 [NCBE MEE, quoted in the analysis] |
| **Licensee** (enters with possessor's consent for own purpose, **includes social guests**) | Warn of/make safe **known** hidden dangers the licensee is unlikely to discover. No duty to inspect | Rest. 2d §342 [NCBE MEE] |
| **Invitee** (business visitors; public invitees on land open to public) | Reasonable care incl. **inspect** to discover hidden dangers, then repair/warn | Rest. 2d §343 [SEC] |

- The MEE analysis shows the examiners expect you to see **classification** as the pivot: a boy on a hidden logging trail might be a licensee (implied consent from a path; sign was snow-covered) or a trespasser, with attractive nuisance unlikely where the child was doing an adult activity (snowmobiling) [NCBE]. "A trespasser is one who enters or remains upon land without a privilege" (Rest. 2d §329) [NCBE].
- **Scope-of-invitation:** an invitee who goes where the invitation doesn't reach is a licensee/trespasser there [SEC].
- **Active operations** (driving, machinery): duty of reasonable care to all lawfully present, and known trespassers [SEC].
- **Open and obvious dangers:** traditionally no duty to warn; modern/Rest. 2d §343A: still liable if harm is foreseeable despite obviousness (distraction, no alternative); in comparative-fault states, obviousness often goes to comparative fault [SEC].
- **Modern approach:** California's *Rowland v. Christian* (1968) replaced the licensee/invitee distinction with reasonable care under the circumstances; **about half of the states** have modified or abolished the classifications; **only a handful** fully abolished all categories, including trespassers [NCBE MEE: Kionka]. Rest. 3d (2010) §51: reasonable care to all entrants except **flagrant trespassers** (§52: only refrain from willful/wanton harm, plus help to imperiled helpless trespassers) [SEC via search summary of the 2009 draft; verify final numbering: UNSURE].
- **Off-premises:** possessor must use reasonable care re artificial conditions that endanger people on adjacent public ways; **natural conditions** traditionally no duty (urban decayed trees: many courts now impose one) [SEC].
- **Landlords:** traditionally not liable for leased premises except: common areas, concealed known defects, premises leased for public admission, negligent repairs, promise to repair; modern trend to reasonable care (Sargent v. Ross, N.H. 1973) [SEC].
- **Firefighter's/police rule:** professional rescuers can't recover for the negligence that created the very need for their services (exceptions for independent negligence, hidden dangers, reckless conduct); some states abolished [SEC].
- **Recreational use statutes:** free public recreational use = no duty except willful/malicious failure to warn [SEC; state-specific].

**Negligent infliction of emotional distress (outline II.E.3; NextGen: zone of danger, bystander, physical symptoms, special relationships, negligent death notice).**
- **Direct victim, "zone of danger":** plaintiff placed in immediate risk of physical impact by the defendant's negligence may recover for the resulting fright/distress; those outside the zone cannot. In *Consolidated Rail v. Gottshall*, 512 U.S. 532 (1994), the Court adopted it for FELA and noted it was then followed in 14 jurisdictions, with the "relative bystander" test followed by nearly half the states and the physical-impact test by few [SCOTUS, read]. Many jurisdictions require physical symptoms/manifestation; a growing number do not [SEC; UNSURE on count].
- **Bystander** (Dillon v. Legg 1968; Thing v. La Chusa 1989): recovery for witnessing injury to a close relative requires (1) close relationship, (2) presence at the scene, (3) **contemporaneous sensory perception** of the injury-producing event; **a mother who arrives afterward doesn't qualify** under Thing [SEC]. Some jurisdictions additionally require the bystander to be in the zone of danger [SEC; UNSURE].
- **Special relationship/undertaking:** distress without physical danger is recoverable when the defendant's activity makes serious emotional harm especially likely: negligent mishandling of a corpse; erroneous message announcing a close relative's death (NextGen: "negligent reporting of a family member's death") [NCBE NextGen text + SEC].
- **Fear of future disease:** serious, reasonable fear; FELA asbestos cases (Metro-North v. Buckley: no recovery for fear without present symptoms; Gottshall line) [SEC memory; UNSURE].

**Pure economic loss (outline II.E.4).** No negligence recovery for purely economic loss without personal injury or damage to plaintiff's own property (*Robins Dry Dock* 1927) [SEC]. NextGen defines it: "economic harm caused without corresponding personal injuries or property damage" [NCBE]. Exceptions: negligent misrepresentation (limited class), intended beneficiaries of professional services (negligent will drafter), identifiable foreseeable plaintiffs in a few states (People Express), public nuisance special injury, commercial fishers in oil-spill cases [SEC]. **Products:** damage only to the product itself is economic loss in warranty/contract, not tort; the Supreme Court's *East River S.S. v. Transamerica Delaval*, 476 U.S. 858 (1986), held (in admiralty) that a manufacturer in a commercial relationship has "no duty under either a negligence or strict products-liability theory to prevent a product from injuring itself" [SCOTUS, read].

**Other duty rules.** Professionals owe duties to clients; physician's duty only to patients unless special relationship; unborn children (prenatal injury recoverable if born alive); wrongful birth/pregnancy/life vary [SEC; not MBE staples].

### 3.2 Standard of care (outline II.B)
- **Reasonably prudent person under like circumstances**, objective, a question for the jury unless reasonable minds couldn't differ. Include the actor's *physical* characteristics (blindness, a sudden unforeseeable stroke), superior knowledge/skill; exclude mental deficiency, low intelligence, emotional instability, and voluntary intoxication [SEC; Rest. 3d §§11-12]. Sudden, unforeseeable incapacity (heart attack, seizure with no warning) is not negligence [SEC].
- **Children:** child of like age, intelligence, experience (Rest. 2d §283A). **Adult activities** (motor vehicles incl. snowmobiles, boats, planes): held to the adult standard (Rest. 2d §283A cmt. c; Rest. 3d §10 cmt. f lists "driving a car, a tractor, and a motorcycle, and operating other motorized vehicles such as minibikes, motorscooters, dirt bikes, and snowmobiles") [NCBE MEE July 2015, quoted]. The "rule of sevens" (under 7 incapable; 7-14 rebuttably incapable; over 14 capable) is a minority/traditional rule; Rest. 3d §10(b): children under five are incapable of negligence [SEC; UNSURE which the MBE would reward: avoid it in graded items].
- **Professionals:** knowledge and skill of a member of the profession in good standing, in similar circumstances. Medical: national standard now for specialists and (majority) general practitioners; "locality rule" has largely given way. **Expert testimony required** unless negligence is within common knowledge (sponge left in patient). Informed-consent: states split between a reasonable-physician and a reasonable-patient disclosure standard [SEC; UNSURE which is the majority: do not grade]. Failure to obtain informed consent is negligence, not battery, when the patient consented to the procedure itself [SEC].
- **Emergency** (NextGen names it): conduct is judged by what a reasonable person would do in the emergency, if the actor did not create it; many comparative states drop the separate instruction [NCBE NextGen text + SEC].
- **Common carriers, innkeepers:** traditionally "highest degree of care," modern trend ordinary reasonable care [SEC].
- **Custom** (outline II.B.2; *The T.J. Hooper*, 2d Cir. 1932): evidence of reasonable care but **not conclusive** either way; an entire industry can lag [SEC]. In professional malpractice, custom largely sets the standard.
- **Negligence per se** (outline II.B.2; NextGen text): statute/ordinance violation establishes the standard of conduct if (1) plaintiff is in the class of persons the statute was designed to protect and (2) the harm is of the type the statute was designed to prevent. Effect in most states: unexcused violation = breach (some states: presumption or evidence). **Causation and damages must still be proved.** Excuses: compliance would be more dangerous, incapacity, emergency not of own making, ignorance of the facts that trigger the statute, reasonable attempt to comply. **Licensing statutes** (driving without a license) generally don't create negligence per se, since the lack of license doesn't cause the crash [SEC]. **Compliance with a statute or regulation** is evidence of due care, not a bar [SEC]. NextGen also names "the defendant's use of a statute to defend against a negligence claim" (compliance) [NCBE].
- **Res ipsa loquitur** (outline II.C; NextGen text): circumstantial evidence of negligence where (1) the event is of a kind that does not ordinarily occur without negligence, (2) caused by an instrumentality in the **defendant's exclusive control** (modern: more probably than not the defendant's negligence), (3) not due to the plaintiff's voluntary action. Effect: usually a **permissible inference** that gets plaintiff past a directed verdict; a few states make it a presumption shifting the burden of production [SEC]. *Ybarra v. Spangard* lets an anesthetized patient reach multiple defendants in control [SEC].
- **Premises proof:** slip-and-fall plaintiff must show defendant created the condition or had actual/constructive notice; some states relax for self-service stores [SEC].

### 3.3 Item bank: duty and standard of care

**NEG-1 (unforeseeable plaintiff).** A freight handler negligently drops a crate of glassware near a loading door. Unknown to everyone, a nearby passenger's unlabeled package of fireworks is jolted and explodes; the blast topples a heavy sign onto a woman 40 feet away. She sues the freight company for negligence. Under the majority view, the company is:
(A) not liable to her, because nothing suggested any risk to someone in her position from the handler's carelessness. (B) liable, because but for the handler's negligence she would not have been hurt. (C) liable, because the harm was a direct result of the negligent act. (D) not liable, because the passenger was the real wrongdoer.
**Best: A.** Majority (Cardozo): duty only to foreseeable plaintiffs in the zone of danger. B and C reflect the minority "universal duty" approach; D is wrong because the passenger's wrongdoing isn't the stated reason (the package was a hidden cause, not a deliberate wrong).

**NEG-2 (no duty to rescue).** Jun, a strong swimmer, sees an adult stranger struggling in a public lake, 30 feet from shore, and does nothing, though a rescue would have been easy. The stranger drowns. Jun's liability in negligence is:
(A) none, because there is no general duty to rescue a stranger. (B) none unless Jun is a lifeguard. (C) full, because rescue was easy and risk-free. (D) full, because a duty arises when the danger is obvious.
**Best: A.** B is half-true (lifeguards, by special relationship, would owe a duty) but states the exception incorrectly as the whole rule; C/D describe the civil-law rule.

**NEG-3 (voluntary undertaking: leaving worse off).** Dara finds a hiker unconscious on a trail and drags him off the trail into a hollow, where he is out of sight of other hikers, then leaves to avoid being late. The hiker, who would have been seen within minutes on the trail, lies undiscovered for hours and suffers hypothermia. Dara is:
(A) liable, because having begun aid she left him in a worse position. (B) not liable, because she had no duty to help at all. (C) not liable, because Good Samaritan laws protect rescuers. (D) liable only if she acted recklessly.
**Best: A.** B ignores the undertaking; C: Good Samaritan statutes protect good-faith aid, not abandonment that worsens risk (and don't create or excuse duties here); D wrong standard.

**NEG-4 (control of third parties).** Parent Luz knows her 15-year-old has repeatedly beaten younger children and that he keeps a hunting rifle in an unlocked closet at home. She does nothing. He shoots a neighbor's child. The neighbor sues Luz. Her best defense is:
(A) none strong: a parent who knows of a child's dangerous propensity and can control him has a duty to take reasonable steps. (B) parents are never liable for their children's torts. (C) the child's act was an intentional tort, which supersedes. (D) she did not intend the harm.
**Best: A.** Liability is for the parent's own negligence, not vicarious. B states the vicarious rule but misses direct negligence; C: foreseeable intentional acts do not supersede when they are the very risk; D: negligence needs no intent.

**NEG-5 (licensee: known concealed danger).** Host Carl knows the third step of his back stairs is rotten and has not warned anyone. A social guest steps through it and is injured. Under the traditional rule, Carl is:
(A) liable, because he knew of a concealed danger the licensee could not discover. (B) not liable, because social guests are trespassers. (C) not liable, because licensees take the premises as they find them. (D) liable only if Carl had inspected the stairs recently.
**Best: A.** Licensee: duty to warn/make safe known concealed hazards. D is the invitee rule (duty to inspect); B and C misclassify.

**NEG-6 (undiscovered adult trespasser).** An adult cuts across a fenced industrial yard at night to avoid a long walk and falls into an unmarked drainage trench he did not know about. The owner did not know anyone crossed the yard. The owner is:
(A) not liable in negligence under the traditional rules, because the owner owes an undiscovered trespasser no duty regarding conditions. (B) liable, because the trench was unmarked. (C) liable, because all entrants are owed reasonable care. (D) liable only for punitive damages.
**Best: A.** C reflects the modern rule (rejected by most states; NCBE's own analysis calls the traditional status rules the ones "most [states] have retained"). In a stem, if the jurisdiction "has abolished status distinctions," C wins instead.

**NEG-7 (attractive nuisance).** A homeowner leaves an old refrigerator with a latching door in his unfenced side yard, next to a playground where children often play. A seven-year-old climbs in, the door closes, and she is injured. The homeowner is:
(A) subject to liability if the burden of removing the danger was slight compared with the risk to children. (B) not liable, because the child was a trespasser. (C) liable only if the refrigerator attracted her into the yard. (D) not liable, because refrigerators are not inherently dangerous.
**Best: A.** Rest. 2d §339 balancing; B ignores the child-trespasser rule; C: attraction is not required under the Restatement.

**NEG-8 (standard of care: adult activity).** A 14-year-old drives a motorboat on a lake at high speed and strikes a swimmer. In deciding whether the boy was negligent, the jury should use the standard of:
(A) a reasonably prudent adult operating a motorboat. (B) a reasonable 14-year-old. (C) a reasonable 14-year-old with the boy's boating experience. (D) no standard, because minors cannot be negligent.
**Best: A.** Adult activity (motorized vehicles) overrides the child standard (NCBE MEE cites snowmobiles).

**NEG-9 (mental condition not considered).** A man with a delusional disorder, who had been warned by his doctor not to drive, drives his car and, in the grip of a delusion, runs a red light, injuring a cyclist. In the cyclist's negligence action, the man's mental condition:
(A) is not considered; he is held to the standard of a reasonably prudent sober, sane person. (B) lowers the standard to a reasonable person with his disorder. (C) excuses him because he lacked intent. (D) excuses him because he had no control during the delusion.
**Best: A.** Mental illness/limitation doesn't lower the adult standard. Contrast sudden incapacity with no warning (heart attack), which is a defense; D would fit only an unforeseeable total loss of consciousness, and the warning facts defeat it.

**NEG-10 (negligence per se: type of harm).** An ordinance requires commercial kitchens to install ceiling sprinklers to limit the spread of fire. A restaurant omits them. During a dinner, a ceiling tile unrelated to any fire falls and injures a customer. The customer asserts negligence per se. The claim will:
(A) fail on this theory, because the harm was not of the type the ordinance was meant to prevent. (B) succeed, because the restaurant violated the ordinance. (C) succeed, because customers are within the protected class. (D) fail, because ordinances never support negligence per se.
**Best: A.** Both prongs (class and type of harm) are needed. C satisfies only one prong. D: ordinances commonly are treated like statutes.

**NEG-11 (negligence per se: causation).** A driver without a valid license is lawfully stopped at a red light when a truck whose driver ran the light hits her. In her suit against the trucker, the trucker argues the driver's unlicensed status makes her negligent per se. The argument will:
(A) fail, because the lack of a license did not cause the collision. (B) succeed, because she violated a safety statute. (C) succeed, because licensing statutes protect other drivers. (D) fail, because she was a passenger.
**Best: A.** Licensing violations don't cause the harm; causation must still be shown. D invents facts.

**NEG-12 (custom).** All local tugboat operators run without weather radios. A tug operator without one is caught in an unforecast storm and wrecks barges. The barge owner sues for negligence. The industry custom:
(A) is relevant but not conclusive; the jury may find the operators negligent despite the custom. (B) is conclusive proof of due care. (C) is inadmissible. (D) is conclusive only if the radios were expensive.
**Best: A.** *The T.J. Hooper*.

**NEG-13 (res ipsa).** A surgical patient awakens with a burn on her shoulder, far from the surgical site. During the operation she was unconscious and under the control of the surgeon, an anesthesiologist, two nurses and the hospital. She sues all of them and can offer no direct evidence of which person caused it. On the res ipsa issue, the court should:
(A) allow the jury to infer negligence by those in control of the patient's care. (B) direct a verdict for the defendants because plaintiff has no direct evidence. (C) shift the burden of persuasion to the defendants as a matter of law. (D) deny it because multiple defendants were present.
**Best: A.** *Ybarra* logic; C overstates (permissive inference in most states).

**NEG-14 (res ipsa: plaintiff's conduct).** A passenger in a building's elevator had been jumping up and down for a full minute when the elevator suddenly dropped two floors and injured her. The building's maintenance company has exclusive responsibility for the elevator. She sues it under res ipsa loquitur, and the company shows her jumping could have caused the drop. In a jurisdiction that still follows the traditional elements, res ipsa is:
(A) unavailable, because the evidence does not exclude the plaintiff's own conduct as a cause. (B) available, because elevators don't drop without negligence. (C) available, because the company has exclusive control. (D) unavailable, because there was no direct evidence of negligence.
**Best: A.** Traditional third element: not caused by the plaintiff's own voluntary action. Many comparative-fault states drop that element and reduce damages instead; the stem must say which applies. D misstates what res ipsa is for.

**NEG-15 (NIED: bystander timing).** A mother is at home when a speeding driver strikes her child in the street outside; she hears nothing, and a neighbor tells her five minutes later. The mother suffers severe distress. Her NIED claim against the driver will:
(A) fail, because she did not contemporaneously perceive the event. (B) succeed, because she is a close relative. (C) succeed, because the child was seriously hurt. (D) succeed because she saw the child at the hospital.
**Best: A.** Bystander recovery requires presence and contemporaneous sensory perception (Thing).

**NEG-16 (NIED: zone of danger).** A cyclist narrowly avoids being hit when a driver negligently runs a stop sign; she is unhurt physically but suffers panic attacks with vomiting. Under the zone-of-danger approach she:
(A) may recover for the distress, because she was placed in immediate risk of physical impact. (B) cannot recover, because she was not physically hurt. (C) may recover only if she was actually touched. (D) cannot recover, because distress is not compensable.
**Best: A.** B/C describe the discarded impact rule.

**NEG-17 (death notice).** A hospital employee negligently telephones a woman that her husband died in surgery; he is in fact alive and recovering. She spends two days in severe distress with physical symptoms. She sues the hospital. She will:
(A) recover under the special-relationship/undertaking rule for negligent reporting of a close relative's death. (B) not recover, because she was never in a zone of danger. (C) not recover, because no physical impact occurred. (D) recover only if the hospital intended to deceive her.
**Best: A.** NextGen names negligent reporting of a family member's death; the MBE treats it as a special-relationship category.

**NEG-18 (pure economic loss).** A contractor negligently severs a power cable owned by the utility, blacking out a bakery for three days. The bakery's ovens and goods are undamaged; it loses a week's profits. The bakery sues the contractor in negligence. It will:
(A) not recover, because it suffered only pure economic loss. (B) recover, because its loss was foreseeable. (C) recover because it was a customer of the utility. (D) recover if the contractor was grossly negligent.
**Best: A.** The utility, whose property was physically damaged, could recover; the bakery cannot. (Spoilage of its own goods would be property damage and change the result.)

## 4. NEGLIGENCE - part B: causation, multiple tortfeasors, vicarious liability, defenses, damages

### 4.1 Actual cause (outline II.D.1-2)
- **But-for:** harm would not have occurred absent the conduct. Fails where the same harm would have happened anyway.
- **Multiple sufficient causes** (each alone would have caused the whole harm; e.g., two independent fires merge): each is a **substantial factor** and each is a cause-in-fact (*Kingston*/*Anderson* line, [SEC]). **Multiple necessary causes:** each is but-for [NCBE NextGen names both].
- **Alternative liability** (*Summers v. Tice*, Cal. 1948): two defendants both negligent toward plaintiff, only one caused the harm, plaintiff cannot tell which; **burden shifts** to each defendant to exculpate himself; if neither can, both are jointly and severally liable [SEC; LII]. Applies where defendants are before the court; not for unknown manufacturers.
- **Market share** (*Sindell v. Abbott Labs*, Cal. 1980; DES): fungible product, manufacturer unknown; liability by market share. Accepted in a minority of states [SEC, UNSURE count].
- **Concert of action** (common plan/tacit agreement, e.g., drag race): each liable for the other's tort [SEC].
- **Loss of chance:** traditional rule: no recovery if survival chance was 50% or less; many states now allow recovery for the lost chance, often proportionally (*Herskovits*; *Matsuyama*): a split [SEC]. Do not grade.
- **Divisible harm:** each defendant liable for the part he caused; defendants bear the burden of proving a division [SEC].

### 4.2 Proximate cause (outline II.E.1; NextGen: foreseeability, "scope of the risk," intervening and superseding causes)
- **Foreseeable type of harm, any extent:** Wagon Mound (the type of harm must have been foreseeable) and Rest. 3d §29: liable only for harm within the **scope of the risks** that made the conduct negligent. The "direct consequences" test (*Polemis*) is rejected [SEC].
- **Falling-tree rule of thumb:** a negligent act that is a but-for cause but increased none of the risk of the harm that occurred (speeding driver is in the wrong place when a tree falls) is not a proximate cause [SEC; Rest. 3d §29 illustrations style, my paraphrase: UNSURE as to exact illustration wording].
- **Eggshell (thin skull) plaintiff:** take the plaintiff as found; once some physical harm of a foreseeable type is shown, liable for full extent even if unforeseeable (Rest. 2d §461; NextGen damages names "thin skin" rule) [NCBE NextGen + SEC].
- **Intervening causes:** an intervening force is superseding (cuts the chain) only if unforeseeable/extraordinary. **Foreseeable intervening forces do NOT supersede:** negligent medical treatment of the original injury (Rest. 2d §457), negligent rescuers, reaction of the plaintiff or others in the emergency, subsequent disease from weakened condition, foreseeable third-party negligence, and **foreseeable crimes or intentional torts of third parties** (the very risk that made the defendant's conduct negligent) [SEC; Rest. 2d §§442-449 from memory]. Typically superseding: extraordinary natural events, wholly unforeseeable criminal acts [SEC].
- **Rescue doctrine:** a negligent defendant is liable to a rescuer injured in a reasonable rescue attempt [SEC].
- **Plaintiff's own post-injury negligence:** treated as avoidable consequences/comparative fault [SEC].

### 4.3 Multiple tortfeasors (outline II.D.3; NextGen VIII.A)
- **Default:** **joint and several liability** where harm is indivisible; plaintiff may collect the whole from any liable defendant (one satisfaction) [NCBE]. Many states have abolished or limited it (several-only, threshold, economic damages only) [SEC]; the MBE tells you when.
- **Contribution:** a defendant who paid more than his share may recover the excess from other tortfeasors; shares by relative fault percentages in comparative systems (otherwise per capita) [SEC; Uniform Contribution Among Tortfeasors Act]. Intentional tortfeasors are generally barred from contribution from negligent co-defendants under UCATA [SEC; UNSURE].
- **Indemnity (full shift):** (1) vicariously liable party against the primarily negligent one (employer v. employee); (2) innocent retailer/wholesaler held strictly liable v. manufacturer; (3) express contract [SEC; NCBE MEE Feb 2015: the store that paid for its employee's negligence is "entitled to indemnification from the driver"].
- **Settlement:** a release of one tortfeasor does not release the others unless it says so (modern); nonsettling defendants get credit by the **pro tanto** (dollar-for-dollar) or **comparative-share** method, state-specific [SEC]. Put the method in the stem.
- **Plaintiff's comparative fault with several defendants:** compare plaintiff's share against the total of all defendants' fault [SEC].

### 4.4 Liability for acts of others (outline II.F)
- **Employer v. independent contractor [NCBE MEE Feb 2015]:** right to control the manner and means of the work (Rest. 3d Agency §7.07(3)(a); Rest. 2d Agency §220): skill required, who supplies tools, duration, method of payment, whether part of the employer's regular business; label in the contract is not determinative.
- **Scope of employment** [NCBE MEE Feb 2015]: Rest. 2d Agency §228: (a) work of the kind employed to perform, (b) substantially within authorized time and space, (c) motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer. Rest. 3d §7.07(2): "An employee acts within the scope of employment when performing work assigned by the employer or engaging in a course of conduct subject to the employer's control" [quoted in the NCBE analysis]; the companion sentence excluding "an independent course of conduct not intended by the employee to serve any purpose of the employer" is from my memory [UNSURE on wording]. Violation of the employer's rules does not take an act out of scope. Small deviation (minor detour) stays in; a **frolic** (substantial departure for personal ends) is out; return to the route re-enters scope [SEC]. "Going and coming" commutes generally outside scope [SEC].
- **Intentional torts of employees:** generally outside scope unless force is authorized/foreseeable in the job (bouncer, security guard, repossession), or the act is motivated at least in part by the employer's business [SEC].
- **Employer's own (direct) negligence:** negligent hiring, retention, supervision, entrustment: liable even when the act is outside scope, if the risk that was ignored materialized [SEC].
- **Independent contractors:** hirer not liable except: (1) **inherently dangerous / peculiar-risk** work (blasting, demolition, crop dusting; Rest. 2d §§416, 427), (2) **nondelegable duties** (premises safety to invitees, public roads/sidewalk upkeep, landlord common areas, statutory/regulatory duties), (3) hirer's own negligence in selection or in exercising retained control, (4) apparent agency (hospital ER physicians) [NCBE outline II.F.2 names nondelegable duties; rest SEC].
- **Owner of a car/bailor:** at common law not vicariously liable for a permissive driver; liable for negligent entrustment; some states impose liability by **family-purpose doctrine** or **owner-liability statutes**; the federal Graves Amendment protects rental/lease companies from vicarious liability for owning [SEC].
- **Parents:** not vicariously liable for a child's torts at common law (statutes in many states cap liability for willful property damage); liable for own negligent supervision/entrustment [SEC; NextGen: "parental responsibility for the negligence of minor children"].
- **Imputed negligence:** a driver's negligence is not imputed to a passenger; imputed from employee to employer when the employer sues; from spouse/parent generally no [SEC].
- **NextGen:** vicarious liability of principals is tested under Business Associations; Torts keeps parents and nondelegable duties [NCBE].

### 4.5 Defenses (outline II.G)
- **Contributory negligence** (AL, DC, MD, NC, VA [NCBE]): any plaintiff fault bars recovery. **Last clear chance** rescues the plaintiff: if defendant had the last opportunity to avoid harm and knew (or, for a helpless plaintiff, should have known) of the peril [SEC; the "inattentive plaintiff requires actual knowledge" variation is the Restatement approach: UNSURE]. Contributory negligence is no defense to **intentional torts** or willful/wanton conduct in the traditional rule; comparative-fault states increasingly compare reckless defendant vs negligent plaintiff [SEC; UNSURE].
- **Comparative negligence:** **pure** (MBE default [NCBE]): damages reduced by plaintiff's percentage, no bar. **Modified:** bars plaintiff at 50% or more ("not as great as") or at 51% or more ("not greater than"); state rule must be in the stem. Do **not** teach state counts as exam facts: LII Wex says "almost one-third of states" are pure and names CA, FL, NY, but Florida changed to a modified-51% bar for most negligence suits in 2023 (HB 837, from my memory: [UNSURE: verify]), so Wex is out of date. NCBE only needs the concept.
- **Assumption of risk:** *express* (release/exculpatory clause: enforceable if clear and covering the risk, not for gross negligence/recklessness/intentional harm or where it violates public policy, e.g., essential services *Tunkl*); *implied primary* (inherent risks of an activity; defendant owes no duty: foul ball at a baseball game; *Knight v. Jewett*); *implied secondary* (plaintiff knowingly encountered a risk created by defendant's negligence): **merges into comparative negligence** in comparative states (NextGen: "including secondary implied assumption of risk") [SEC + NCBE NextGen text]. Requires actual knowledge and voluntary encounter.
- **Plaintiff's duty to mitigate** (avoidable consequences): no recovery for harm reasonably avoidable after injury (e.g., refusing a safe, routine treatment); seat-belt evidence rules vary by state [SEC; NextGen: "failure to mitigate"].
- **Immunities** (outline lists "privileges and immunities" only under intentional torts): governmental immunity (FTCA waiver with discretionary-function and intentional-tort exceptions; *Feres* doctrine), charitable and interspousal largely abolished, parent-child partly [SEC; low priority]. Statutes of limitation/repose and discovery rule: state law, not on the MBE outline.

### 4.6 Damages, survival and wrongful death (NextGen VIII; MBE outline note)
- **Compensatory:** general (pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment, consortium) and special (medical expenses, lost earnings, property damage); future loss reduced to present value [SEC; NextGen VIII.B text]. **Nominal damages are not available in negligence** (damage is an element) but are available for intentional torts like trespass and battery [SEC].
- **Collateral source rule** (traditional): benefits from independent sources (health insurance, sick pay, charity) are not deducted; many states changed by statute [SEC].
- **Punitive damages:** require malice, willful or reckless conduct, not mere negligence. Due process (*BMW v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996): reprehensibility, ratio, comparable penalties; *State Farm v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408 (2003): "few awards exceeding a single-digit ratio between punitive and compensatory damages, to a significant degree, will satisfy due process"; a 145-to-1 award was excessive) [SCOTUS, read]. *Philip Morris v. Williams* (juries may not punish for harm to nonparties) [SEC; not opened].
- **Statutory limits:** many states cap noneconomic damages [SEC; NextGen: "statutory limitations on recovery"]. **American rule** on attorney's fees [NextGen text].
- **Loss of consortium:** spouse (and, in many states, parent/child); derivative of the injured person's claim, so reduced by the injured person's comparative fault and subject to the same defenses [SEC].
- **Survival action:** the decedent's own claim continues in the estate: pre-death pain and suffering, medical expenses, lost earnings before death. **Wrongful death:** survivors' own statutory claim: loss of financial support, companionship (consortium), funeral expenses (and, by state, more). Decedent's comparative fault reduces both [SEC]. The MBE says to assume both exist [NCBE]; the usual test is *which claim covers which loss*.
- **Defamation claims do not survive** the plaintiff's death at common law [SEC].

### 4.7 Item bank: causation, multiple tortfeasors, vicarious liability, defenses, damages

**CAU-1 (but-for).** A hotel negligently posts no lifeguard at its pool. A guest is found at the bottom of the pool; medical evidence shows he suffered a fatal cardiac arrest on the pool deck and was already dead when he fell in. The guest's estate sues the hotel for negligence. The estate will:
(A) lose, because a lifeguard would not have changed the outcome. (B) win, because the hotel breached its duty. (C) win, because a pool without a lifeguard is a nuisance. (D) lose, because guests assume the risk at hotel pools.
**Best: A.** No but-for causation. B skips causation; D invents an assumption of risk.

**CAU-2 (multiple sufficient causes).** Two unrelated factories each negligently allow a fire to escape. The fires merge and burn down Rosa's barn; either fire alone would have destroyed it. Each factory argues that the barn would have burned even without its fire. Rosa can recover from:
(A) each factory for the whole loss, because each fire was a substantial factor. (B) neither, because neither fire was a but-for cause. (C) each for only half. (D) only the factory whose fire arrived first.
**Best: A.** Substantial factor test for multiple sufficient causes; they are jointly and severally liable. (D has some support under some views if the first fire alone had already destroyed the barn; the stem says they merged.)

**CAU-3 (alternative liability).** Hal and Ike, hunting together, each negligently fire at a deer in Jo's direction. One pellet hits Jo's eye. No one can tell whose gun fired it. Jo sues both. The court should:
(A) shift the burden to each defendant to prove he did not cause the injury, and hold both liable if neither does. (B) enter judgment for both defendants because Jo can't prove who caused it. (C) hold each liable for half. (D) require Jo to prove which shot hit her before any recovery.
**Best: A.** *Summers v. Tice*.

**CAU-4 (eggshell plaintiff).** A driver negligently rear-ends Pam's car at 5 mph; an ordinary person would have had minor whiplash. Pam has an undiagnosed bone disease and suffers multiple fractures. The driver is liable for:
(A) all of Pam's injuries, because a tortfeasor takes the plaintiff as he finds her. (B) only the whiplash. (C) none, because the fractures were unforeseeable. (D) only the injuries a reasonable driver would have foreseen.
**Best: A.** Foreseeability governs the type of harm (physical injury), not its extent.

**CAU-5 (scope of risk).** Dev drives 20 mph over the limit. A few seconds before reaching an intersection, a healthy tree beside the road suddenly falls and crushes his passenger's seat. Had Dev driven at the limit, he would have been 200 yards behind. The passenger sues Dev. The passenger will probably:
(A) lose, because the falling tree was not within the risks that made speeding negligent. (B) win, because but for the speeding the passenger would not have been under the tree. (C) win, because speeding violates a statute. (D) lose, because the passenger assumed the risk of riding.
**Best: A.** But-for is satisfied, but proximate cause (scope of risk) fails. B stops at actual cause. C: negligence per se needs the harm to be of the type the statute targets.

**CAU-6 (negligent medical treatment).** Driver Vik negligently breaks Lena's leg. During the repair, the surgeon negligently severs a nerve, causing foot drop. Lena sues Vik for all her injuries. Vik is:
(A) liable for the foot drop as well, because negligent medical treatment is a foreseeable consequence of the original injury. (B) liable only for the broken leg, because the surgeon's negligence is superseding. (C) not liable for anything, because Lena's doctor is responsible. (D) liable for the foot drop only if the surgeon is insolvent.
**Best: A.** Ordinary negligent treatment is not superseding. (The surgeon is also liable; Vik and the surgeon are jointly and severally liable for the foot drop, with Vik additionally liable for the leg.)

**CAU-7 (foreseeable crime).** A landlord knows the front-door lock of a tenement building is broken and that burglaries have recently occurred in the neighborhood. He doesn't repair it for two months. A burglar enters and assaults a tenant. The tenant sues the landlord for negligence. The criminal act is:
(A) not a superseding cause, because it was the very risk that made the failure to repair negligent. (B) a superseding cause, because intentional crimes always supersede. (C) irrelevant, because landlords are never liable for crimes. (D) a superseding cause, because the burglar is the sole wrongdoer.
**Best: A.** B states a rigid rule that doesn't exist; C overstates (duty depends on state law and the stem's facts).

**CAU-8 (rescue).** Dee negligently leaves her delivery truck on a slope without setting the brake. It starts rolling toward a toddler in a yard. A passerby, Moe, dashes in, stops the truck, and wrenches his back. Moe sues Dee. Moe will:
(A) recover, because a rescuer is a foreseeable plaintiff. (B) not recover, because he voluntarily assumed the risk. (C) not recover, because he was a stranger with no duty to help. (D) recover only if the toddler was hurt.
**Best: A.** "Danger invites rescue."

**MUL-1 (J&S default).** Pat, who was entirely free of fault, suffers $200,000 in damages in a collision caused by Dan (60% at fault) and Eve (40% at fault). Eve is insolvent. Assuming the NCBE default rules, Pat may recover:
(A) $200,000 from Dan, who may then seek contribution from Eve. (B) $120,000 from Dan and nothing more. (C) $80,000 from Eve and $120,000 from Dan. (D) $200,000 from Dan only if Eve is also joined.
**Best: A.** Joint and several liability places the insolvency risk on the solvent defendant. B reflects several-only liability.

**MUL-2 (comparative fault + J&S).** Sam is 30% at fault, Tia 50% and Una 20% in a three-car collision; Sam's damages are $100,000. Under pure comparative fault and joint and several liability, Sam may recover:
(A) $70,000, collectible in full from either Tia or Una. (B) $50,000 from Tia and $20,000 from Una, nothing more from either. (C) nothing, because Sam was partly at fault. (D) $100,000, because the others were more at fault.
**Best: A.** The award is reduced by Sam's share and the reduced award is joint and several. B is several-only liability; C is contributory negligence.

**MUL-3 (indemnity).** A delivery company is held vicariously liable for $50,000 after its employee negligently injured a pedestrian; the company itself was not at fault. The company's best claim against the employee is for:
(A) full indemnity, because the company's liability was purely vicarious. (B) contribution of half, because they are joint tortfeasors. (C) nothing, because the employee was acting within scope. (D) punitive damages.
**Best: A.** NCBE's Feb 2015 analysis states the store is "entitled to indemnification from the driver."

**VIC-1 (minor detour).** A pizza shop's driver, on a delivery run, turns two blocks off his usual route to buy a pack of gum and, while turning back, negligently hits a pedestrian. The pedestrian sues the shop. The shop is:
(A) liable, because a minor deviation does not take the driver outside the scope of employment. (B) not liable, because buying gum was personal. (C) not liable, because the shop's rules forbid personal stops. (D) liable only if the shop knew about the stop.
**Best: A.** Frolic requires a substantial departure; violation of rules doesn't remove scope (NCBE Feb 2015: employer's rules don't control).

**VIC-2 (frolic).** The same driver, after his last delivery, drives 40 miles in the opposite direction to visit a friend and, there, negligently hits a pedestrian. The shop's liability is:
(A) none, because he was on a frolic of his own. (B) full, because he was driving the shop's car. (C) full, because he was still on the clock. (D) partial, based on the shop's share of fault.
**Best: A.** B/C are not scope tests. (If the stem said he was returning to the shop after the friend visit, analysis changes.)

**VIC-3 (employee's intentional tort).** A nightclub bouncer, while ejecting a loud patron, uses grossly excessive force and breaks the patron's arm. The patron sues the club. The club is:
(A) liable, because force is a foreseeable part of the bouncer's job. (B) not liable, because battery is an intentional act. (C) liable only if it ordered the force. (D) not liable, because the bouncer exceeded instructions.
**Best: A.**

**VIC-4 (independent contractor and inherently dangerous work).** A city hires an independent demolition firm to implode a vacant building. Debris from the careless blast damages a neighboring store. The store sues the city. The city is:
(A) liable, because it hired the contractor to perform inherently dangerous work. (B) not liable, because it hired an independent contractor. (C) liable only if the city controlled the details. (D) not liable unless it was negligent in choosing the contractor.
**Best: A.** B states the general rule, ignoring the exception. (Many courts treat this as a nondelegable duty.)

**VIC-5 (negligent hiring: direct liability).** A company knowingly hires as a driver an applicant with three DUI convictions and lets him keep the company van at home overnight. On a Sunday, driving the van on a purely personal trip, he crashes while drunk and injures a pedestrian. She sues the company on two theories: respondeat superior and negligent hiring/entrustment. Which statement is most accurate?
(A) Respondeat superior probably fails because the trip was personal, but the direct-negligence claim may succeed because the company ignored the very risk that materialized. (B) Both claims fail because the trip was outside the scope of employment. (C) Both claims succeed because the company owned the van. (D) The negligent-hiring claim fails because it requires the tort to be within the scope of employment.
**Best: A.** Negligent hiring/entrustment is the employer's own fault and doesn't depend on scope. D confuses direct and vicarious liability.

**VIC-6 (owner of a car).** Olga lends her car to a friend, whom she reasonably believes is a careful driver. The friend negligently crashes into a cyclist. Absent any statute or family-purpose doctrine, Olga is:
(A) not liable, because an owner is not vicariously liable for a permissive driver at common law. (B) liable, because she owns the vehicle. (C) liable, because she consented to the use. (D) liable if the friend is insolvent.
**Best: A.** Negligent entrustment would require facts that the friend was unfit.

**DEF-1 (pure comparative).** Under the NCBE default, Ann (70% at fault) and Bo (30% at fault) are in a collision. Ann's damages are $100,000. Ann can recover:
(A) $30,000 from Bo. (B) $0, because Ann was more than half at fault. (C) $100,000. (D) $70,000.
**Best: A.** (Bo's share only; B and a modified-51% jurisdiction give $0.)

**DEF-2 (modified comparative).** In a jurisdiction that bars a plaintiff whose fault is 50% or more, Cy (50% at fault) is injured by Dee (50% at fault). Cy's damages are $80,000. Cy recovers:
(A) nothing. (B) $40,000. (C) $80,000. (D) $0 or $40,000, at the jury's option.
**Best: A.** (In a 51% bar jurisdiction the answer would be $40,000. The stem states which rule applies.)

**DEF-3 (last clear chance).** In a contributory-negligence jurisdiction, Fay negligently falls asleep on a railroad crossing. The engineer sees her 300 yards ahead, realizes she is not moving, and has ample time to stop, but does not. Fay is injured. Fay:
(A) can recover under last clear chance. (B) cannot recover, because her own negligence put her there. (C) recovers half under comparative negligence. (D) cannot recover because the engineer was not reckless.
**Best: A.** The defendant had the last clear chance to avoid the harm and actual knowledge of her helpless peril.

**DEF-4 (primary assumption of risk).** A spectator at a minor-league ballgame in the unscreened seats is hit by a foul ball and injured. She sues the team for negligence in not providing a protective screen over that section. The most likely outcome is:
(A) the team prevails; a spectator assumes the inherent risk of foul balls in unscreened seats, so no breach of duty. (B) the team prevails, because the spectator was comparatively negligent. (C) the spectator prevails, because the team could easily have installed a screen. (D) the spectator prevails because the risk was not obvious.
**Best: A.** Primary assumption of risk (no duty as to inherent risks), well-known baseball rule [SEC; limited-duty rule varies; tag UNSURE as to MBE detail].

**DEF-5 (express release).** A climbing gym requires patrons to sign a clear release of all claims for negligence. Pia signs and is injured when a gym employee, who has been told a rope is badly frayed, consciously decides not to replace it. The gym's reliance on the release:
(A) may fail, because releases are generally not enforced against gross negligence or reckless conduct. (B) will succeed, because Pia signed it. (C) fails, because releases for recreation are always void. (D) will succeed, because an employee's conduct cannot be gross negligence.
**Best: A.** Express assumption of risk covers ordinary negligence only.

**DEF-6 (comparative fault no defense to intentional tort).** Qui negligently leaves a gate open; Rex, enraged at Qui, punches him in the face while Qui is distracted. In Qui's battery action, Rex argues Qui's negligence reduces damages. The argument will:
(A) fail, because a plaintiff's ordinary negligence is not a defense to an intentional tort in most states. (B) succeed, because comparative fault is universal. (C) succeed if Qui was more than 50% at fault. (D) succeed because the punch was provoked.
**Best: A.** (Provocation by words alone is not a defense; at most it reduces punitive damages.)

**DAM-1 (collateral source).** Lu's medical bills of $40,000 from a negligent driver's accident were fully paid by her health insurer. Under the traditional rule, in her suit against the driver the jury should:
(A) include the $40,000 in damages, ignoring the insurance. (B) deduct the $40,000. (C) hear evidence of the insurance and decide whether to deduct. (D) award only noneconomic damages.
**Best: A.** Collateral source rule.

**DAM-2 (punitive damages).** Ed negligently rear-ends Flo at a red light because he glanced at a text. Flo sues for $20,000 compensatory and $2 million punitive damages. The punitive claim will probably fail because:
(A) negligence, even if careless, is not the malicious, reckless or willful conduct required for punitive damages. (B) punitive damages are not recoverable in torts. (C) punitive damages are limited to intentional torts. (D) a $2 million award exceeds the constitutional ratio.
**Best: A.** C is too narrow (reckless conduct suffices). D is a due process question that would arise only after liability for punitive damages was established.

**DAM-3 (survival vs wrongful death).** A driver's negligence kills Greg, who suffered three hours of conscious pain at the scene. His wife and minor child sue. In an action for Greg's conscious pain and suffering, the claim belongs to:
(A) Greg's estate, under the survival statute. (B) the wife, under the wrongful death statute. (C) the child, as sole heir. (D) no one; pain and suffering is not recoverable after death.
**Best: A.** Wrongful death covers the survivors' own losses (support, companionship, funeral). The NCBE outline tells you to assume both statutes exist.

**DAM-4 (consortium is derivative).** Hal is injured by Jan's negligence, but Hal is 40% at fault. Hal's wife sues Jan for loss of consortium. Her recovery will be:
(A) reduced by Hal's 40% fault. (B) full, because her own conduct is blameless. (C) barred, because consortium isn't recoverable by spouses. (D) reduced only if she shared fault.
**Best: A.** Derivative claim.

## 5. STRICT LIABILITY, PRODUCTS LIABILITY AND NUISANCE (MBE category III, and nuisance from IV.A)

### 5.1 Common-law strict liability: animals (outline III; NOT in the NextGen Torts outline)
Source for most of this subsection: Restatement (Second) of Torts §§504-517 as summarized in secondary material [SEC]; I could not open the Restatement text. Section numbers are from memory and one summary: tag [UNSURE] on numbers, [SEC] on substance.
- **Wild animals** (not customarily domesticated: lions, bears, chimpanzees, venomous snakes): possessor strictly liable for harm resulting from the animal's dangerous propensity characteristic of its class (or any propensity the possessor knows of), however carefully kept (Rest. 2d §507). Covers harm done by panicked flight from the animal. **Undiscovered trespassers** generally cannot use strict liability (they would have to prove negligence or an intentional tort) [SEC; two study guides agree]. Unconfirmed claim in one guide that public zoos and common carriers are held only to negligence: [UNSURE].
- **Domestic animals** (dogs, cats, horses, cattle): strict liability only if the possessor **knew or had reason to know of the animal's abnormally dangerous propensity** (Rest. 2d §509: "scienter"/the "one-bite rule," though no prior bite is required: repeated lunging, attack training, or earlier aggression gives notice); otherwise negligence. Normal playful behavior (jumping on visitors) isn't notice. **Dog-bite statutes** in many states impose strict liability by statute; the stem must say [SEC].
- **Trespassing livestock** (Rest. 2d §504): possessor of livestock (cattle, horses, sheep, pigs; not household pets) is strictly liable for physical harm to land or chattels by their trespass (crops, soil, structures); not for personal injury; fence-out statutes in some western states [SEC].
- **Defenses:** plaintiff's knowing and unreasonable encounter of the risk (assumption of risk) and, in modern comparative systems, comparative fault; ordinary inattentive contributory negligence was classically not a defense [SEC]. Harm must flow from the animal's dangerous propensity (a gentle animal that knocks someone over by accident isn't the risk).

### 5.2 Abnormally dangerous activities (outline III; NextGen III)
- **Origin:** *Rylands v. Fletcher* (1868) [SEC].
- **Rest. 2d §§519-520 (six factors):** (a) existence of a high degree of risk of some harm; (b) likelihood that the harm will be great; (c) inability to eliminate the risk by reasonable care; (d) extent to which the activity is not a matter of common usage; (e) inappropriateness of the activity to the place where it is carried on; (f) extent to which its value to the community is outweighed by its dangerous attributes. **Rest. 3d §20:** activity is abnormally dangerous if (1) it creates a foreseeable and highly significant risk of physical harm even when reasonable care is exercised by all actors, and (2) it is not an activity of common usage [SEC for both statements]. Question of **law for the court**, not the jury [SEC].
- **Typical strict-liability activities:** blasting/explosives, storing large quantities of toxic/explosive substances in populated areas, nuclear/radioactive material, crop dusting and fumigation (courts divide), certain well drilling [SEC]. **Typically negligence only (common usage / can be made safe):** driving, ordinary aviation, municipal gas mains and water mains, household plumbing, hunting [SEC]. Hazmat transport and pipelines: courts divide [UNSURE].
- **Scope of liability (NCBE NextGen text: "the common test that the harm must arise from the risk that made the activity abnormally dangerous"):** only harm resulting from the abnormally dangerous character of the activity (blast damage and flying debris, not an animal's hypersensitivity; *Foster v. Preston Mill*, Wash. 1954, mink killing kits after nearby blasting [SEC, case from one study guide: UNSURE on holding]). Abnormally sensitive activity of the plaintiff: Rest. 2d §524A [SEC].
- **Who may sue:** foreseeable persons and property harmed; **trespassers** generally cannot (Rest. 2d §520B, from my memory and one summary [UNSURE]). NextGen text lists "who may sue" as a tested element [NCBE]. Hirer of an independent contractor for abnormally dangerous work remains liable [SEC].
- **Defenses:** comparative negligence is a defense in modern law (NextGen: "the use of comparative negligence as a defense") [NCBE]; knowing and voluntary assumption of risk; unforeseeable extraordinary intervening events [SEC]. **Due care is no defense.**

### 5.3 Products liability (outline III; NextGen IV: "different theories ... manufacturing defect, design defect, and failure to warn, who may sue, who may be sued, and defenses")
Theories: (1) strict tort liability (Greenman v. Yuba Power 1963; Rest. 2d §402A; Rest. 3d Products Liability §§1-2); (2) negligence (MacPherson v. Buick; no privity needed); (3) warranty under UCC Article 2; (4) misrepresentation (Rest. 2d §402B).

**Rest. 2d §402A strict liability elements** [SEC; LII; CALI summary]: (1) defendant is a **seller engaged in the business of selling** such products (manufacturers, assemblers, wholesalers, distributors, retailers, commercial lessors); (2) product in a **defective condition [unreasonably dangerous to the user, in the text; most courts now drop "unreasonably"]**; (3) reaches the user **without substantial change** and the defect existed when it left the defendant's control; (4) actual and proximate cause; (5) physical harm to person or property **other than the product itself**. Applies even if the seller used all possible care and the plaintiff bought from someone else (§402A(2)) [CALI/Said Torts quoting §402A(1)-(2)].
- **Not strictly liable:** **casual sellers** (garage sale), **pure service providers** (physicians, dentists; blood banks by statute), many courts limit used-goods sellers; hybrid sale-service uses a predominant-purpose test [SEC].
- **Who may sue:** users, consumers, **bystanders** (majority and Rest. 3d §1: "persons"), not just purchasers; privity abolished [SEC; CALI says §402A text says "ultimate user or consumer" while Rest. 3d §1 covers anyone harmed].
- **Rest. 3d §2 three defect categories** [Florida Bar Journal, UO article, CALI via search summaries]:
  1. **Manufacturing defect:** departs from intended design even though all possible care was exercised: strict liability.
  2. **Design defect:** foreseeable risks of harm could have been reduced or avoided by adoption of a **reasonable alternative design**, and omission makes the product not reasonably safe (risk-utility test; plaintiff generally must show a feasible RAD). Many states still allow or use the **consumer-expectations test** (alone or with risk-utility). The Third Restatement's RAD requirement is a widely cited but not universal rule [SEC; LII Wex notes both tests]. Inherent dangers with no feasible safer design (knives, whiskey, tobacco) aren't defective by that fact alone.
  3. **Inadequate instructions or warnings:** foreseeable risks could have been reduced by reasonable instructions/warnings and omission makes the product not reasonably safe. Warning must be comprehensible and convey nature and severity of risk; **no duty to warn of open and obvious dangers** [SEC].
  - Design and warning claims turn on reasonableness (risk-utility), so they resemble negligence; only manufacturing defects are truly strict [Florida Bar Journal summary].
- **Malfunction doctrine / Rest. 3d §3:** circumstantial proof of a defect when the harm ordinarily occurs only from a defect and other causes are excluded (Rest. 3d §3) [SEC].
- **Learned intermediary doctrine:** warnings for prescription drugs and certain devices go to the prescriber (Rest. 3d §6); exceptions like mass immunization campaigns [SEC]. Federal preemption: premarket-approved device claims preempted (*Riegel*); brand-name failure-to-warn generally not (*Wyeth v. Levine*); generic design/warning claims largely preempted (*Mensing*, *Bartlett*) [SEC; not opened; low priority for the MBE].
- **Post-sale duty to warn** may arise; no common-law duty to recall absent statute/undertaking [SEC].
- **Component parts:** liable if the component is itself defective, or if the supplier substantially participates in integrating it into a defective design (Rest. 3d §5) [SEC; UNSURE on section].
- **Defenses and limits:** (a) **comparative fault** (majority) reduces recovery, including unreasonable, knowing assumption of risk; ordinary failure to discover a defect is not a defense [SEC]; (b) **foreseeable misuse** is not a defense (maker must design/warn against it), **unforeseeable** misuse or **substantial post-sale alteration** that causes the harm relieves the maker [SEC]; (c) **state of the art** relevant to design/warning, not to manufacturing defects; (d) **regulatory compliance** is evidence, not a bar; (e) **disclaimers** don't defeat personal-injury strict liability; (f) statutes of repose [SEC].
- **Economic loss rule:** damage to the product itself or lost profits belongs to warranty/contract (*East River* [SCOTUS, read]).
- **Successor liability:** generally none for predecessor's products unless assumption, de facto merger/continuation, fraud; product-line exception in a few states [SEC].
- **Distribution chain:** an innocent retailer of a sealed defective product is strictly liable to the plaintiff and may **indemnify** against the manufacturer; negligence liability for retailers requires a reason to know of the defect [SEC].

**UCC Article 2 warranties** (read at law.cornell.edu/ucc/2) [UCC text read for §2-316 and §2-318]:
- **§2-313 express warranty:** affirmation of fact/promise/description/sample that is part of the basis of the bargain.
- **§2-314 implied warranty of merchantability:** merchant seller; goods fit for ordinary purposes. **§2-315 fitness for particular purpose:** seller has reason to know the buyer's particular purpose and that buyer relies on seller's skill/judgment to select goods, and buyer relies.
- **§2-316(2) disclaimers:** to exclude merchantability the language "must mention merchantability and in case of a writing must be conspicuous"; to exclude fitness the disclaimer must be in a conspicuous writing [UCC text, read]. Notice of breach within a reasonable time (§2-607(3)(a)) [SEC].
- **§2-318 third-party beneficiaries** (states choose one): **Alt A** warranty extends to "any natural person who is in the family or household of his buyer or who is a guest in his home" injured in person; **Alt B** "any natural person who may reasonably be expected to use, consume or be affected by the goods" and is injured in person; **Alt C** any person (not just natural) injured (including non-personal injury); seller may not exclude or limit the operation of the section (Alt C: with respect to personal injury) [UCC text, read].
- Consequential damages for injury to the person in consumer goods: limitation is prima facie unconscionable (§2-719(3)) [SEC].
- **Rest. 2d §402B:** a seller of a chattel who makes, by public advertising or labels, a **material misrepresentation of fact** about its character or quality is liable for **physical harm** to a consumer who justifiably relies, even if the misrepresentation was innocent [my memory; one secondary source didn't cover it: UNSURE].

### 5.4 Nuisance (outline IV.A; NextGen V)
- **Private nuisance** (Rest. 2d §821D): a **nontrespassory** invasion of another's interest in the **private use and enjoyment of land** (plaintiff must have a possessory interest: owner or tenant). Actionable when (a) the invasion is **substantial** (significant harm to a person of normal sensitivity) and (b) the defendant's conduct is **intentional and unreasonable**, or unintentional but negligent/reckless/abnormally dangerous (§822) [SEC]. **Unreasonable:** gravity of harm outweighs the utility of the conduct (§826(a)), or the harm is serious and the defendant could pay for it and continue (§826(b)). Factors: extent, duration, character of harm; social value of the activity; suitability to the locality (the "**locality rule**" is named in NextGen text [NCBE]); burden of avoidance.
- **Hypersensitive plaintiffs** lose unless an ordinary person or ordinary use would also be affected (§821F) [SEC].
- **Trespass vs nuisance:** trespass = tangible physical invasion of possession; nuisance = interference with use and enjoyment (odors, noise, light, vibration, smoke); same conduct can be both [SEC; NextGen text: "distinctions among private nuisance, trespass, and public nuisance"]. Blocking light, air or view: no nuisance at common law; modern exceptions (spite fences; *Prah v. Maretti* solar panels) [SEC].
- **Remedies:** damages, injunction, abatement. **Boomer v. Atlantic Cement** (N.Y. 1970): where the economic disparity is great, court may deny the injunction on condition that the defendant pay **permanent damages**. **Spur Industries v. Del E. Webb** (Ariz. 1972): developer that came to the feedlot got an injunction against the feedlot but **had to indemnify the feedlot's relocation costs** [SEC: both from one study guide; consistent with my knowledge].
- **Defenses:** "**coming to the nuisance**" is a factor, not an absolute bar; zoning compliance is evidence, not a defense; statutory authorization; right-to-farm statutes; plaintiff's fault (comparative for negligent nuisance; not a defense to intentional) [SEC].
- **Public nuisance** (Rest. 2d §821B): unreasonable interference with a right common to the general public (health, safety, morals, peace, comfort, convenience). Public officials sue; a **private person needs special harm different in kind (not just degree)** from the public's (§821C) [SEC]. Standing example: commercial fisher whose livelihood is destroyed by pollution vs recreational anglers [SEC]. Mass-tort uses (opioids) are contested [SEC; UNSURE, do not teach].

### 5.5 Item bank: strict liability, products, nuisance

**STR-1 (wild animal).** Colin keeps a pet cheetah, exercising every reasonable precaution. At a fundraiser for which he has brought it on a short leash, the cheetah lunges and claws a guest. The guest sues. Colin is:
(A) strictly liable, because he kept a wild animal and the harm flowed from its dangerous propensities. (B) not liable, because he used utmost care. (C) liable only if he was negligent. (D) not liable, because the guest was a licensee.
**Best: A.** Due care is irrelevant for wild animals. D: strict liability protects invitees and licensees; only undiscovered trespassers are excluded.

**STR-2 (domestic dog with notice).** Ned knows his dog has twice snapped at and chased mail carriers. The dog bites a mail carrier on the path to Ned's door. No statute applies. The carrier's best theory is:
(A) strict liability, because Ned knew of the dog's dangerous propensity. (B) none, because the dog had never bitten anyone. (C) negligence per se. (D) none, because mail carriers assume the risk of dogs.
**Best: A.** A prior bite isn't required; notice of the abnormally dangerous propensity is.

**STR-3 (domestic dog without notice).** Ora's good-natured dog, which has never shown aggression, bites a child who steps on its tail. No statute applies. The child's parents sue Ora for strict liability. They will:
(A) lose on strict liability, because Ora had no reason to know of any dangerous propensity. (B) win, because dogs are inherently dangerous. (C) win, because the child was a licensee. (D) lose, because children cannot recover from animal keepers.
**Best: A.** (Negligence might still be argued; dog-bite statutes would change this.)

**STR-4 (trespassing livestock).** Farmer Gil's cattle break through a pasture fence and trample a neighbor's soybean crop. At common law, the neighbor can recover from Gil:
(A) the value of the crop, on strict liability for trespassing livestock, without proof of negligence. (B) nothing, unless Gil knew the fence was weak. (C) nothing, because cattle are domestic animals. (D) only nominal damages.
**Best: A.** Livestock trespass is strict for property damage.

**STR-5 (blasting; due care irrelevant).** A quarry, using state-of-the-art techniques and every precaution, blasts rock. A fragment flies half a mile and cracks the windshield of a car parked at a school. The car's owner sues. The quarry is:
(A) liable, because blasting is an abnormally dangerous activity and the harm came from that danger. (B) not liable, because it exercised due care. (C) liable only if the blast violated a statute. (D) not liable, because blasting is a lawful, licensed activity.
**Best: A.**

**STR-6 (scope of the risk).** A quarry's blasting (a recognized abnormally dangerous activity) produces a loud noise that scares a neighbor's rare, highly excitable show birds into stopping laying for a season. The neighbor sues for strict liability. The claim will:
(A) probably fail, because the harm did not result from the risk that makes blasting abnormally dangerous (explosions, flying debris, concussion), but from the plaintiff's unusually sensitive use. (B) succeed, because blasting is strict liability. (C) succeed, because the noise was foreseeable. (D) fail, because noise is always only a nuisance.
**Best: A.** Strict liability covers only harms from the abnormal danger (Rest. 2d §519(2); §524A). [Treat as medium-confidence: tag UNSURE.]

**STR-7 (ordinary activity: negligence only).** A bakery in a commercial district runs gas ovens connected to the city main. A negligent hookup by the bakery's contractor leaks gas, and an explosion injures a customer next door. The neighbor's claim against the bakery:
(A) must be based on negligence, because operating gas ovens is a common activity that reasonable care makes safe. (B) is strict liability, because gas explosions are dangerous. (C) is strict liability, because the harm affected a third party. (D) fails, because the contractor, not the bakery, was negligent.
**Best: A.** (D ignores nondelegable duties and the bakery's own conduct, but the stem asks about the nature of liability.)

**PRO-1 (innocent retailer).** A grocery sells a sealed jar of pasta sauce produced by a manufacturer; a production error left glass shards in the jar. The grocery could not have discovered them. A customer is injured. In a strict products liability action, the grocery is:
(A) liable, as a commercial seller of a product with a manufacturing defect, and may seek indemnity from the manufacturer. (B) not liable, because it exercised reasonable care in selecting its supplier. (C) not liable, because only manufacturers can be strictly liable. (D) liable only if it knew of the defect.
**Best: A.** B and D describe negligence.

**PRO-2 (bystander).** A defect in a new car's braking system, caused by a production error, makes the brakes fail. The car hits a pedestrian crossing the road. The pedestrian sues the manufacturer in strict liability. The pedestrian:
(A) can recover, because bystanders foreseeably endangered by the product may sue. (B) cannot recover, because she was not a purchaser. (C) cannot recover, because she was not a user. (D) can recover only if the manufacturer was negligent.
**Best: A.**

**PRO-3 (casual seller).** Walt, a retired teacher, sells his used lawnmower, whose blade guard he knows is cracked, at a garage sale. A buyer is injured. A claim against Walt in strict products liability:
(A) fails, because Walt is not engaged in the business of selling mowers; the buyer must rely on negligence or misrepresentation theories. (B) succeeds, because the product was defective. (C) succeeds, because Walt knew of the crack. (D) fails, because used goods are never subject to strict liability.
**Best: A.** (Knowledge helps a misrepresentation/negligence claim, not strict liability.)

**PRO-4 (design defect: reasonable alternative design).** Zed is injured by a table saw that had no blade-brake device. At trial he shows that an inexpensive, proven blade-brake was feasible, would have prevented his injury, and would not impair the saw's usefulness. The manufacturer says the saw performed exactly as designed. Under Restatement (Third) §2(b), Zed:
(A) may prevail, because the omission of a reasonable alternative design renders the saw not reasonably safe. (B) loses, because the saw matched its design. (C) loses, because table saws are inherently dangerous. (D) prevails only if the saw deviated from the manufacturer's specifications.
**Best: A.** D describes a manufacturing defect.

**PRO-5 (open and obvious danger).** Kitchen knife maker sells a chef's knife without any warning that the blade is sharp. A user cuts herself while chopping. A failure-to-warn claim:
(A) fails, because the danger is open and obvious and there is no duty to warn. (B) succeeds, because no warning was given. (C) succeeds, because knives could be made with a safer design. (D) succeeds under strict liability regardless of obviousness.
**Best: A.**

**PRO-6 (substantial alteration).** After buying a shredder, an owner removes its safety guard so it can process larger items. A worker is injured. The worker sues the maker. The claim will likely fail because:
(A) the product had been substantially altered after it left the maker's control in an unforeseeable way. (B) the worker assumed the risk. (C) shredders are inherently dangerous. (D) the maker complied with industry standards.
**Best: A.** (If removal of guards was foreseeable and the maker could have prevented it by design, the answer might change.)

**PRO-7 (learned intermediary).** A drug maker adequately warns prescribing physicians of a serious side effect but gives patients no warning. A physician, aware of the warning, prescribes the drug anyway after weighing risks; the patient suffers the side effect. The patient's failure-to-warn claim against the maker will:
(A) probably fail, because the maker's duty was discharged by adequately warning the prescribing physician. (B) succeed, because patients must always be warned directly. (C) succeed, because the drug was dangerous. (D) fail, because drugs can't be defective.
**Best: A.**

**PRO-8 (UCC §2-318 Alt A).** Ben buys a dining table from a furniture store. At dinner, a defect in a leg causes the table to collapse and injure his houseguest, Gwen. In a state that adopted Alternative A of UCC §2-318, Gwen:
(A) may sue the seller for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability. (B) may not, because she lacks privity. (C) may sue only if she had been the buyer's family member. (D) may sue only in tort.
**Best: A.** Alt A extends warranties to guests in the buyer's home (a passer-by hurt by the table would need Alt B or C).

**PRO-9 (economic loss).** A new washing machine overheats due to a defective part and melts itself, destroying only the machine. The buyer sues the maker in strict tort for the cost of the machine. The claim will:
(A) fail in tort, because damage only to the product itself is economic loss to be addressed in warranty or contract. (B) succeed, because the product was defective. (C) succeed, because strict liability covers all product-related loss. (D) fail, because machines can't be defective.
**Best: A.**

**NUI-1 (private nuisance: zoning and utility).** A cement plant's dust and noise substantially interfere with the use and enjoyment of its neighbors' homes. The plant is lawfully zoned and employs hundreds of local residents. In the neighbors' private nuisance action, the plant's arguments that its operation is lawful and socially valuable are:
(A) factors in weighing the gravity of the harm against the utility of the conduct, but not complete defenses. (B) a complete defense, because lawful conduct is never a nuisance. (C) irrelevant, because only the neighbors' harm matters. (D) a complete defense, because the plant was zoned for industrial use.
**Best: A.**

**NUI-2 (coming to the nuisance).** A developer buys land near an established cattle feedlot and builds a retirement community. The residents sue the feedlot for nuisance. The feedlot's best argument is that residents came to the nuisance. This argument will:
(A) be a factor, but not necessarily a bar; a court may enjoin the feedlot but require the developer to compensate it. (B) be a complete defense. (C) be irrelevant. (D) require dismissal because the feedlot is a protected agricultural use.
**Best: A.** (Spur Industries; right-to-farm statutes could change D.)

**NUI-3 (public nuisance: special harm).** A paper mill dumps toxins in a river, killing the fish. A commercial fisherman whose livelihood is destroyed and a weekend recreational angler both sue for public nuisance. Likely result:
(A) the fisherman may sue because his harm differs in kind from the public's; the angler's harm is only different in degree. (B) both may sue. (C) neither may sue; only the state can. (D) only the angler may sue.
**Best: A.**

**NUI-4 (nuisance vs trespass).** A neighbor's workshop emits constant loud grinding noise and dust that drifts into Ivy's yard. A different neighbor throws a pile of branches over Ivy's fence. Respectively, Ivy's best claims are:
(A) nuisance for the noise and dust; trespass for the branches. (B) trespass for both. (C) nuisance for both. (D) trespass for the noise and nuisance for the branches.
**Best: A.** Tangible invasion is trespass; intangible interference is nuisance.

## 6. OTHER TORTS (MBE category IV): defamation, privacy, misrepresentation, interference with business relations

### 6.1 Defamation: common law
Elements (Rest. 2d §558) [SEC/REST]: (1) a **false and defamatory** statement of fact concerning the plaintiff (tends to harm reputation or deter others from associating); (2) **of and concerning** the plaintiff (a reasonable recipient would understand it refers to him; small, identifiable groups can sue, large groups can't; the dead can't be defamed); (3) **publication** to at least one third party who understands it (statement to the plaintiff alone isn't publication); (4) **fault** (constitutional minimum, below); (5) harm/damages. A **republisher** is liable as if she originated it, even if she names the source [SEC].
- **Libel** (written/permanent form; broadcasts often treated as libel): damages presumed at common law. **Slander** (spoken/transitory): plaintiff must prove **special damages** (specific pecuniary loss) unless **slander per se**: statement (1) imputes a crime (traditionally serious or morally turpitudinous), (2) imputes a loathsome (venereal) disease, (3) injures plaintiff in business, trade, profession or office, (4) imputes serious sexual misconduct (unchastity) [SEC; Rest. 2d §§570-574 from memory]. Libel per quod (defamatory only with extrinsic facts) needs special damages in some states [SEC].
- **Truth** is a defense (burden now on plaintiff in public-concern/media cases, below). **Opinion:** a statement that implies undisclosed defamatory facts is actionable; pure opinion and rhetorical hyperbole are not [SCOTUS Milkovich].
- **Absolute privileges** (survive malice): statements in judicial proceedings by judge, lawyer, witness, party, juror (relevant to the proceeding); legislative proceedings; high executive officials in official duties; communications between spouses; consent [SEC]. **Qualified (conditional) privileges** (lost if abused: knowledge of falsity/reckless disregard, publication beyond need, improper purpose): protection of one's own interest, interest of the recipient or a third person (employer references, reports to police), common interest, **fair and accurate report of official proceedings** [SEC].
- Online: **47 U.S.C. §230(c)(1)**: "No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider" [statute text read at law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230]. So the platform is shielded; the original poster is not. Probably beyond what the MBE tests; low priority [UNSURE].

### 6.2 Defamation: constitutional limits (outline IV.B: "constitutional limitations") [SCOTUS opinions read]
| Plaintiff / speech | What the plaintiff must show | Damages | Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public official**, statement about official conduct | **Actual malice**: knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard of whether it was false | presumed damages not automatic; actual malice needed for punitive | *New York Times v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964): "prohibits a public official from recovering damages for a defamatory falsehood relating to his official conduct unless he proves ... 'actual malice' ... with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not" |
| **Public figure** (all-purpose: "pervasive fame or notoriety"; limited-purpose: "voluntarily injects himself or is drawn into a particular public controversy" and is a figure only for that range of issues) | Same actual malice standard, by **clear and convincing proof** | Presumed and punitive damages available once actual malice shown | *Gertz v. Robert Welch*, 418 U.S. 323 (1974) (pp. 342-345 as quoted); extension to public figures from *Curtis Publishing v. Butts* [cited in Gertz] |
| **Private figure, matter of public concern** | At least **negligence** ("so long as they do not impose liability without fault, the States may define for themselves the appropriate standard of liability"); plaintiff must prove falsity (*Hepps*) | **Actual injury only** (impaired reputation, humiliation, mental anguish) unless actual malice; **no presumed or punitive damages** without actual malice | *Gertz*; *Philadelphia Newspapers v. Hepps*, 475 U.S. 767 (1986) ("the common-law presumption that defamatory speech is false cannot stand when a plaintiff seeks damages against a media defendant for speech of public concern") |
| **Private figure, matter of private concern** | State law governs fault | Presumed and punitive damages permitted without actual malice | *Dun & Bradstreet v. Greenmoss Builders*, 472 U.S. 749 (1985) (plurality; "permitting recovery of presumed and punitive damages ... absent a showing of 'actual malice' does not violate the First Amendment when the defamatory statements do not involve matters of public concern") |
- **Reckless disregard** is subjective: the defendant must have had serious doubts or high awareness of probable falsity; "failure to investigate does not in itself establish bad faith" (*St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727 (1968), read) [SCOTUS].
- **Who is a public figure?** Gertz's plaintiff, a reputable lawyer representing a victim's family in a civil case, was not a public figure by representing a client; most people are private figures; "truly involuntary public figures must be exceedingly rare" [SCOTUS Gertz, read].
- **Opinion:** *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal*, 497 U.S. 1 (1990): no separate First Amendment "opinion" privilege; protected are statements on matters of public concern that are not "provable as false" and statements that "cannot reasonably be interpreted as stating actual facts" (hyperbole, parody). "In my opinion Jones is a liar" is actionable; saying Jones "shows his abysmal ignorance by accepting the teachings of Marx and Lenin" is not [SCOTUS, read].
- **IIED by publication:** public figures can't recover for a parody without a false statement of fact made with actual malice (*Hustler v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988)) [SCOTUS, read].

### 6.3 Invasion of privacy: four torts (Rest. 2d §§652A-652E)
Text verified for §§652B-D from the Harvard privacy site; §652E from memory and Time v. Hill [SCOTUS read]:
1. **Intrusion upon seclusion (§652B):** "One who intentionally intrudes, physically or otherwise, upon the solitude or seclusion of another or his private affairs or concerns ... if the intrusion would be highly offensive to a reasonable person." **No publication required**; the wrong is complete on intrusion. No reasonable expectation of privacy in public places [SEC].
2. **Appropriation of name or likeness (§652C):** "One who appropriates to his own use or benefit the name or likeness of another." Commercial/other benefit of the defendant; no "highly offensive" element. Newsworthy, informational, parody uses are protected; but the Court held the First Amendment doesn't immunize broadcasting a performer's entire act without consent (*Zacchini v. Scripps-Howard*, 433 U.S. 562 (1977)) [SCOTUS: syllabus/opinion grepped only: UNSURE on holding wording].
3. **Public disclosure of private facts (§652D):** "One who gives publicity to a matter concerning the private life of another ... if the matter publicized is of a kind that (a) would be highly offensive to a reasonable person, and (b) is not of legitimate concern to the public." Needs **publicity** (to the public at large, not a few people). **Truth is not a defense.** Constitutional limits: the State may not impose sanctions on accurate publication of a rape victim's name obtained from judicial records open to public inspection (*Cox Broadcasting v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469 (1975)) [SCOTUS, read]; truthful information lawfully obtained about a matter of public significance may not be punished "absent a need to further a state interest of the highest order" (*Florida Star v. B.J.F.*, 491 U.S. 524 (1989)) [SCOTUS, read].
4. **False light publicity (§652E):** publicity that places the plaintiff in a false light that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person, where the defendant knew of or recklessly disregarded the falsity. On matters of public interest the First Amendment requires knowing or reckless falsity (*Time, Inc. v. Hill*, 385 U.S. 374 (1967): "constitutional protections for speech and press preclude the application of the New York statute to redress false reports of matters of public interest in the absence of proof that the defendant published the report with knowledge of its falsity or in reckless disregard of the truth") [SCOTUS, read]. A few states don't recognize false light [SEC].
- Privacy rights are personal: they don't survive death, can't be asserted by relatives, and corporations have no privacy right (except against appropriation) [SEC]. Defenses: consent, newsworthiness, absolute/qualified privileges where a publication is involved [SEC].

### 6.4 Misrepresentation (outline IV.C; NextGen VI: fraudulent and negligent)
- **Intentional misrepresentation (fraud/deceit)**, Rest. 2d §525: (1) false representation of material past/present fact (also intent not to perform), (2) **scienter** (knowledge of falsity or reckless indifference), (3) intent to induce reliance, (4) **justifiable reliance**, (5) damages (pecuniary loss). **Opinion/puffery** generally not actionable; opinion by a person with superior knowledge or implying facts can be. **Silence** is not misrepresentation unless there is a duty to disclose: fiduciary/confidential relationship, partial disclosure (half-truth), active concealment, known latent defect basic to the transaction in some contexts (home sales), correcting a prior statement that has become false [SEC; Rest. 2d §551 from memory]. Contributory negligence of the plaintiff in failing to investigate is generally not a defense to intentional fraud [SEC; UNSURE]. Damages: out-of-pocket and, in fraud, benefit-of-the-bargain in many states; punitive damages possible [SEC; UNSURE on majority].
- **Negligent misrepresentation**, Rest. 2d §552: one who in the course of business, profession or employment (or any transaction in which he has a pecuniary interest) supplies **false information for the guidance of others in their business transactions** is liable for **pecuniary loss** caused by justifiable reliance if he fails to exercise reasonable care in obtaining or communicating the information; liability limited to the **person or limited group** for whose benefit and guidance he intends to supply the information or knows the recipient intends to supply it, in a transaction he intends to influence (or knows the recipient so intends). States split into the Restatement approach (common), the strict-privity/near-privity approach (*Ultramares*, New York), and a broad foreseeability approach (minority) [SEC; UNSURE on count].
- **Innocent misrepresentation**: Rest. 2d §552C: seller/lessor/exchanger who makes a material misrepresentation to induce a deal, even innocently, liable for out-of-pocket pecuniary loss; adopted in some states [SEC]. Not listed in NextGen.
- **Consumer physical harm:** Rest. 2d §402B (section 5.3).
- **Economic loss rule / contract overlap:** many courts bar tort claims for losses that merely repeat contract duties; fraud in the inducement is usually still actionable [SEC; UNSURE].

### 6.5 Interference with business relations (outline IV.D; NOT in NextGen)
- **Interference with existing contract** (Rest. 2d §766): intentional and improper inducement of a third person not to perform a contract with the plaintiff. Elements: valid contract; defendant's knowledge of it; intentional interference causing breach (nonperformance); improper (not privileged); damages [SEC]. Impropriety is judged by factors in §767 (nature of conduct, motive, interests of each, social interests, proximity of causation, relationship of the parties).
- **Interference with prospective advantage** (Rest. 2d §766B): reasonable expectancy of a relationship; knowledge; intentional interference by improper means or purpose; damages. Many states require **independently wrongful** means (fraud, defamation, violence, statutory violation) [SEC].
- **Competitor's privilege** (Rest. 2d §768): competition is not improper if the competitor does not use wrongful means (fraud, defamation, threats), does not restrain trade unlawfully, and acts at least in part to advance his own competing interest; the privilege applies to prospective relations and contracts terminable at will, not to inducing breach of a definite-term contract [SEC; Rest. 2d §768 from memory: UNSURE].
- **Injurious falsehood (trade libel, Rest. 2d §623A):** false statement disparaging plaintiff's goods/title causing pecuniary loss; needs special damages, knowledge or reckless falsity [SEC].

### 6.6 Item bank: other torts

**DFM-1 (slander per se).** At a neighborhood party, Bix says to several guests, "Dr. Kim is a fraud; he's never passed a medical board exam." Dr. Kim's patient volume does not change, and he can prove no specific financial loss. Kim sues Bix for slander. Kim will:
(A) survive a motion to dismiss for lack of special damages, because the statement is slander per se (injures him in his profession). (B) lose, because slander always requires proof of special damages. (C) lose, because the statement was an opinion. (D) win only if Bix acted with actual malice.
**Best: A.** C: "fraud who never passed a board exam" asserts provable fact. D confuses constitutional fault rules (actual malice applies to public figures/public concern damages) with slander per se.

**DFM-2 (publication).** Lena mails a sealed, handwritten letter to Mo that calls him a thief and a liar. Mo alone opens and reads it. Mo sues for defamation. He will:
(A) lose, because there was no publication to a third party. (B) win, because the letter was libelous per se. (C) win, because the statements were false. (D) win only if he proves special damages.
**Best: A.** Publication requires communication to someone other than the plaintiff.

**DFM-3 (private figure, public concern).** A newspaper negligently reports that Rhea, a local plumber, was arrested for fraud, when in fact someone else with a similar name was. Rhea proves harm to her reputation and mental anguish but not that the paper doubted the story. In her defamation action, she may recover:
(A) compensation for proven actual injury, but not presumed or punitive damages. (B) presumed and punitive damages, because the report was false. (C) nothing, because she did not prove actual malice. (D) nominal damages only.
**Best: A.** *Gertz*: private figure on a matter of public concern; negligence suffices for liability, actual malice is required for presumed/punitive damages.

**DFM-4 (public official, actual malice).** A newspaper publishes a false story that the mayor took a bribe, relying on a single anonymous source it did not try to check. The mayor sues. There's no evidence the paper doubted the story. The mayor will probably:
(A) lose, because failure to investigate is not itself actual malice. (B) win, because the paper was negligent. (C) win, because the story was false and defamatory per se. (D) lose, because truth is presumed.
**Best: A.** *Sullivan*/*St. Amant*. D: the common-law presumption of falsity is reversed for public-concern speech (the plaintiff must prove falsity).

**DFM-5 (limited-purpose public figure).** Pru leads a loud, widely covered campaign against a proposed landfill. A local station falsely says she was fired from her job for stealing, a story unrelated to the landfill controversy; the station was negligent. Pru is treated as:
(A) a private figure as to this unrelated statement, so negligence suffices. (B) a public figure for all purposes. (C) a public official. (D) a limited-purpose public figure for this statement, so actual malice is required.
**Best: A.** Limited-purpose figure status extends only to issues related to the controversy she joined (*Gertz*). [UNSURE: some courts require germaneness of the statement to the controversy; D can be right if the stem ties the statement to the controversy.]

**DFM-6 (fact vs opinion).** A restaurant critic writes, "The chef's pasta is the worst I have ever tasted, a crime against Italy." The chef sues for defamation. The claim will:
(A) fail, because the statement is hyperbole and opinion not provable as false. (B) succeed, because it harms the chef's business. (C) succeed if the critic acted with actual malice. (D) succeed because there is no opinion privilege.
**Best: A.** *Milkovich* has no separate opinion privilege, but non-provable opinion and hyperbole aren't actionable statements of fact.

**DFM-7 (qualified privilege).** Omar's former supervisor, in good faith, tells a prospective employer, "Omar was fired for embezzling from the company." The supervisor had reasonable grounds, but the claim turns out to be wrong. In Omar's defamation suit, the supervisor will:
(A) prevail, because a qualified privilege protects good-faith statements to a person with a legitimate interest, absent knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard. (B) lose, because the statement was false. (C) prevail only if the statement was true. (D) lose, because employer references are never privileged.
**Best: A.**

**DFM-8 (republication).** A radio host says, "Now, I'm only repeating what Channel 5 reported, but Councilman Rao accepts bribes." The report was false. In Rao's defamation action, the host's attribution to Channel 5:
(A) is not a defense; a republisher is liable as if she originated the statement, subject to the applicable fault standard. (B) is a complete defense. (C) shifts all liability to Channel 5. (D) is a privilege only if the host believed it.
**Best: A.** (Rao, a public official, must still show actual malice.)

**PRI-1 (intrusion).** A private detective secretly plants a microphone in a woman's bedroom and listens to her private calls for a week. He publishes nothing. Her best claim is:
(A) intrusion upon seclusion, which requires no publication. (B) public disclosure of private facts. (C) false light. (D) appropriation.
**Best: A.**

**PRI-2 (public disclosure: truth no defense).** A hospital clerk posts on a public social media account the true fact that a coworker is receiving treatment for a serious mental illness. The coworker sues for public disclosure of private facts. The clerk's best defense, that the statement is true, will:
(A) fail, because truth is not a defense to this tort. (B) succeed, because truth defeats every privacy claim. (C) succeed, because the post was only seen by followers. (D) fail only if the clerk acted with malice.
**Best: A.**

**PRI-3 (public records).** A newspaper accurately reports a defendant's name and the charge, copied from a public court filing, in a story about a criminal prosecution. The defendant's family sues for public disclosure of private facts. The claim will:
(A) fail, because the First Amendment bars liability for accurately publishing information from public judicial records. (B) succeed, because the facts were embarrassing. (C) succeed, because privacy outweighs press interests. (D) fail because the family consented.
**Best: A.** *Cox Broadcasting*.

**PRI-4 (false light vs appropriation).** An advertising agency, without permission, uses a stock photo of Tess, a private person, in a billboard for a dating service, with a caption implying she is a subscriber. Tess has never used it. Her strongest claim is:
(A) appropriation of her likeness (and arguably false light), because her likeness was used for the defendant's commercial benefit without consent. (B) intrusion. (C) public disclosure of private facts. (D) none, because she is not a public figure.
**Best: A.**

**MIS-1 (puffery).** A car dealer tells a buyer, "This is the best used sedan on the lot; you'll love it." The car turns out to have an ordinary transmission problem. The buyer sues for fraud. The statement is:
(A) not actionable, because it is a vague opinion or puffery, not a statement of fact. (B) actionable, because it induced the sale. (C) actionable, because dealers have superior knowledge. (D) actionable if the dealer knew about the transmission.
**Best: A.** (If the dealer had said "the transmission was just rebuilt," D-type facts matter.)

**MIS-2 (negligent misrepresentation: limited group).** An accountant negligently prepares an audit overstating a company's profits. He knows the company will give the audit to First Bank for a specific loan, and First Bank relies on it. The company later defaults. Under the Restatement (Second) approach, First Bank:
(A) can recover its pecuniary loss from the accountant. (B) cannot recover, because there was no privity. (C) cannot recover unless the accountant intended to deceive. (D) can recover only its out-of-pocket loss from the company.
**Best: A.** §552 liability extends to the limited group the supplier knows will rely. (In an Ultramares/near-privity state the answer depends on the relationship between the accountant and the bank; the stem must say.)

**ECO-1 (interference with contract).** Rival Co. knows that Supplier is under a two-year written exclusive contract to sell Acme all its widgets. Rival Co. offers Supplier a much higher price and Supplier breaches its contract with Acme. Acme sues Rival. Rival's best defense is competition. This defense will:
(A) probably fail, because inducing breach of an existing definite-term contract is improper. (B) succeed, because every competitor may compete. (C) succeed, because Supplier freely chose to breach. (D) succeed, because Acme can sue Supplier.
**Best: A.** (D: that Acme has a contract remedy doesn't negate the tort.)

**ECO-2 (prospective advantage).** A pizzeria cuts prices and runs ads to take customers from a rival. Separately, it tells the rival's customers that the rival "uses expired cheese," which is false. As to the rival's tortious interference with prospective advantage claim, the pizzeria's conduct is:
(A) privileged as to the price cuts and ads, but not the lies. (B) privileged as to both. (C) tortious as to both. (D) tortious as to the price cuts and privileged as to the lies.
**Best: A.** Competition by fair means is privileged; fraud/defamation are improper means.

## 7. Cross-cutting: traps, distractor patterns, and how to use this in the app

### 7.1 The twelve traps that cost MBE points in Torts (collected; the first six are tied to the NCBE outline defaults)
1. **Contributory negligence is not the default.** Pure comparative fault is. Use contributory/last clear chance only if the stem says so (AL, DC, MD, NC, VA).
2. **Joint and several liability is the default**, so the solvent defendant pays everything and seeks contribution.
3. **Assume wrongful death and survival actions exist.** Don't pick "no cause of action because the victim is dead."
4. **Intent is not motive.** A joking or loving motive doesn't defeat intent; mistake doesn't defeat intent (trespass, conversion).
5. **Assault = apprehension of imminent contact**, battery = contact; false imprisonment needs awareness or harm.
6. **Strict liability ignores due care**, but only for harm within the abnormal risk; and due care (not negligence) is also the wrong question for manufacturing defects.
7. **Duty first:** no duty to rescue; exceptions are special relationship, prior conduct, undertaking (that increases risk or induces reliance).
8. **Landowner classification decides the duty**; the traditional scheme is the MBE-safe default; trespasser children have §339; invitees get inspection; licensees (incl. social guests) get warnings about known hidden dangers.
9. **Negligence per se needs class AND type of harm**, still needs causation, and a licensing violation usually isn't enough.
10. **Superseding cause must be unforeseeable.** Foreseeable negligence, medical malpractice, rescue, and crimes that are the very risk do not supersede.
11. **Vicarious liability:** employee = control; scope = serving the employer, not obeying every rule; independent contractor exceptions (nondelegable duty, inherently dangerous work); negligent hiring is direct, not vicarious.
12. **Defamation:** who is the plaintiff, what is the topic: public official/figure = actual malice; private figure on public concern = negligence and actual injury only; truth burden on plaintiff for public concern; no separate opinion privilege.

### 7.2 Distractor patterns seen in this bank (real MBE wrong answers tend to look like these; derived from the logic of the rules, not from released questions)
- The right result with the **wrong reason** (liability because "but for" when the real issue is scope of risk).
- A rule from the **wrong body of law** (strict-liability reasoning in a negligence problem; negligence per se where the statute doesn't fit).
- **"Always/never"** (e.g., "intentional crimes always supersede", "words alone are never assault").
- Treating **fault of someone else** as negating liability when joint and several liability applies.
- **Adding a requirement** the tort doesn't have (physical injury for IIED; touching the body for battery; privity for negligence; proof of actual loss for libel per se).
- **Applying the plaintiff's status** wrongly (invitee/licensee; public vs private figure).
- **Mixing majority and Restatement** positions without the stem saying which.
